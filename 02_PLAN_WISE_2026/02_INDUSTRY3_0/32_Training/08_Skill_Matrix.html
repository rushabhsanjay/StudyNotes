1. Definition of a Skill Matrix

A skill matrix is a structured record that maps employees or teams against the skills, knowledge, experience, and authorization required to perform specific tasks.

In industrial maintenance and automation, it helps answer five practical questions:

  • Who can perform a task independently?
  • Who requires supervision or further training?
  • Who is authorized to work on safety-critical equipment?
  • Which critical skills are available on each shift?
  • Which capability gaps create operational risk?

A skill matrix is not simply a list of training courses completed. It should reflect demonstrated capability and the level of independence permitted for each task.

For example, an employee may have attended PLC training but still require supervision when diagnosing a live production fault. Training attendance is evidence of exposure, not proof of independent troubleshooting competence.

2. Objectives of a Skill Matrix

A properly maintained skill matrix supports:

  • Competency-based work allocation.
  • Shift-wise maintenance coverage.
  • Training and development planning.
  • Reduced dependence on individual experts.
  • Identification of single points of failure in technical knowledge.
  • Succession and workforce planning.
  • Safer allocation of high-risk work.
  • Cross-training between electrical, mechanical, controls, and instrumentation teams.
  • Evidence for internal audits and applicable competency requirements.
  • Assessment of training effectiveness.
  • Planning for new equipment, technology, and process changes.

The purpose is not to rank people for its own sake. It is to ensure that the organization has the right capabilities available for the work it must perform.

3. Skill Matrix vs Related Records

Record Main purpose Example
Skill matrix Maps people against required skills and competency levels Technician competency in PLC diagnostics
Training matrix Tracks required and completed training Electrical safety training status
Authorization register Records permitted work or formal authorization Authorized electrical work personnel
Qualification record Records formal education or credentials Diploma in Electrical Engineering
Training attendance Records participation in a training event Siemens PLC workshop attendance
Competency assessment Records evidence of demonstrated capability Practical PLC fault-diagnosis assessment
Job description Defines role responsibilities Maintenance executive responsibilities
Manpower plan Defines staffing requirements Required technicians per shift

These records are related but not interchangeable. A training record may support a competency assessment, while formal authorization may require additional approvals beyond technical skill.

4. Skill Categories in Industrial Maintenance

A useful skill matrix covers technical skills, diagnostic capability, safety, documentation, and operational knowledge.

HMI Alarm analysis, tag verification, application diagnostics

The matrix should reflect the site's actual equipment and work scope. Listing every possible technology creates a large spreadsheet that looks impressive but may be operationally useless.

5. Defining Competency Levels

Competency levels need clear behavioral definitions. Otherwise, one person's "expert" becomes another person's "I watched a video once."

A five-level scale is useful for many technical teams.

Level Label Demonstrated capability
0 Not assessed No valid assessment evidence is available
1 Awareness Understands basic concepts and recognizes common components
2 Assisted Performs defined tasks with guidance or direct supervision
3 Independent Performs routine tasks independently within approved procedures
4 Advanced Handles complex faults, analyzes root causes, and improves methods
5 Specialist Provides deep technical expertise, develops standards, and mentors others

Important distinctions

Not assessed is not the same as incompetent. It means that the organization lacks sufficient evidence to assign a reliable competency rating.

Independent does not mean unrestricted authorization. A technically competent person may still require a permit, role authorization, specialist approval, or a particular safety qualification.

Specialist does not mean infallible. Even experienced personnel must follow approved procedures and verify unfamiliar conditions.

The organization may use a four-level or other scale, but definitions must remain consistent across departments.

6. Competency vs Authorization

Competency and authorization must be tracked separately when the work requires formal permission.

Example Competency question Authorization question
Electrical panel work Can the person correctly perform the task? Is the person permitted and qualified to perform it under site rules?
PLC programming Can the person understand and modify the program? Is the person authorized to make the change on that asset?
Robot safety validation Does the person understand the validation method? Is the person approved to conduct or sign off the validation?
Calibration Can the person perform the required verification? Is the person authorized to release the instrument or calibration result?
CNC parameter recovery Can the person understand the parameter system? Is the person approved to perform the OEM-controlled recovery procedure?

A skill matrix should never be used to bypass authorization requirements.

7. Building the Skill Matrix

A practical matrix is built from operational requirements rather than from the skills that employees happen to possess today.

Step 1: Define the work scope

Identify:

  • Machines and production areas.
  • Critical equipment and subsystems.
  • Common breakdown types.
  • Planned maintenance activities.
  • Required software and engineering tools.
  • Safety-critical tasks.
  • Shift coverage requirements.
  • Emerging technologies and planned projects.

Step 2: Define required skills

Break broad skills into assessable tasks.

For example, "PLC" is too broad to assess accurately. Separate it into skills such as:

  • Identify PLC hardware and I/O.
  • Read online diagnostics.
  • Trace input and output signals.
  • Interpret an existing program.
  • Diagnose communication faults.
  • Compare online and offline projects.
  • Recover from approved backup.
  • Implement controlled program changes.
  • Validate the change and document it.

These skills require different levels of competence.

Step 3: Set the required level

Determine the competency level required for each role or task.

A routine inspection may require independent execution at Level 3, while advanced program recovery may require Level 4 or specialist support, depending on the site's risk and authorization rules.

Step 4: Assess actual capability

Use observed performance and documented evidence rather than self-rating alone.

Step 5: Identify gaps

Compare actual competency against required competency.

Step 6: Create development actions

Assign training, supervised practice, practical assessments, or specialist mentoring.

Step 7: Reassess and update

Confirm that the employee can demonstrate the required capability and update the matrix through a controlled process.

8. Designing a Useful Matrix Structure

A matrix usually has employees as rows and individual skills as columns, or uses a normalized database-style structure when the number of employees and skills is large.

Simple matrix

Employee ID PLC diagnostics VFD troubleshooting CNC diagnostics Electrical safety
E001 4 3 2 3
E002 3 4 1 3
E003 2 2 3 2

The ratings are illustrative. In a real system, each rating should be supported by an assessment record.

For operational planning, maintain separate information for:

  • Required competency level.
  • Actual competency level.
  • Assessment date.
  • Assessor.
  • Evidence reference.
  • Authorization status where applicable.
  • Training or development action.
  • Target reassessment date.

Do not overload a single cell with competency, authorization, training status, and comments. Keep the information structured so it can be filtered and analyzed reliably.

9. Required vs Actual Competency

The required competency level represents the capability needed for the work. The actual competency level represents the demonstrated capability of the employee.

Define the competency gap as:

\[ G = R - A \]

Where:

  • \(G\) = competency gap.
  • \(R\) = required competency level.
  • \(A\) = actual competency level.

For planning, a positive gap indicates that the required level exceeds the assessed level. A zero or negative gap indicates that the assessed level meets or exceeds the numeric requirement, subject to authorization and other prerequisites.

Example:

  • Required PLC diagnostics level: 4.
  • Assessed level: 2.
  • Gap: \(4 - 2 = 2\).

This employee requires a development plan before being relied upon for work requiring Level 4 capability.

The numeric gap does not measure how difficult the skill is to acquire. It is a planning indicator, not a scientific measurement of expertise.

10. Competency Assessment Methods

A reliable assessment uses evidence appropriate to the skill and its risk.

Written or oral assessment

Useful for verifying concepts, terminology, safety principles, and troubleshooting logic.

Limitation: theoretical knowledge does not establish practical ability.

Practical demonstration

The employee performs a defined task under controlled conditions.

Examples:

  • Trace an input signal through a PLC system.
  • Diagnose a simulated drive fault.
  • Interpret an electrical drawing.
  • Verify a sensor signal.
  • Restore a test application from an approved backup.

Observation during work

The assessor observes the employee performing normal tasks.

The observation should use defined criteria and should not expose the employee or production equipment to unnecessary risk.

Troubleshooting exercise

The employee diagnoses a simulated or safely controlled fault and explains the evidence supporting the conclusion.

Work-history review

Completed work orders, verified repairs, approved changes, and documented problem-solving may provide supporting evidence. Work history alone may not prove that a task was performed independently or correctly.

Assessment evidence

Record:

  • Skill assessed.
  • Assessment method.
  • Task or scenario.
  • Expected performance.
  • Actual performance.
  • Safety compliance.
  • Assessor.
  • Date.
  • Result.
  • Required development action.

11. Skill Matrix for Shift Coverage

A team can have high average competency and still be vulnerable if only one person can diagnose a critical system.

For example, if only one technician can recover a critical PLC, the department may have a coverage gap when that technician is absent or assigned elsewhere.

Shift coverage analysis should consider:

  • Required skills per shift.
  • Number of independently competent personnel.
  • Critical tasks that require formal authorization.
  • Availability of specialist support.
  • Expected response time.
  • Leave, training, and shift rotation.
  • Equipment criticality.
  • Dependencies on vendors or external specialists.

Example

Critical skill Required people per shift Independently competent people Coverage assessment
PLC fault diagnosis 2 3 Meets numerical requirement
VFD troubleshooting 2 2 Meets numerical requirement
CNC recovery 1 1 Potential single point of failure
Industrial network recovery 1 0 Capability gap

These figures are illustrative. A coverage assessment must account for shift assignment and authorization, not just department-wide totals.

12. Single Point of Failure in Technical Knowledge

A technical knowledge single point of failure exists when an essential task depends on one person's knowledge or availability.

Examples include:

  • Only one employee understands a legacy CNC controller.
  • Only one person can recover an industrial PC image.
  • Only one technician knows the correct robot backup and validation process.
  • Only one engineer can diagnose a particular test-bed application.
  • Only one employee understands a legacy PLC communication configuration.

Risk-reduction measures

  • Cross-train additional employees.
  • Create approved troubleshooting guides.
  • Document configuration and recovery procedures.
  • Preserve engineering files and verified backups.
  • Use supervised practical exercises.
  • Maintain vendor and specialist escalation routes.
  • Review coverage during shift and leave planning.

Documentation reduces dependency but does not automatically create practical competence. A person must still be able to use the documented procedure correctly.

13. Training and Development Planning

The skill matrix becomes useful when it generates specific development actions.

A development plan should include:

  • Employee or role.
  • Skill to be developed.
  • Required competency level.
  • Current assessed level.
  • Development method.
  • Trainer or mentor.
  • Practice tasks.
  • Assessment criteria.
  • Target date.
  • Evidence of completion.
  • Reassessment outcome.

Development methods

  • Classroom or structured technical training.
  • OEM training.
  • Guided study.
  • Supervised troubleshooting.
  • Practical work on training rigs.
  • Controlled simulation.
  • Review of drawings and actual equipment architecture.
  • Participation in RCA investigations.
  • Approved project work.
  • Mentoring by an experienced specialist.

Example development plan

Skill Current level Target level Development action Verification
PLC I/O diagnostics 2 3 Supervised fault tracing Practical assessment
VFD fault diagnosis 2 3 Fault-code analysis and controlled exercises Scenario-based assessment
SQL data verification 1 2 Guided query and data-quality exercises Demonstration
RCA 2 3 Participate in a documented investigation Review of analysis and evidence

Completion of a training course should not automatically increase a competency rating. The rating should change when sufficient evidence shows that the target capability has been achieved.

14. Skill Matrix and Maintenance Risk

Competency gaps can affect safety, reliability, quality, and recovery time.

A risk-based approach prioritizes gaps according to the consequences of missing capability.

Consider:

  • Severity of a possible failure.
  • Likelihood of the task being required.
  • Availability of alternate competent personnel.
  • Time required to obtain specialist assistance.
  • Consequences of delayed recovery.
  • Safety and quality implications.
  • Frequency of the task.
  • Complexity of the equipment.

A low-frequency skill can still be critical if failure consequences are severe.

For example, a rare safety-controller recovery procedure may deserve a high training and coverage priority even if it is used only occasionally.

15. Role-Based Competency Requirements

A single universal skill matrix may be too broad. Different roles need different competency profiles.

Role Typical competency emphasis
Operator Normal operation, abnormality recognition, safe stopping and escalation
Maintenance technician Inspection, routine diagnosis, component replacement within authorization
Senior technician Advanced diagnosis, complex repair, mentoring, verification
Controls engineer PLC, HMI, networks, controlled changes, software recovery
Instrumentation specialist Measurement systems, calibration, signal integrity, instrumentation faults
Maintenance planner PM strategy, work planning, asset history, materials and schedules
Maintenance supervisor Competency coverage, work authorization, risk, prioritization, performance
Reliability engineer Failure analysis, maintenance strategy, trends, reliability improvement
OT or automation specialist System architecture, security, network dependencies, backup and recovery

These are general examples. Actual roles and requirements depend on the organization, equipment, and formal authorization system.

16. Maintaining the Matrix Over Time

A skill matrix becomes unreliable if it is created once and never updated.

Review it when:

  • New equipment is introduced.
  • A control system or software platform changes.
  • A person completes a successful competency assessment.
  • A task is performed incorrectly or requires significant support.
  • A formal authorization expires or changes.
  • A new safety requirement is introduced.
  • A technician changes role.
  • An employee joins or leaves the team.
  • Repeated failures expose a capability gap.
  • A recovery or maintenance procedure is revised.

Periodic reviews should confirm that ratings remain supported by evidence. The review interval should reflect the skill, risk, changes in technology, and applicable site requirements.

A rating for a frequently used routine task may remain credible through recent observations. A rarely used, high-risk recovery skill may require a specific practical reassessment.

17. Skill Matrix Data Quality and Governance

A reliable matrix needs defined ownership and consistent rules.

Governance requirements

  • One controlled source of truth.
  • Unique employee and skill identifiers.
  • Standardized competency definitions.
  • Defined assessment and reassessment methods.
  • Recorded assessment evidence.
  • Clear ownership for updates.
  • Controlled access to personnel information.
  • Version history and change tracking.
  • Review dates.
  • Separation of competency and authorization records.
  • Defined rules for incomplete or expired evidence.

Employee competency records are sensitive workplace information. Access should be restricted to legitimate operational, training, and management needs.

Avoid unsupported ratings based on personal opinions or informal reputation. Ratings should be defensible through observable evidence.

18. Excel-Based Skill Matrix Design

Excel can support a small or medium-sized team when the workbook is properly structured.

Suggested worksheets

Worksheet Purpose
EMPLOYEES Employee identifiers, roles, departments, shifts, active status
SKILL_MASTER Skill IDs, names, categories, definitions
ROLE_REQUIREMENTS Required competency by role and skill
ASSESSMENTS Employee, skill, level, assessor, date, evidence
AUTHORIZATIONS Authorization scope, status, validity where applicable
DEVELOPMENT_PLAN Gaps, training actions, owners, target dates
DASHBOARD Coverage, critical gaps, overdue assessments, development status

Data model

A normalized assessment record should contain one employee, one skill, one assessment result, and one assessment date per record.

Example:

Employee ID Skill ID Level Assessment date Evidence ID
E001 PLC_DIAG 3 2026-09-10 ASSESS-104
E001 VFD_DIAG 2 2026-09-12 ASSESS-109
E002 PLC_DIAG 4 2026-09-14 ASSESS-112

This structure is easier to filter, audit, and analyze than manually entering every detail into a very wide spreadsheet.

A visual matrix can still be generated from the underlying structured records using PivotTables, formulas, or Power Query.

19. Useful Skill Matrix KPIs

KPI Calculation or definition Purpose
Required competency coverage Required skill assignments meeting the defined competency and authorization rules divided by applicable assignments Shows whether capability requirements are met
Critical skill gap count Number of critical requirements not met Highlights operational risk
Training action closure Closed development actions divided by actions due in the defined period Measures development follow-through
Assessment currency Applicable assessments within the defined review period divided by assessments requiring review Shows whether evidence is current
Shift coverage Critical skills available at the required level and authorization on each shift Supports shift planning
Specialist dependency Critical tasks dependent on one available competent person Identifies single points of failure
Practical assessment pass rate Successful assessments divided by completed assessments Indicates demonstrated outcomes
Repeat competency gap Recurring gap in a previously addressed skill Identifies ineffective development actions

Define the denominator and reporting period consistently. Exclude genuinely non-applicable requirements rather than counting them as gaps or successes.

Averages can hide critical weaknesses. For example, an excellent average skill score does not compensate for having no authorized person available for a critical task.

20. Practical Example: Automation Maintenance Team

Suppose a maintenance team supports PLCs, VFDs, CNC machines, HMIs, industrial networks, and a quality test bed.

The skill matrix reveals:

  • Several employees can perform routine PLC diagnostics.
  • Only one employee can troubleshoot a particular CNC control.
  • No employee has demonstrated the required level for recovering a monitoring server.
  • Multiple employees have completed VFD training, but only two have demonstrated independent troubleshooting.
  • Safety authorization requirements are met for some tasks but not all shifts.

The correct response is not to assign higher scores to make the dashboard look healthier.

Instead:

  1. Identify which gaps affect safety, production, quality, or recovery.
  2. Prioritize the most consequential gaps.
  3. Establish supervised practice and practical assessment plans.
  4. Document legacy-system procedures and verified backups.
  5. Train additional employees on critical systems.
  6. Confirm authorization requirements independently.
  7. Review actual shift coverage.
  8. Reassess competence after development.
  9. Monitor whether the dependency and recovery risks have decreased.

The matrix becomes a maintenance capability-planning tool rather than a decorative HR spreadsheet.

21. Common Skill Matrix Mistakes

Mistake Consequence Better practice
Ratings based on self-assessment only Capability may be overstated Use evidence and practical assessment
Training attendance treated as competence Independent ability remains unverified Assess performance after training
One broad skill called "PLC" Hides differences between diagnostic and programming capability Break it into assessable tasks
Competency and authorization combined Unsafe work allocation may result Maintain separate records
No required level defined Ratings have no operational meaning Define requirements by role and task
Matrix never updated Decisions rely on stale information Assign ownership and review triggers
Average score used as the only KPI Critical gaps are hidden Track risk-weighted gaps and shift coverage
No evidence reference Ratings cannot be audited Link assessments to evidence
No development follow-up Gaps remain unresolved Assign actions, owners, and target dates
Too many irrelevant skills Matrix becomes difficult to maintain Focus on actual work and future requirements
No single-point-of-failure review Specialist dependency remains hidden Track critical skill coverage and backups

22. Skill Matrix Audit Checklist

  • Required skills are derived from actual work and equipment.
  • Skills are defined clearly enough to assess.
  • Competency levels have consistent definitions.
  • Required levels are established for relevant roles and tasks.
  • Actual ratings are supported by evidence.
  • Assessment dates and assessors are recorded.
  • Authorization is tracked separately where required.
  • Critical skill gaps are identified.
  • Shift-wise capability coverage is reviewed.
  • Single-person technical dependencies are identified.
  • Training and development actions have owners and target dates.
  • Reassessment is completed after development.
  • New equipment and process changes trigger a competency review.
  • Employee information is appropriately protected.
  • Matrix changes are controlled and traceable.
  • KPIs highlight real capability risks rather than only average scores.

23. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a skill matrix?

A structured mapping of required skills against employees or roles, including demonstrated competency levels and development needs.

Q2. What is the difference between a skill matrix and a training matrix?

A skill matrix records competency against required skills. A training matrix records required and completed training. Training records can support competency assessments but do not automatically prove competence.

Q3. How do you determine competency levels?

Define observable performance criteria and assess the employee through practical demonstrations, controlled troubleshooting exercises, observation, and supporting evidence.

Q4. Why should authorization be separate from competency?

Technical ability does not automatically grant permission to perform regulated, safety-critical, or controlled work.

Q5. How can a skill matrix improve breakdown response?

It identifies who can independently diagnose critical equipment, which shifts lack required capability, and where specialist support or cross-training is needed.

Q6. What is a single point of failure in a skill matrix?

A critical task that depends on one person's availability or knowledge, creating risk when that person is absent or unavailable.

Q7. How do you prioritize skill gaps?

Assess their consequences for safety, production, quality, recovery time, and operational resilience, then assign development actions based on risk.

Q8. How do you measure training effectiveness?

Verify that the employee demonstrates the target skill through an appropriate assessment and, where relevant, confirm improved performance in actual work.

Q9. Why are competency averages insufficient?

Averages can hide missing specialist capability or a lack of authorized personnel for critical tasks. Coverage must be evaluated at the individual skill and shift level.

Q10. How should the skill matrix be maintained?

Use a controlled source of truth, standardized skill definitions, evidence-backed assessments, assigned ownership, periodic reviews, and updates triggered by personnel or technology changes.

24. Final Principles

A skill matrix should connect workforce capability to the actual technical and operational requirements of the plant.

Its value comes from three things:

  1. Accurate assessment: ratings reflect demonstrated capability.
  2. Risk visibility: critical gaps and single-person dependencies are visible.
  3. Action: identified gaps lead to training, supervised practice, authorization checks, documentation, and reassessment.

The objective is to ensure that critical maintenance and automation work can be performed safely, competently, and reliably across the shifts and systems the organization must support.
