1. Definition

FAT (Factory Acceptance Test) is a planned and documented set of inspections and tests performed at the supplier's or manufacturer's facility to verify that equipment or a system meets agreed specifications and acceptance criteria before delivery to the customer.

FAT helps identify design defects, programming errors, configuration problems, missing functions, and integration issues while the equipment is still at the manufacturing or integration facility.

Examples:

  • Testing a PLC-controlled machine before it is shipped to an automotive plant.
  • Verifying robot sequences and PLC handshakes at a system integrator's facility.
  • Checking CNC machine functions before delivery.
  • Testing HMI screens, alarms, recipes, and operating modes.
  • Verifying that a SCADA application displays the correct simulated or available test data.

Key principle: FAT provides evidence that the system meets the agreed factory-test requirements. It does not automatically prove that the equipment will work correctly after installation at the customer's site.

2. Why FAT is required

Industrial automation systems can be expensive and complicated to modify after delivery. Detecting defects at the supplier's facility generally makes correction easier than discovering them after installation and production integration.

FAT is intended to:

  1. Verify compliance with the approved functional specification.
  2. Check hardware, software, and configuration before shipment.
  3. Verify the required machine sequences and operating modes.
  4. Test alarms, faults, interlocks, permissives, and defined trip responses.
  5. Check communication and interfaces between integrated devices.
  6. Identify incomplete functions and defects.
  7. Provide objective evidence for customer acceptance.
  8. Reduce the risk of expensive rework during site installation.
  9. Confirm that required documentation and deliverables are available.
  10. Establish a record of the tested system version.

FAT is especially valuable when a project includes custom PLC programming, robot integration, CNC interfaces, safety systems, or several interconnected automation subsystems.

3. Where FAT fits in a project

A typical project sequence is:

Requirements and specifications
              ↓
Engineering and design
              ↓
Manufacturing and programming
              ↓
Integration at supplier's facility
              ↓
FAT preparation and execution
              ↓
Defect correction and retesting
              ↓
Customer acceptance / release for shipment
              ↓
Transportation and site installation
              ↓
SAT: Site Acceptance Test
              ↓
Commissioning and operational readiness

The precise sequence depends on the contract. Some projects conduct FAT in stages, while others require partial acceptance before the complete system is available.

FAT is not simply a demonstration of the machine running. It is a structured test against defined requirements.

4. FAT versus related terms

Term Location or context Main purpose
FAT Supplier's or manufacturer's facility Verify agreed functions and acceptance criteria before delivery
SAT Customer's installation site Verify the installed system and site-specific interfaces
Commissioning Installation and operational-readiness process Prepare and verify the system for intended operation
Routine test Defined manufacturing or quality-control process Verify specified characteristics of a product or component
Inspection Any relevant project stage Examine installation, workmanship, configuration, or documentation
Validation Defined requirements and intended-use context Establish documented evidence that requirements are satisfied

FAT and SAT may form part of the broader commissioning and acceptance process. Their exact scope should be established in the project documentation.

5. FAT test planning

A FAT should be planned before testing begins. The customer and supplier need a common understanding of what will be tested, how it will be tested, and what constitutes a pass.

A typical FAT plan identifies:

  • Equipment and system scope.
  • Approved drawings and functional specifications.
  • Software and firmware versions.
  • Required test cases.
  • Test setup and prerequisites.
  • Test equipment and simulation methods.
  • Expected results and acceptance criteria.
  • Customer and supplier responsibilities.
  • Defect classification and reporting method.
  • Retest and approval process.
  • Required test records and supporting evidence.

Example of a FAT test case

Field Example
Test ID FAT-PLC-012
Function Fixture clamping sequence
Preconditions PLC powered, test mode enabled, required simulated inputs available
Test action Initiate the clamping sequence
Expected result Clamp command is issued and the sequence waits for closed feedback
Pass criteria Correct command and sequence state are observed
Evidence Recorded PLC states and signed test sheet
Result Pass, fail, or blocked

The test case should be specific enough that two competent testers can execute it and evaluate the result consistently.

Not every test can be completed fully at the supplier's facility. Some physical processes, site utilities, upstream/downstream interfaces, or installation-dependent functions may need to be verified during SAT or commissioning.

7. FAT methods: actual equipment versus simulation

A factory test may use real hardware, simulated signals, virtual devices, or a combination of methods.

Actual hardware testing

The test uses installed components such as PLCs, drives, remote I/O, HMI panels, sensors, or actuators.

Advantages:

  • Verifies real hardware and configuration.
  • Can expose wiring, addressing, and device-integration problems.
  • Provides stronger evidence for functions that can be physically exercised.

Limitations:

  • Full production machinery may not be available.
  • Site-specific utilities and interfaces may be absent.
  • Some physical process conditions cannot be reproduced at the supplier's facility.

Simulation-based testing

The test uses simulated inputs, software models, test harnesses, or virtual controllers where appropriate.

Advantages:

  • Allows repeatable tests of many operating conditions.
  • Makes unusual or abnormal conditions easier to reproduce.
  • Can test sequence logic before all physical equipment is available.

Limitations:

  • Simulation may not reproduce physical dynamics, electrical noise, mechanical behavior, or actual device failure modes.
  • A simulated sensor signal does not prove that the real sensor is wired and installed correctly.
  • Simulated network behavior does not necessarily represent the final plant network.

A simulated FAT result proves only what the simulation and test setup actually cover. The test report should identify simulated elements and functions that remain unverified.

8. Example: FAT for an automotive robot station

Suppose a system integrator builds a robot welding station containing a robot controller, PLC, fixture, HMI, and associated drives.

Test 1: Hardware and configuration

Verify the installed devices against the approved equipment list. Record the PLC, robot, drive, and HMI software or configuration versions as required.

Test 2: I/O mapping

Check the defined inputs and outputs, including:

  • Part present.
  • Clamp open and closed feedback.
  • Robot ready.
  • Robot fault.
  • Cycle start request.
  • Cycle complete.

Where real field devices are unavailable, identify which signals are simulated.

Test 3: Normal sequence

Verify the specified sequence:

Part present
     ↓
Fixture clamps
     ↓
Clamp feedback confirmed
     ↓
Robot cycle permitted
     ↓
Robot completes programmed operation
     ↓
Cycle-complete feedback received
     ↓
Fixture releases

Test 4: Abnormal conditions

Test the agreed scenarios, such as:

  • Part-present signal absent.
  • Clamp feedback fails to arrive.
  • Robot fault becomes active.
  • Cycle-complete signal times out.
  • Communication between relevant controllers is interrupted.

Confirm that the observed response matches the specified behavior. Do not assume that every fault should produce the same stop response.

Test 5: Operator interface

Verify:

  • Machine status indications.
  • Alarm descriptions.
  • Manual and automatic mode behavior.
  • Reset conditions.
  • Diagnostic information.
  • Access controls where required.

Test 6: Safety-related verification

Verify the safety functions within the approved FAT scope and record any functions that require final site verification or validation.

Testing at the supplier's facility must follow the approved safety procedure. Simulation or software indication alone is not proof that the physical safety function is effective.

Test 7: FAT report

Record each test result, defect, corrective action, retest result, and outstanding item. Obtain the required approvals before the equipment is released under the project's acceptance process.

9. FAT defects and retesting

A FAT frequently identifies defects. Finding defects is not automatically evidence of a poor FAT; identifying them before shipment is one of its main purposes.

A useful defect record includes:

Field Purpose
Defect ID Unique reference
Description What failed or was incomplete
Test reference Which test exposed the defect
Severity Impact on safety, functionality, quality, or acceptance
Reproduction steps How the issue can be observed
Responsible party Who owns the corrective action
Corrective action What was changed or repaired
Retest result Whether the original failure is resolved
Closure status Open, corrected, retested, accepted, or otherwise dispositioned

Example

A robot-station FAT reveals that the PLC does not detect a missing cycle-complete signal.

The supplier corrects the timeout logic, reruns the test, verifies the alarm and recovery behavior, and records the retest result.

The original failure must not simply be marked as closed because the code was changed. The relevant behavior needs to be retested.

For a significant program or configuration change, the team should also assess whether related tests need to be repeated. Correcting one function can unintentionally affect another.

10. FAT acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria define what must be true for a test to pass.

Examples:

  • The specified sequence executes in the correct order.
  • Required feedback signals are interpreted correctly.
  • Defined alarms appear for the corresponding conditions.
  • A specified trip response occurs under the test condition.
  • Required data is transmitted to the correct interface.
  • The system meets the agreed performance threshold.
  • Required documents and backups are delivered.

A result should be evaluated against the agreed criteria, not simply the opinion that the equipment appears to work.

Common acceptance outcomes

  • Pass: The test meets its acceptance criteria.
  • Fail: The test does not meet its acceptance criteria.
  • Blocked or not tested: The test could not be completed because a prerequisite or required resource was unavailable.
  • Conditional acceptance: The authorized parties accept the system subject to explicitly recorded conditions, where the contract permits it.

A blocked test is not a pass. Likewise, conditional acceptance does not mean that every outstanding issue has disappeared through the magic of paperwork.

11. FAT limitations

Passing FAT does not guarantee that the complete production installation will operate correctly.

FAT may not fully verify:

  • Site power quality and installed electrical infrastructure.
  • Actual field wiring after transportation and installation.
  • Plant network behavior and cybersecurity configuration.
  • Actual upstream and downstream line coordination.
  • Site utilities, compressed air, hydraulics, and environmental conditions.
  • Final physical safety-device installation and site-specific risks.
  • Long-duration reliability or sustained production performance.
  • Real product quality under actual manufacturing conditions.

These limitations are why SAT, commissioning, and production validation remain important.

12. Common FAT mistakes

  • Starting FAT without approved specifications and acceptance criteria.
  • Demonstrating only the normal production cycle.
  • Ignoring alarms, trips, interlocks, and recovery behavior.
  • Failing to test interfaces between PLCs, robots, CNCs, and drives.
  • Treating simulated inputs as proof of physical installation.
  • Failing to record the tested software and configuration versions.
  • Marking a test as passed without objective evidence.
  • Closing defects without retesting the affected behavior.
  • Accepting undocumented deviations from the agreed design.
  • Failing to identify which tests must be completed at the customer's site.
  • Shipping equipment without the required approval or disposition of outstanding defects.

13. Interview questions and answers

Q1. What is FAT?

FAT is a planned and documented test process at the supplier's or manufacturer's facility to verify that equipment meets agreed requirements before delivery.

Q2. What is the primary purpose of FAT?

To identify defects and verify required functionality before shipment, reducing the risk of expensive corrections after site installation.

Q3. What is the difference between FAT and SAT?

FAT is performed at the supplier's facility before delivery. SAT is performed at the customer's site to verify the installed system and its site-specific interfaces.

Q4. Can FAT be performed using simulation?

Yes, when simulation is suitable for the agreed test objectives. The test records must identify what was simulated and what remains to be verified using actual equipment.

Q5. What is an FAT test case?

A documented test specifying the function, prerequisites, actions, expected results, acceptance criteria, and recorded outcome.

Q6. Why must abnormal conditions be tested during FAT?

They verify that faults, missing feedback, communication loss, and other specified conditions produce the required diagnostics and protective responses.

Q7. What is a FAT punch list?

A record of defects, incomplete work, deviations, or outstanding items identified during testing, along with their ownership and closure status.

Q8. Does passing FAT mean that commissioning is complete?

No. FAT occurs before delivery and cannot establish that the final site installation, utilities, field wiring, and production interfaces are all correct.

Q9. What should happen when a test fails?

Record the failure, investigate the cause, correct the problem, retest the affected function, and evaluate whether the changes require additional regression testing.

Q10. Why should software versions be recorded during FAT?

The record establishes which program and configuration were actually tested. It helps ensure that the delivered system corresponds to the tested and accepted version.

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
RELEASE FOR DELIVERY AS AUTHORIZED

For an automation engineer, FAT is an opportunity to verify the integrated design before it reaches the production plant. The most valuable FAT is not the one that produces the fewest reported defects; it is the one that systematically tests the agreed requirements, exposes meaningful problems, and provides reliable evidence of what has and has not been verified.
