1. What is Discrete Automation?

Discrete automation is automation used to manufacture, assemble, move, inspect, or test individual products, parts, or units through distinct operations.

The word discrete is the key.

A discrete manufacturing process deals with things that can generally be treated as individual identifiable units.

Examples:

  • automobile
  • engine
  • gearbox
  • shaft
  • bracket
  • bearing
  • electronic PCB
  • bottle
  • packaged product
  • machined component

The product exists as an individual unit.

For example:

Part 1
Part 2
Part 3
Part 4
Part 5

Each part can be counted, tracked, inspected, rejected, transferred, assembled, or reworked.

That is fundamentally different from a process where material continuously flows through the plant, such as:

liquid
gas
steam
oil
chemical solution

2. The Core Idea

The simplest mental model is:

Discrete automation controls a sequence of operations performed on individual products or components.

For example:

Part arrives
      ↓
Part detected
      ↓
Part clamped
      ↓
Machining
      ↓
Part unclamped
      ↓
Part transferred
      ↓
Inspection
      ↓
PASS / FAIL

Each stage is a distinct event or operation.

The automation system determines:

  • when an operation starts
  • whether it is allowed to start
  • what sequence must be followed
  • whether the previous operation is complete
  • whether the part is present
  • whether the machine is ready
  • whether a fault exists
  • when the next operation can begin

This is why sequence logic is so important in discrete automation.

3. What Makes a Process "Discrete"?

There are several characteristics.

3.1 Individual products

The product can be identified as an individual unit.

Example:

Part A
Part B
Part C

rather than an uninterrupted quantity of material.

3.2 Distinct operations

The manufacturing process consists of identifiable steps.

For example:

Load
↓
Clamp
↓
Drill
↓
Unclamp
↓
Unload

Each operation has a beginning and an end.

3.3 Sequential behavior

Operations often need to happen in a particular order.

For example:

Clamp
   ↓
Clamp confirmation
   ↓
Spindle ON
   ↓
Spindle at speed
   ↓
Machining
   ↓
Spindle OFF
   ↓
Unclamp

The machine should not simply execute everything simultaneously because apparently even machines appreciate being told what happens first.

3.4 Events and states

Discrete automation commonly deals with conditions such as:

READY
RUN
STOP
FAULT
PART PRESENT
CLAMPED
CYCLE COMPLETE
PASS
FAIL

These are distinct states or events.

8. Controller

In Industry 3.0 discrete automation, the PLC is one of the most important controllers.

Examples from your broader study area include:

  • Allen-Bradley PLCs
  • Siemens PLCs
  • Mitsubishi PLCs
  • other industrial controllers

The PLC receives inputs, executes logic, and controls outputs.

Simplified:

      ↓
    Sensors
      ↓

      ↓
    Logic
      ↓

      ↓

9. PLC Logic in Discrete Automation

The PLC may perform:

  • sequence control
  • interlocking
  • permissive checking
  • timing
  • counting
  • position control
  • motor control
  • valve control
  • fault detection
  • machine state management
  • communication
  • safety-related coordination

For example:

IF
    Part_Present = TRUE
AND
    Door_Closed = TRUE
AND
    Clamp_Retracted = TRUE
AND
    Machine_Ready = TRUE

THEN
    Clamp_Extend = TRUE

Then:

IF Clamp_Extended = TRUE
THEN
    Start_Machining = TRUE

That is the essence of discrete sequence control.

10. Discrete Automation Is Heavily Sequence-Oriented

This is one of the most important concepts to understand.

A discrete machine frequently behaves like:

STATE 0
IDLE
  ↓
STATE 1
WAIT FOR PART
  ↓
STATE 2
CLAMP
  ↓
STATE 3
PROCESS
  ↓
STATE 4
UNCLAMP
  ↓
STATE 5
UNLOAD
  ↓
STATE 6
CYCLE COMPLETE
  ↓
STATE 0

This is effectively a state-based process.

The machine changes state when certain conditions are satisfied.

11. Example: Automated Assembly

Suppose a machine assembles a bearing into a housing.

The sequence could be:

1. Wait for housing
2. Detect housing
3. Clamp housing
4. Verify clamp
5. Detect bearing
6. Pick bearing
7. Move bearing to housing
8. Position bearing
9. Press bearing
10. Verify press position/force
11. Release component
12. Inspect assembly
13. Mark PASS/FAIL
14. Transfer assembly
15. Cycle complete

Every step is discrete.

The controller has to coordinate:

  • sensors
  • pneumatic cylinders
  • servo axes
  • robot
  • gripper
  • press
  • inspection system
  • conveyor

This is exactly where discrete automation becomes powerful.

15. Discrete Signals

Discrete automation commonly uses digital signals.

A digital signal generally has two logical states:

0 = OFF
1 = ON

or:

FALSE
TRUE

Examples:

Part present = 1
Door closed = 1
Motor running = 1
Clamp extended = 1
Fault = 0/1

This is one reason the term discrete is associated with digital control.

But an important distinction:

Discrete automation does not mean that every signal in the system must be digital.

A discrete machine can also use analog signals.

For example:

  • pressure
  • temperature
  • position
  • force
  • current
  • speed

So:

Discrete manufacturing ≠ only digital signals.

16. Discrete Automation Can Use Analog Data

Suppose a press machine performs an assembly operation.

The machine may use:

Part detected → digital
Clamp confirmed → digital
Press start → digital

Press force → analog
Press position → analog
Temperature → analog

The manufacturing operation is still discrete automation because the product is processed as an individual unit through distinct operations.

This distinction is important.

17. Discrete vs Continuous

A simple comparison:

Discrete Automation Continuous Process Automation
Individual products/units Continuous material flow
Distinct operations Continuous process
Strong sequence orientation Strong process-variable orientation
Parts can be counted individually Material often measured by flow/volume
Machines/robots/conveyors common Pumps/valves/process equipment common
PLC/CNC/robot controllers common PLC/DCS/process controllers common
Automotive manufacturing Oil refining
Assembly Chemical processing
Machining Continuous fluid processing

Do not confuse continuous with simply "the machine runs continuously."

A CNC can run for hours continuously while still being a discrete manufacturing process because it processes individual parts.

24. What Does "Automation" Actually Mean Here?

It is not simply:

"PLC controls machine."

Automation means that the system performs decisions and actions with reduced direct human intervention.

For example, instead of an operator manually doing:

Detect part
↓
Clamp
↓
Start machine
↓
Wait
↓
Stop machine
↓
Unclamp
↓
Remove part

the automated system performs these operations automatically.

The operator may instead:

  • load material
  • monitor the process
  • respond to faults
  • perform setup
  • change tooling
  • approve production
  • perform maintenance

28. Discrete Automation and Maintenance

For maintenance, a discrete machine can be viewed as:

INPUT
↓
CONTROL
↓
SEQUENCE
↓
ACTUATION
↓
PROCESS
↓
FEEDBACK

A fault can occur anywhere.

Example:

Input problem

Part sensor fails.

Result:

PLC never detects part
→ sequence does not start

Control problem

PLC logic/configuration problem.

Result:

Correct input
→ incorrect decision
→ wrong output

Output problem

PLC commands valve ON but output hardware fails.

Result:

PLC output = ON
but actuator = OFF

Actuator problem

Solenoid operates but cylinder cannot move.

Feedback problem

Cylinder physically reaches position, but sensor fails to report it.

Result:

Machine waits indefinitely

This is why troubleshooting discrete automation requires understanding the entire chain, not merely looking at the PLC program.

29. A Practical Troubleshooting Chain

When a discrete machine does not perform an operation:

1. Is the required condition actually present?
        ↓
2. Is the input reaching the controller?
        ↓
3. Is the logic allowing the operation?
        ↓
4. Is the output being generated?
        ↓
5. Is the actuator responding?
        ↓
6. Is the physical mechanism moving?
        ↓
7. Is feedback confirming the movement?
        ↓
8. Is the sequence advancing?

This is extremely useful in real maintenance work.

A discrete system can become extremely complex.

Multiple PLCs
Multiple robots
CNC machines
Vision systems
Safety controllers
Servo systems
Industrial networks

So:

Discrete ≠ simple

and:

Continuous ≠ necessarily more advanced

They describe the nature of the manufacturing process, not its technological sophistication.

34. The Most Important Difference: Discrete vs Process

Keep this comparison ready:

DISCRETE

Part
 ↓
Operation
 ↓
Part
 ↓
Operation
 ↓
Finished product

versus:

PROCESS

Raw material
 ↓
Continuous process
 ↓
Controlled variables
 ↓
Finished material

Discrete automation often asks:

What should happen next?

Process automation often asks:

What should the process variable be maintained at?

Examples of discrete concerns:

  • Is the part present?
  • Is the clamp closed?
  • Is the door closed?
  • Is the robot ready?
  • Has the operation completed?
  • Should the conveyor start?

Examples of process concerns:

  • Is temperature at the setpoint?
  • Is pressure maintained?
  • Is flow correct?
  • Is level controlled?
  • Is composition within limits?

There can absolutely be overlap, but this distinction is an excellent foundation.

36. Interview-Level Questions

Q1. What is discrete automation?

Discrete automation is the automation of manufacturing processes involving individual products or units and distinct, sequenced operations.

Q2. Give examples of discrete automation.

Automated assembly, CNC machining, robotic welding, packaging, material handling, automated inspection and testing.

Q3. Why is PLC widely used in discrete automation?

Because discrete machines require sequencing, logical decisions, interlocks, timing, counting, I/O control and coordination of multiple devices.

Q4. Is discrete automation limited to digital signals?

No. Discrete automation can use both digital and analog signals. The term refers primarily to the nature of the manufacturing process, not exclusively to signal type.

Q5. Is CNC discrete automation?

Yes. CNC machining processes individual workpieces through defined machining operations.

Q6. Is robotics part of discrete automation?

Yes, robots are commonly used in discrete manufacturing for handling, assembly, welding, inspection and other operations.

Q7. What is the basic control principle?

Sense → Decide → Act → Verify → Continue sequence.

Q8. What is the difference between discrete manufacturing and continuous manufacturing?

Discrete manufacturing processes individual identifiable units through distinct operations, while continuous manufacturing processes material continuously through a process.

Q9. Can a discrete automation system contain analog signals?

Yes. For example, a discrete assembly machine may use analog force, pressure, temperature or position measurements.

Q10. Does discrete mean simple?

No. Discrete automation systems can contain highly complex PLC, robot, CNC, servo, vision, safety and networking systems.

37. Final Consolidated Definition

Discrete automation is the use of automatic control systems to manufacture, assemble, handle, inspect, test, or process individual products or units through defined and usually sequential operations.

The core characteristics are:

Individual units

→ distinct operations

→ sequence

→ sensing

→ decision/logic

→ actuation

→ feedback/verification

→ next operation

For your Industry 3.0 foundation, the most important mental chain is:

Part → Sensor → PLC/Controller → Sequence Logic → Actuator → Operation → Feedback → Next State

And the distinction to retain before moving to the next topic is:

Discrete automation deals primarily with individual units and distinct operations; process automation deals primarily with continuously varying process conditions.
