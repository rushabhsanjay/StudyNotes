1. Definition

Commissioning is the systematic process of inspecting, configuring, testing, verifying, and validating equipment or a complete system to confirm that it has been installed correctly, functions as intended, meets specified requirements, and is ready for operation.

Commissioning connects engineering design with actual industrial operation. It verifies that the individual components, control logic, interfaces, safety functions, and overall process work together as required.

Examples:

  • Commissioning a new PLC-controlled machine before production starts.
  • Commissioning a VFD-driven conveyor after installation.
  • Commissioning a robot welding station integrated with fixtures, PLCs, and safety systems.
  • Commissioning a replacement CNC controller after a retrofit.
  • Commissioning a SCADA system that collects PLC data and displays alarms and trends.

Key principle: Installing equipment does not prove that it works correctly. Commissioning provides documented evidence that the system is ready for its intended use.

2. Why commissioning is required

A machine may contain individually functional components but still fail when integrated into the production line.

For example, a PLC may execute its program correctly, a robot may move correctly in isolation, and a fixture may clamp correctly in manual mode. However, the production cycle may fail because the robot and PLC use different handshake signals or because the fixture feedback arrives too late.

Commissioning helps to:

  1. Verify that the installed system matches the approved design.
  2. Confirm correct wiring, power supplies, network connections, and device configuration.
  3. Test PLC logic, HMI screens, drives, CNCs, robots, and other control devices.
  4. Verify sequences, interlocks, permissives, alarms, trips, and recovery behavior.
  5. Confirm integration between machines and subsystems.
  6. Validate required safety functions using approved procedures.
  7. Identify defects before production release.
  8. Establish baseline performance and configuration records.
  9. Train operators and maintenance personnel where required.
  10. Confirm readiness for handover to production and maintenance.

3. Where commissioning fits in a project

Commissioning is one stage in a larger equipment lifecycle. Its exact boundaries vary by company, contract, and project methodology.

Requirements and specifications
              ↓
Engineering design
              ↓
Equipment manufacture and programming
              ↓
FAT: Factory Acceptance Test
              ↓
Transportation and installation
              ↓
SAT: Site Acceptance Test
              ↓
Commissioning and integrated validation
              ↓
Production trials and performance verification
              ↓
Handover and operational support

This is a common sequence, not a universal rule. Some commissioning activities begin during installation, some occur before or alongside SAT, and some acceptance activities are part of commissioning itself.

Related terms

Term Main purpose
Installation Physically place and connect equipment
Configuration Set device parameters, addresses, software settings, and operating options
Testing Execute checks to verify particular functions or requirements
FAT Test and assess equipment at the supplier's facility before delivery or shipment
SAT Test and assess equipment at the actual installation site
Commissioning Prepare and verify the system for intended operation, including integration and readiness
Validation Establish documented evidence that specified requirements or intended use are satisfied
Handover Transfer the accepted system, documentation, and responsibilities to the operating organization

These activities overlap. FAT and SAT are often defined contractually as acceptance stages, while commissioning covers a broader set of preparation, integration, and operational-readiness activities.

4. Types of commissioning activities

Activity What is checked Industrial example
Mechanical commissioning Alignment, mounting, movement, lubrication, and installation Checking conveyor alignment
Electrical commissioning Wiring, earthing, protection, voltage, and electrical integrity Verifying a motor feeder
Instrumentation commissioning Sensor installation, calibration, scaling, and signal response Verifying a 4-20 mA pressure transmitter
PLC commissioning Program execution, I/O mapping, sequence logic, and diagnostics Testing a clamp sequence
Drive commissioning Motor parameters, protection, direction, and speed control Configuring a VFD
CNC commissioning Axis configuration, homing, interlocks, and machine functions Testing axis movement after a controller retrofit
Robot commissioning Frames, tools, payloads, programs, and interfaces Verifying robot-to-fixture coordination
Network commissioning Device addresses, communication, data exchange, and fault behavior Verifying PLC-to-remote-I/O communication
Safety commissioning Required safety functions and protective responses Testing guard interlocks and emergency-stop functions
Process commissioning Product flow, cycle sequence, process parameters, and quality Verifying a welding cycle
Performance commissioning Throughput, cycle time, stability, and specified performance Confirming required production rate

Commissioning must cover the interactions between these areas, not merely check each item independently.

5. The commissioning process

Stage 1: Review requirements and documentation

Before energizing equipment, review the approved engineering documents and requirements.

Typical documents include:

  • Electrical schematics and wiring diagrams.
  • PLC, HMI, CNC, and robot program versions.
  • I/O lists and tag databases.
  • Network architecture and IP-address allocation.
  • Pneumatic and hydraulic diagrams.
  • Equipment manuals and parameter lists.
  • Functional specifications and sequence descriptions.
  • Risk assessments and safety-function documentation.
  • Inspection and test plans.
  • Approved commissioning checklists.

The objective is to establish what the system is supposed to do and what evidence will demonstrate correct operation.

Stage 2: Verify installation readiness

Inspect the installed equipment before functional testing.

Typical checks include:

  • Correct mounting, alignment, and mechanical clearances.
  • Correct cable routing, terminations, and identification.
  • Protective earthing and electrical protection.
  • Correct power-supply ratings and polarity.
  • Pneumatic and hydraulic connections.
  • Network cabling and device identification.
  • Guards, access points, and required safety equipment.
  • Completion of required inspections and permits.

Any defect that makes energization or testing unsafe must be resolved before proceeding.

Stage 3: Verify power and device configuration

Energize equipment in accordance with the approved procedure.

Check:

  • Control and power supplies.
  • PLC and remote-I/O module status.
  • Device addresses and communication.
  • VFD motor data and operating parameters.
  • Sensor and actuator configuration.
  • CNC and robot configuration backups.
  • HMI communication and displayed status.
  • Device firmware and software versions where relevant.

Configuration changes should be controlled and documented. A working machine with undocumented parameters is a future breakdown disguised as a successful project.

Stage 4: Perform individual device tests

Test each relevant component or subsystem before integrated automatic operation.

Examples:

  • Verify that each sensor changes state at the correct physical condition.
  • Verify that each actuator responds to its intended command.
  • Confirm motor direction and drive response using an approved test procedure.
  • Check that analog inputs display the correct engineering values.
  • Verify PLC I/O mapping.
  • Confirm HMI commands and status indications.
  • Test device diagnostics and fault reporting.

Where practical, compare the physical condition with the electrical signal and the value shown in the control system.

Stage 5: Test sequences and control logic

Verify the machine's intended operating sequence.

For a simple fixture:

Part detected
     ↓
Clamp command issued
     ↓
Clamp-closed feedback confirmed
     ↓
Process operation enabled
     ↓
Process completion confirmed
     ↓
Clamp released
     ↓
Part removed / cycle complete

Check:

  • Correct sequence order.
  • Expected transition conditions.
  • Interlocks and permissives.
  • Timeouts and fault responses.
  • Alarm messages and diagnostics.
  • Manual, setup, and automatic modes.
  • Recovery from incomplete cycles.
  • Behavior after power interruption or communication loss, where applicable.

Testing must verify both normal behavior and defined abnormal-condition responses.

Stage 6: Verify system integration

Industrial equipment rarely operates in isolation. Test the interfaces between subsystems.

Examples:

  • PLC-to-robot handshake.
  • PLC-to-CNC cycle-start and cycle-complete signals.
  • VFD run commands and running/fault feedback.
  • Remote-I/O communication.
  • Barcode or identification systems.
  • Safety-controller interfaces.
  • HMI and SCADA data exchange.
  • Upstream and downstream line coordination.

For a robot station, a handshake might include Robot_Ready, Start_Request, Robot_Busy, Cycle_Complete, and Robot_Fault.

Each signal needs a defined meaning, ownership, expected timing, and behavior if communication or feedback is lost.

Stage 7: Verify safety functions

Safety verification must follow the approved risk assessment, safety requirements, and validation procedure.

Depending on the machine, checks may include:

  • Emergency-stop functions.
  • Guard-door interlocks.
  • Safety scanners or light curtains.
  • Safe torque off or other drive safety functions.
  • Safety-related stopping behavior.
  • Prevention of unexpected restart.
  • Reset behavior after a safety event.
  • Safety-system diagnostics.

The tests must confirm the required protective response under the specified conditions. Merely observing that an HMI displays an emergency-stop alarm is not sufficient evidence that the safety function works correctly.

Safety-related tests must be conducted by authorized personnel using approved procedures. Do not bypass safety devices to accelerate commissioning.

Stage 8: Run production trials and verify performance

After the required preliminary checks pass, conduct controlled trials using the approved test plan.

Depending on the project, verify:

  • Correct production sequence.
  • Cycle time and throughput.
  • Product quality.
  • Repeatability and process stability.
  • Changeover and recipe behavior.
  • Fault recovery.
  • Performance over the required operating period.
  • Required upstream and downstream coordination.

Passing a short functional test does not automatically prove that the system can sustain the required production rate or quality.

Stage 9: Document results and hand over

Record the commissioning results and unresolved issues.

Typical handover deliverables include:

  • Completed inspection and test checklists.
  • FAT and SAT results where applicable.
  • Open-item or punch-list records.
  • Final PLC, HMI, drive, CNC, and robot backups.
  • Approved parameter and configuration records.
  • Updated electrical and network drawings.
  • Alarm and fault lists.
  • Maintenance and operating instructions.
  • Spare-parts information.
  • Training records.
  • Outstanding risks, restrictions, and corrective actions.
  • Formal acceptance and handover records.

A system should not be represented as fully commissioned if required acceptance criteria remain unmet. Any permitted outstanding items need explicit authorization, ownership, and a defined closure plan.

6. Commissioning checklists

A checklist makes the process repeatable and provides evidence that important checks were completed.

A typical commissioning checklist may contain:

No. Check Expected result Evidence
1 Installation inspection Matches approved drawings and specifications Inspection record
2 Protective earthing Meets the applicable requirements Test record
3 Power-supply verification Correct voltage and polarity Measurement record
4 PLC and I/O status No unresolved module faults Controller diagnostics
5 Sensor verification Correct state under the defined condition I/O test sheet
6 Actuator verification Correct response to command Functional test record
7 Drive verification Correct motor behavior and feedback Drive test record
8 Sequence verification Correct sequence transitions Test results
9 Alarm and trip verification Correct indication and protective response Functional test record
10 Safety-function verification Required safety response demonstrated Approved validation record
11 Communication verification Required data exchange is reliable Network test record
12 Performance verification Meets specified acceptance criteria Production trial data
13 Backup and documentation Approved final versions stored Backup register
14 Handover Required deliverables accepted Handover record

The actual checklist must be specific to the machine, project, and applicable standards. A generic checklist is a starting point, not a substitute for engineering requirements.

7. Example: Commissioning a VFD-driven conveyor

Suppose a new conveyor is installed with a motor, VFD, PLC, and HMI.

Step 1: Installation and electrical checks

Verify motor connections, protective earthing, drive installation, cable routing, and the required electrical protection.

Step 2: Drive configuration

Enter the correct motor nameplate information and approved application settings. Verify the intended control mode and command source.

Step 3: Individual operation

Use the approved test procedure to verify the motor's direction and behavior. Confirm that mechanical movement is unrestricted and that the system is safe to test.

Step 4: PLC integration

Verify the PLC run command, drive-ready status, running feedback, speed reference, and fault feedback.

Step 5: Interlocks and trips

Verify that the conveyor cannot start when required conditions are unsatisfied. Test defined fault responses, such as drive fault feedback, according to the approved test plan.

Step 6: HMI and diagnostics

Confirm that the operator can see the conveyor state and relevant faults. Verify that the displayed information matches the actual control-system state.

Step 7: Production trial

Run the conveyor under controlled conditions and confirm that it meets the required performance and process criteria.

Step 8: Handover

Save the final drive parameters, PLC/HMI versions, test results, drawings, and maintenance information.

This process verifies the complete system, rather than assuming that a successful motor test proves the conveyor is ready for production.

8. Commissioning versus maintenance

Commissioning and maintenance use many of the same technical skills, but their starting conditions differ.

Aspect Commissioning Maintenance
Starting point New, modified, relocated, or newly integrated equipment Equipment already in service
Main objective Establish and verify readiness for intended operation Preserve or restore required performance
Typical work Configuration, installation checks, functional tests, integration Inspection, fault diagnosis, repair, preventive work
Baseline Establishes initial operating and configuration records Compares current condition against the baseline
Testing Demonstrates compliance with specified requirements Demonstrates that repair or maintenance restored the required condition
Documentation Commissioning and acceptance records Maintenance history, breakdown records, and service reports

After a major retrofit, maintenance personnel may need to repeat selected commissioning activities to verify that the modified system works correctly.

For example, replacing a PLC with a different model may require I/O verification, program validation, network checks, sequence testing, safety validation where affected, and production trials.

9. Common commissioning mistakes

  • Energizing equipment before required installation and safety checks are complete.
  • Testing individual components but not their interfaces.
  • Failing to verify motor direction and physical actuator movement.
  • Using incorrect device parameters or motor nameplate data.
  • Ignoring alarms, trips, and recovery behavior during testing.
  • Testing only normal operation and not defined abnormal conditions.
  • Changing PLC logic without recording the version or validating the change.
  • Bypassing interlocks or safety functions to make a test pass.
  • Accepting displayed signals without checking whether they reflect the real physical condition.
  • Declaring success after a brief run without checking the required performance criteria.
  • Failing to save final software, parameters, drawings, and test records.
  • Handing over a machine with unresolved critical defects or undocumented restrictions.

10. Interview questions and answers

Q1. What is commissioning?

Commissioning is the systematic process of inspecting, configuring, testing, and verifying equipment and its integrated functions to establish readiness for intended operation.

Q2. What is the difference between installation and commissioning?

Installation physically places and connects the equipment. Commissioning verifies that the installed equipment is configured correctly and performs as required.

Q3. What is the difference between FAT and commissioning?

FAT is an acceptance-testing stage usually performed at the supplier's facility before delivery. Commissioning covers the wider preparation and verification needed to make the installed system ready for operation. The activities can overlap.

Q4. What is the difference between FAT and SAT?

FAT is conducted at the factory or supplier's facility. SAT is conducted at the actual installation site to verify the installed system and its site-specific interfaces against the agreed requirements.

Q5. Why is I/O checking important during commissioning?

It verifies that physical devices, wiring, I/O addresses, PLC tags, and displayed states correspond correctly. An incorrect I/O mapping can cause a program to interpret one physical signal as another.

Q6. Why must interlocks and trips be tested?

They ensure that defined operating restrictions and protective responses function as intended under both normal and abnormal conditions.

Q7. Why is system integration testing necessary?

Subsystems that work independently may fail when connected because of incompatible signal definitions, timing, configuration, communication, or sequence assumptions.

Q8. What is a punch list?

A punch list records outstanding defects, incomplete work, or items requiring correction before or after acceptance, with ownership and closure status.

Q9. What documents should be handed over after commissioning?

Typically, approved software backups, parameter records, drawings, test results, equipment manuals, maintenance information, spare-parts information, and unresolved-item records.

Q10. When is commissioning considered complete?

When the defined commissioning and acceptance criteria have been met, required evidence has been recorded, and any remaining items are handled according to the approved acceptance and handover process.

11. Final mental model

SPECIFY
What must the system do?

INSPECT
Was it installed correctly?

CONFIGURE
Are the devices and software set up correctly?

TEST
Do individual components work?

INTEGRATE
Do the components work together?

VALIDATE
Does the system meet its specified requirements?

VERIFY PERFORMANCE
Can it operate as intended under the required conditions?

DOCUMENT AND HAND OVER
Is the system ready for controlled operation and maintenance?

For an automation engineer, commissioning is where design decisions meet physical reality. It tests whether the hardware, software, process, safety functions, and interfaces form a working system rather than merely a collection of installed components.
