1. Definition of a Downtime Record

A downtime record is a structured record of the period during which equipment, a machine, a production line, or a defined process is unavailable or unable to perform its required function.

It captures when downtime started, when it ended, why it occurred, which equipment was affected, how much production was lost, and whether the downtime was planned or unplanned.

In industrial maintenance, downtime records provide the time-based evidence used to evaluate equipment availability, maintenance performance, production losses, and reliability improvement opportunities.

For example, when a PLC-controlled assembly machine stops because of a remote I/O communication failure, the downtime record captures the defined start and end times, affected equipment, downtime classification, production impact, and linked breakdown or maintenance records.

A downtime record answers a fundamental question: How much time was lost, under which conditions, and for what reason?

2. Objectives of Downtime Recording

Downtime recording is used to:

  • Measure equipment and production-line unavailability.
  • Distinguish planned downtime from unplanned stoppages.
  • Identify the main contributors to lost production time.
  • Calculate equipment availability and downtime trends.
  • Support MTTR and MTBF calculations using consistent definitions.
  • Compare downtime between machines, lines, shifts, and production areas.
  • Identify chronic equipment problems and recurring losses.
  • Evaluate maintenance response and restoration performance.
  • Support capacity planning and production scheduling.
  • Prioritize preventive maintenance and engineering improvements.
  • Reconcile PLC, SCADA, production, and maintenance records.
  • Provide evidence for operational reviews and improvement projects.

Downtime data is useful only when the measurement rules are consistent. Two machines cannot be compared fairly if one report includes planned stops and the other excludes them.

3. Downtime Record vs Breakdown Report vs Failure Report

Record Primary purpose Typical question answered
Downtime record Capture the duration and classification of lost availability How long was the equipment unavailable?
Breakdown report Document an unplanned stoppage and restoration activities What happened during the breakdown and how was service restored?
Failure report Analyze the technical failure mode, mechanism, and cause What failed and why did it fail?
Fault/alarm history Preserve diagnostic events generated by equipment What faults or alarms occurred, and when?
Production loss record Quantify lost output or production opportunity How many units or how much throughput was lost?
Maintenance work order Record authorized maintenance execution What work, labor, and materials were used?

These records should be linked wherever possible. A single failure may generate multiple alarms, one breakdown report, one downtime interval, a maintenance work order, and a detailed failure investigation.

4. What Counts as Downtime?

Downtime depends on the equipment's required function and the organization's measurement rules.

Examples include:

  • A CNC machine cannot execute its production cycle.
  • A robot is unavailable because of a servo fault.
  • A conveyor stops due to a motor or drive failure.
  • A PLC loses communication with critical remote I/O.
  • A test bench cannot perform its required test.
  • A production line stops because a critical upstream machine is unavailable.
  • A machine operates in a restricted state that reduces its required production capacity.

Not every stopped machine represents production downtime. A machine may be stopped because there is no production demand, a planned break is in progress, or upstream material is unavailable.

Similarly, a machine may continue operating while degraded performance creates a production loss without a complete stoppage.

Define the downtime scope before recording the event.

5. Types of Downtime

5.1 Planned downtime

Time scheduled in advance for maintenance, inspections, modifications, calibration, or other approved activities.

Examples:

  • Preventive maintenance shutdown.
  • Scheduled CNC servicing.
  • Planned electrical panel inspection.
  • Approved software upgrade.
  • Planned machine relocation or commissioning.

5.2 Unplanned downtime

Unexpected equipment unavailability that interrupts the intended operation.

Examples:

  • PLC hardware failure.
  • VFD trip.
  • Robot servo fault.
  • Mechanical seizure.
  • Sensor failure.
  • Unexpected utility loss.

5.3 Maintenance downtime

Time during which equipment is unavailable for maintenance activities. Depending on local definitions, this may include diagnosis, repair, waiting, testing, and restoration.

Maintenance downtime is not automatically equal to hands-on repair time.

5.4 Production downtime

Time during which production cannot achieve the required output because of the event.

Production downtime can differ from equipment downtime when redundant equipment, buffers, parallel processes, or existing production stops affect the actual production impact.

5.5 Minor stops

Short interruptions that may not be classified as full breakdowns under the site's reporting rules.

Examples include repeated sensor interruptions, brief conveyor jams, or short communication recoveries.

Minor stops should not be ignored if their cumulative effect is significant.

5.6 Performance loss without complete downtime

Equipment continues running but at a reduced rate or with degraded performance.

Examples include:

  • A conveyor operating below its target speed.
  • A CNC cycle taking longer because of repeated recovery sequences.
  • A robot slowing down due to a process restriction.
  • A test bench repeating measurements because of unstable readings.

These losses are often tracked separately from downtime to avoid mixing time-based availability with performance-based losses.

7. Defining the Downtime Start and End

A reliable downtime record requires explicit event-boundary rules.

Start time

Possible definitions include:

  • Actual time the equipment lost its required function.
  • Time the controller detected the failure.
  • Time production recognized the stoppage.
  • Time the operator formally recorded the stop.

These timestamps may differ. For technical analysis, retain the actual occurrence time where it can be established and separately record detection or notification times.

If the actual occurrence time is unknown, record the best-supported start time and mark its source or uncertainty.

End time

Possible definitions include:

  • Time the technical fault was corrected.
  • Time functional testing was completed.
  • Time equipment was released for operation.
  • Time production actually resumed.

These are different events. The selected end point must match the KPI being calculated.

For example, an equipment-restoration KPI may end when the machine is verified and released, while a production-loss KPI may end when the production process resumes.

8. Downtime Calculation

For a single uninterrupted downtime interval:

\[ \text{Downtime duration}=\text{End time}-\text{Start time} \]

Example:

  • Start: 10:14
  • End: 11:05
\[ \text{Downtime}=51\text{ minutes} \]

The result is valid only if both timestamps use the same time basis and refer to the defined start and end events.

Convert duration into hours

\[ \text{Downtime hours}=\frac{\text{Downtime minutes}}{60} \]

For 51 minutes:

\[ \frac{51}{60}=0.85\text{ hours} \]

Total downtime

For multiple non-overlapping intervals:

\[ \text{Total downtime}=\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\text{Downtime}\_i \]

If the same equipment has three intervals of 10, 20, and 15 minutes, the total is 45 minutes.

Overlapping intervals must be handled carefully. If two alarms describe the same stoppage, adding both durations would double-count the loss.

9. Gross Downtime and Net Downtime

Gross downtime is the full elapsed duration of a defined equipment stoppage.

Net production downtime is the production loss attributed to the event after applying approved rules for overlapping stops, scheduled shutdowns, redundancy, and actual production availability.

Example:

A machine remains unavailable for 60 minutes, but the production line was already scheduled to stop for 20 minutes during that interval.

Depending on the organization's KPI rules, the record may show:

  • Equipment downtime: 60 minutes.
  • Overlapping scheduled stop: 20 minutes.
  • Incremental production downtime: 40 minutes.

This calculation is appropriate only if the scheduled stop genuinely overlapped and the approved measurement definition allows the exclusion.

Never reduce downtime merely because a technician was waiting for a spare part or another department. Waiting time is still part of elapsed downtime unless the specific KPI explicitly excludes it.

10. Downtime Timeline and Time Categories

A downtime record can use multiple timestamps to separate operational losses from maintenance work.

Event Example time
Failure occurs 10:14
Fault detected 10:14
Maintenance notified 10:16
Technician arrives 10:22
Diagnosis completed 10:37
Repair completed 10:55
Functional test completed 11:02
Production resumes 11:05

From this timeline:

  • Notification delay is 2 minutes.
  • Response time from notification to technician arrival is 6 minutes.
  • Diagnosis time from technician arrival to diagnosis completion is 15 minutes.
  • Repair duration is 18 minutes.
  • Functional test duration is 7 minutes.
  • Elapsed downtime from failure to production resumption is 51 minutes.

These are illustrative calculations, and the categories must be defined consistently. If troubleshooting and repair overlap, the recorded durations should not be added together as though they were independent periods.

11. Equipment Downtime vs Line Downtime

Equipment downtime and production-line downtime are not necessarily equal.

Example 1: Single-machine line

One critical CNC machine is the only machine performing a required operation. When it fails, the production line stops.

Equipment downtime may closely correspond to line downtime.

Example 2: Parallel machines

One of two parallel machines fails, but the second machine continues production.

The failed machine experiences downtime, but the production line may experience no immediate full stoppage. Throughput may still decline.

Example 3: Buffer-supported production

An upstream machine fails, but downstream equipment continues running while buffer inventory remains available.

Line downtime may begin later than the original equipment downtime.

Example 4: Common-cause failure

A shared utility or network switch fails and affects multiple machines simultaneously.

Each machine may have an equipment-level downtime interval, but the line-level loss must be calculated without summing the same elapsed production stoppage repeatedly.

For reporting, identify whether the metric applies to an individual asset, a production line, or the whole plant.

12. Downtime Classification and Reason Codes

Standardized reason codes help identify the largest contributors to lost time.

Category Example reasons
Electrical Power supply fault, overload, wiring defect
PLC and control Controller fault, I/O failure, logic issue
Network Communication loss, switch failure, addressing issue
CNC Spindle, axis, feedback, or control fault
Robot Servo, encoder, controller, or safety-related stop
Mechanical Bearing failure, jam, coupling damage
Pneumatic Air pressure loss, cylinder or valve fault
Hydraulic Pump failure, leakage, pressure loss
Sensor and instrumentation Sensor failure, signal instability, calibration issue
Utilities Power, compressed air, cooling water, environmental supply
Production process Material unavailable, upstream starvation, downstream blockage
Quality Quality hold, failed test, process deviation
Planned maintenance Preventive maintenance, calibration, scheduled modification
Changeover and setup Tool change, product change, setup activity
External Facility issue, external supply disruption, approved external cause

Use separate fields for the downtime category and the technical cause where practical. A production stop caused by an upstream machine is a different classification question from the electrical failure that occurred in that upstream machine.

Do not force every event into an electrical or mechanical category simply because the maintenance department owns the reporting system.

13. Downtime Reason vs Root Cause

A downtime reason is the classification used to explain why the interval was recorded. It may be provisional.

A root cause is the underlying reason established through investigation.

For example:

  • Downtime reason: remote I/O communication failure.
  • Failure mode: communication with the remote I/O device was lost.
  • Immediate cause: intermittent network connection.
  • Root cause: potentially damaged connector retention or unsuitable cable routing, if confirmed by evidence.

A downtime dashboard can use a provisional category before the full investigation is completed. The record should be updated or linked to the final findings when the cause becomes known.

14. Downtime KPIs

14.1 Total downtime

\[ \text{Total downtime}=\sum\_{i=1}^{n}d_i \]

Where \(d_i\) is the duration of each valid, non-duplicated interval.

14.2 Downtime percentage

A commonly used time-based measure is:

\[ \text{Downtime (\\%)}= \frac{\text{Downtime}}{\text{Defined reference time}}\times100 \]

The reference time must be specified, such as planned production time or scheduled operating time. Including non-production periods in the denominator can distort the result.

14.3 Availability

A simplified time-based availability calculation is:

\[ \text{Availability (\\%)}= \frac{\text{Operating time}}{\text{Operating time}+\text{Downtime}}\times100 \]

This formula is appropriate only when operating time and downtime are defined consistently for the same equipment and period.

14.4 Mean downtime per event

\[ \text{Mean downtime}= \frac{\text{Total downtime}}{\text{Number of downtime events}} \]

Use a consistent rule for deciding whether an event qualifies as a breakdown, minor stop, or planned shutdown.

14.5 MTTR

A common repair-time measure is:

\[ \text{MTTR}= \frac{\text{Total relevant repair time}}{\text{Number of repairs}} \]

Some organizations use elapsed time to restore function, while others use active repair time. Always state which convention applies.

14.6 MTBF

For repairable equipment:

\[ \text{MTBF}= \frac{\text{Total operating time}}{\text{Number of relevant failures}} \]

MTBF requires a consistent definition of operating exposure and qualifying failures. Total calendar time should not automatically be treated as operating time.

15. Production Loss Estimation

Downtime may cause a loss of production, but elapsed downtime alone does not determine the number of lost units.

A simple estimate is:

\[ \text{Estimated lost units}= \text{Expected production rate}\times\text{Effective lost time} \]

For example, if the expected rate is 30 units per hour and effective lost production time is 0.5 hours:

\[ 30\times0.5=15\text{ units} \]

This estimate assumes the machine could otherwise have produced at the stated rate and that no buffer, parallel machine, or later recovery offsets the loss.

For more accurate analysis, consider:

  • Actual production rate before the stoppage.
  • Product mix and cycle-time variation.
  • Buffer levels and parallel production capacity.
  • Scrap and rework.
  • Production recovery after restart.
  • Whether the line was scheduled to operate.
  • Bottleneck constraints.

Record whether the lost-unit figure is measured, calculated, or estimated.

16. Downtime Record Example: VFD Trip

Incident

A conveyor stops after a VFD overcurrent trip during acceleration.

Downtime record

Field Example entry
Equipment Conveyor CV-04
Start time 14:10
End time 14:42
Duration 32 minutes
Category Unplanned equipment downtime
Initial reason VFD overcurrent trip
Impact Conveyor unavailable during the affected production interval
Linked record Breakdown report and maintenance work order
Data status Confirmed timestamps; root cause pending investigation

The example records the known event and duration. It does not claim that the drive itself was defective.

After investigation, the reason classification can be updated if evidence identifies mechanical binding, motor-circuit defects, incorrect settings, or another cause.

17. Downtime Record Example: Remote I/O Communication Loss

Incident

A PLC loses communication with a remote I/O adapter, causing a machine sequence to stop.

Recording requirements

  • Equipment and remote I/O identifiers.
  • Defined downtime start and end.
  • PLC and network alarm timestamps.
  • Machine operating state.
  • Production impact.
  • Whether the fault recovered automatically or required intervention.
  • Linked breakdown report.
  • Final reason classification and investigation status.

If communication is lost for a few seconds but the machine does not lose its required function, the event may be recorded as a fault or minor interruption rather than a full downtime event, depending on the reporting rules.

A burst of repeated communication alarms should not automatically generate a separate downtime interval for each alarm.

18. Downtime Record Example: CNC Machine

A CNC machine becomes unavailable after an axis-related fault.

The record should distinguish:

  • The time the axis fault was detected.
  • The time the machine lost its required function.
  • The time maintenance was notified.
  • The time repair was completed.
  • The time required functional testing was completed.
  • The time the machine was released.
  • The time production resumed.

If the CNC is restored but waits for a production operator, the equipment-restoration interval may end before the production-resumption interval. Both timestamps can be useful for different performance measures.

19. Manual Downtime Recording

Manual records are often necessary when automated event capture is unavailable or incomplete.

Recommended procedure

  1. Identify the equipment and affected function.
  2. Record the event start time as accurately as possible.
  3. Record the source of the start time, such as a controller alarm or operator observation.
  4. Select the approved downtime category.
  5. Record the observed reason without inventing a root cause.
  6. Record any production or quality impact.
  7. Update the record when the equipment is restored.
  8. Enter the end time and calculate the duration.
  9. Link the breakdown report or maintenance work order.
  10. Review the record for completeness and duplicate events.

If a timestamp is estimated, mark it as estimated. Do not create false precision by entering seconds when the actual time is only known approximately.

20. Automated Downtime Recording Using PLC and SCADA

Automated downtime recording uses equipment status, controller alarms, production states, or other signals to identify when equipment becomes unavailable.

A conceptual architecture is:

PLC / CNC / Robot / VFD
        ↓
Equipment Status and Event Signals
        ↓
SCADA / Node-RED / Event Processing
        ↓
Downtime State Logic
        ↓
SQL Event Records
        ↓
Power BI / Reliability Dashboard
        ↓
Maintenance Review and Corrective Action

Typical input signals

  • Machine running.
  • Machine ready.
  • Automatic cycle active.
  • Fault active.
  • Emergency stop active.
  • Safety circuit status.
  • Remote I/O communication status.
  • Production mode.
  • Cycle complete.
  • Operator stop request.
  • Upstream or downstream block.
  • Planned maintenance mode.

Not every signal should directly define downtime. The logic must reflect the equipment's required function and the approved production rules.

Important design considerations

  • Define which state transition starts a downtime interval.
  • Define the conditions that end the interval.
  • Filter transient signal changes where justified.
  • Avoid masking genuine short failures through excessive filtering.
  • Synchronize timestamps across data sources.
  • Handle controller restarts and communication outages.
  • Store event identifiers and equipment identifiers.
  • Reconcile automatically detected events with maintenance records.
  • Preserve the original event timestamps when records are later corrected.

21. Downtime State Logic

A simple equipment state model can help standardize recording.

RUNNING
   ↓ Equipment loses required function
DOWNTIME ACTIVE
   ↓ Fault cleared and required conditions restored
RECOVERY / VERIFICATION
   ↓ Equipment released according to defined rules
RUNNING

Real systems may require additional states such as planned stop, setup, blocked, starved, manual operation, safety stop, and degraded operation.

The state model should prevent a machine from being classified as running merely because one fault bit has cleared.

For example, a CNC alarm may clear while the machine remains in manual mode awaiting a reference return. Depending on the metric, the machine may still be unavailable for production.

For a SCADA or maintenance analytics project, a downtime event table might contain:

End_Time End timestamp
Duration_Seconds Calculated duration in seconds
Downtime_Category Planned, unplanned, minor stop, or other approved category

Description Event description

Breakdown_ID Linked breakdown record
Work_Order_ID Linked maintenance work order
Data_Status Confirmed, estimated, incomplete, or under review
Review_Status Open, reviewed, or closed

A conceptual relationship is:

Equipment Master
      ↓
Downtime Event Table
      ↓
Breakdown / Work Order Link
      ↓
Failure Classification
      ↓
Reliability and Production Reporting

Data integrity rules

  • Each event must have a unique identifier.
  • Equipment identifiers must match the asset master.
  • End time must not precede start time.
  • Open intervals must be clearly marked rather than assigned a fabricated end time.
  • Duration should be calculated consistently from timestamps.
  • Overlapping events must follow an explicit counting rule.
  • Missing telemetry must not be interpreted as zero downtime.
  • Corrections should preserve an audit trail where required.

A common error is storing both start/end timestamps and a manually entered duration without checking whether the three values agree.

23. Downtime Analysis in Power BI

Power BI can convert event records into useful operational views.

Useful dashboard measures

  • Total downtime by machine.
  • Total downtime by production line.
  • Planned vs unplanned downtime.
  • Downtime by shift.
  • Downtime by category and reason.
  • Top equipment by total downtime.
  • Number of breakdown-related downtime events.
  • Average downtime per event.
  • Downtime trend by day, week, or month.
  • Restoration time trend.
  • Downtime associated with recurring failures.
  • Production units lost, where a valid estimate or measurement exists.

Example analysis questions

  • Which machine contributes the largest share of unplanned downtime?
  • Is downtime increasing after a machine modification?
  • Which shift experiences the most equipment stoppages?
  • Are network-related failures increasing?
  • Does a high number of minor stops produce a significant cumulative loss?
  • Which failures cause the longest restoration periods?
  • Are repeated breakdowns occurring after the same corrective action?

Avoid misleading dashboards

  • Do not combine planned and unplanned downtime without showing the distinction.
  • Do not compare machines with different scheduled operating hours without suitable normalization.
  • Do not sum overlapping line-level and equipment-level intervals as if they were independent losses.
  • Do not treat missing data as zero.
  • Do not rank equipment by total downtime alone when operating exposure differs substantially.

24. Downtime Data Quality and Validation

Reliable reporting requires automated and manual validation.

Timestamp validation

  • Start time must be valid.
  • End time must be valid when the event is closed.
  • End time must not precede start time.
  • Time zones and daylight-saving rules must be handled where applicable.
  • PLC, SCADA, SQL, and manual timestamps should use a consistent reference.
  • Late-entered records should retain the original event time and entry time separately where required.

Event validation

  • Confirm the equipment was expected to operate.
  • Check whether the event overlaps with an existing event.
  • Verify whether multiple alarms belong to one stoppage.
  • Identify equipment that is unavailable without a corresponding event.
  • Compare automated status changes with breakdown reports.
  • Mark estimated, incomplete, or uncertain data explicitly.

Duration validation

For a closed interval:

\[ \text{Duration seconds}= \text{End timestamp}-\text{Start timestamp} \]

Calculate the duration using the timestamps, then apply the organization's rounding convention for display and KPI reporting.

25. Downtime, MTTR, MTBF, and Availability

These measures are related but are not interchangeable.

Metric What it measures Main data requirement
Downtime Duration of defined unavailability intervals Start and end timestamps
MTTR Average relevant repair duration Repair records and a defined repair-time convention
MTBF Average operating time between relevant failures Failure count and operating exposure
Availability Proportion of defined time the equipment is available or operating Consistent operating-time and downtime definitions
Production loss Lost units or output attributable to the event Production rate, effective lost time, and impact rules

A machine can have many short downtime events but a lower total downtime than a machine with one long event. Therefore, both event frequency and duration should be reviewed.

26. Common Downtime Recording Mistakes

  • Using notification time as the failure start time without identifying the difference.
  • Using repair completion time as production-resumption time.
  • Counting one failure multiple times because it generated several alarms.
  • Double-counting overlapping downtime intervals.
  • Mixing planned maintenance and unplanned failures.
  • Recording estimated times as confirmed.
  • Ignoring short stops because each event appears insignificant.
  • Treating a cleared alarm as proof that production has resumed.
  • Treating machine downtime as identical to production-line downtime.
  • Calculating availability using inconsistent scheduled hours.
  • Assigning root causes before the investigation is complete.
  • Failing to link downtime records to breakdown reports.
  • Recording zero downtime when telemetry is missing.
  • Comparing machines without considering operating exposure or production demand.
  • Changing historical timestamps without preserving traceability.

27. Practical Downtime Recording Checklist

  • The affected equipment and required function are identified.
  • The downtime start and end rules are clearly defined.
  • The event start time is recorded from the best available evidence.
  • The event end time matches the selected KPI definition.
  • The duration is calculated consistently.
  • Planned and unplanned downtime are distinguished.
  • The reason code follows the approved classification system.
  • The production impact is recorded separately where needed.
  • Duplicate and overlapping events are handled correctly.
  • The related breakdown report and work order are linked.
  • Uncertain or estimated data is marked appropriately.
  • Automated events are reconciled with actual equipment behavior.
  • KPI calculations use consistent definitions and reference periods.
  • The record is reviewed for completeness and data quality.
