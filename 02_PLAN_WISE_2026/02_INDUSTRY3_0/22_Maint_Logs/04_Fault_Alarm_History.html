1. Definition of Fault/Alarm History

Fault/alarm history is a chronological record of faults, alarms, warnings, trips, abnormal operating conditions, and related equipment state changes generated by industrial machines and control systems.

It provides evidence about what the equipment detected, when the event occurred, how the system responded, and what happened before and after the event.

In industrial maintenance, fault/alarm history is a primary diagnostic resource for troubleshooting PLCs, CNC machines, robots, VFDs, servo drives, HMIs, SCADA systems, and industrial networks.

For example, a robot may stop with a servo alarm. The alarm history may show that a DC bus undervoltage warning occurred first, followed by a servo fault and then a robot program interruption. This sequence provides more diagnostic value than looking only at the final alarm displayed on the HMI.

An alarm is evidence of a detected condition, not automatic proof of its root cause. The actual cause must be established through investigation.

2. Objectives of Fault/Alarm History

Fault and alarm records help maintenance engineers to:

  • Identify the sequence of events leading to a machine stoppage.
  • Find the first relevant abnormal condition rather than focusing only on the final alarm.
  • Diagnose intermittent faults.
  • Distinguish primary faults from consequential alarms.
  • Identify recurring equipment and component problems.
  • Correlate PLC, CNC, robot, drive, HMI, SCADA, and network events.
  • Reduce unnecessary component replacement.
  • Verify whether a repair resolved the original problem.
  • Support breakdown reports, failure reports, RCA, and CAPA.
  • Monitor alarm frequency, duration, recurrence, and operational impact.
  • Detect developing faults before they cause a major stoppage.
  • Preserve technical evidence for future troubleshooting and reliability analysis.

3. Difference Between an Alarm, Fault, Warning, and Trip

These terms are related, but their exact meanings depend on the manufacturer and control-system configuration.

Term Meaning Example
Alarm Notification that a defined condition requires attention or response Low lubrication pressure
Warning Indication of an abnormal or approaching limit that may not yet prevent operation Drive temperature approaching a limit
Fault A detected condition that indicates an equipment function cannot operate correctly or as required Servo feedback fault
Trip Protective action that stops or disables a function in response to a detected condition VFD overcurrent trip
Event A recorded occurrence or state transition Machine changed from automatic to manual
Interlock A condition that permits or prevents an operation Machine cycle inhibited because a guard condition is not satisfied
Diagnostic message Information describing internal equipment status or a detected issue Remote I/O connection interrupted
Acknowledgement Operator or system confirmation that an alarm has been recognized Operator acknowledges a pressure alarm
Reset Action that clears or reinitializes a fault state when the reset conditions are satisfied Drive fault reset after the underlying condition is corrected

An alarm can exist without a machine stoppage. A fault may generate several alarms, and a protective trip may be the final result of an earlier abnormal condition.

Resetting an alarm does not necessarily correct the underlying fault.

4. Alarm Lifecycle

A typical alarm lifecycle includes detection, activation, acknowledgement, clearance, and possible reset.

Normal operation
        ↓
Abnormal condition detected
        ↓
Alarm or fault becomes active
        ↓
Operator or control system responds
        ↓
Condition remains active or clears
        ↓
Alarm acknowledged, if required
        ↓
Underlying condition corrected
        ↓
Reset or recovery, if required
        ↓
Functional verification
        ↓
Normal operation restored

The sequence is not identical for every alarm. Some alarms clear automatically when the condition returns to normal. Others remain latched until acknowledgement, reset, or an approved recovery procedure.

An alarm may also reappear if the underlying condition remains unresolved.

5. Essential Fields in Fault/Alarm History

A useful alarm record should contain enough information to identify the event and reconstruct its context.

Field Purpose
Event ID Unique identifier for the event
Timestamp Date and time of the event
Equipment ID Machine or equipment identifier
Source device PLC, CNC, robot controller, VFD, HMI, SCADA, or network device
Alarm code Manufacturer-defined or application-defined code
Alarm description Text describing the detected condition
Severity Informational, warning, fault, critical, or approved classification
Event type Activation, clearance, acknowledgement, reset, or state transition
Active status Whether the condition is currently active
Operating state Running, stopped, setup, manual, automatic, or other relevant state
Process context Sequence step, axis, station, recipe, or process stage
Relevant values Available current, voltage, temperature, pressure, or status data
Affected function Machine or process function impacted
Response taken Inspection, repair, reset, isolation, or other action
Related event ID Link to associated alarms or events
Maintenance reference Breakdown, work order, failure report, or CAPA reference

Not every controller provides every field. Record the available information and distinguish unavailable data from values that were never collected.

6. Alarm Severity and Prioritization

Alarm severity should reflect operational consequences and the response required, not merely how frequently an alarm appears.

Priority General interpretation Example
Critical Immediate action may be necessary to protect people, equipment, or a critical process Serious safety-related fault or critical process protection event
High Major equipment function or production is unavailable CNC axis fault preventing production
Medium Degraded function or abnormal condition requires timely investigation Repeated communication interruptions
Low Limited operational impact or maintenance attention required Noncritical filter maintenance warning
Informational State change or diagnostic information Automatic cycle completed

These are illustrative categories. Use the site's approved alarm-priority definitions and response procedures.

A safety-related alarm must be treated according to the applicable safety procedure regardless of whether the HMI displays it as high, medium, or low priority.

7. Alarm Codes and Descriptions

An alarm code identifies a defined condition within a particular equipment or application.

Examples include:

  • PLC diagnostic fault codes.
  • CNC servo or spindle alarm codes.
  • Robot controller fault codes.
  • VFD overcurrent or undervoltage codes.
  • Servo encoder or position-error codes.
  • Remote I/O communication errors.
  • HMI communication or application errors.
  • Industrial switch port or network diagnostics.

Always record the exact code and the equipment that generated it.

The same numeric code may have different meanings on different manufacturers, controller families, firmware versions, or software applications. A code without its source device and context may be misleading.

For technical interpretation, use the relevant manufacturer's documentation and the applicable firmware or controller version.

8. First-Out Fault and Consequential Alarms

A machine stoppage may produce a cascade of alarms.

For example, when a remote I/O adapter loses communication, the PLC may no longer receive input data. This can cause sequence faults, missing feedback alarms, actuator timeout alarms, and a machine-stop indication.

The first alarm in the displayed list is not necessarily the original cause. Event ordering, device response delays, and timestamp accuracy must be considered.

Example

Time Event Possible interpretation
14:10:00.100 Remote I/O communication interrupted Potential initiating event
14:10:00.150 Feedback signal unavailable Possible consequence
14:10:00.300 Sequence timeout Possible consequence
14:10:00.500 Machine cycle stopped Resulting operational effect

This example illustrates event correlation, not a confirmed diagnosis.

A first-out indication is a feature that identifies the first detected fault according to a particular controller or protection system's logic. It can be valuable, but it is not infallible evidence of the physical root cause.

9. Alarm History vs Live Alarm Screen

A live alarm screen and an alarm history serve different purposes.

Feature Live alarm screen Alarm history
Main purpose Show current or uncleared alarms Preserve previous events
Typical use Immediate operator response Troubleshooting and trend analysis
Cleared alarms May disappear from the active list May remain recorded
Sequence reconstruction Limited unless additional event details are available Better when timestamps and transitions are preserved
Intermittent faults May miss the event after it clears Can preserve the evidence
Maintenance analysis Useful for current symptoms Useful for historical investigation

Never assume that a cleared alarm has disappeared from every history buffer. Conversely, never assume that every controller retains an unlimited history.

10. Fault/Alarm History in PLC Systems

PLC diagnostics can include controller faults, I/O module errors, task or program exceptions, communication faults, and application-generated alarms.

Information to capture

  • PLC manufacturer and controller model.
  • Controller name and firmware version.
  • Diagnostic buffer entries.
  • Module identity and slot or network location.
  • Relevant I/O addresses and tag names.
  • Controller operating mode.
  • Task, program, or routine context where relevant.
  • Communication status.
  • Timestamp and event order.
  • Recent program, configuration, or hardware changes.

Practical example

A PLC reports a remote input module fault.

The investigation should determine whether the problem involves device power, network communication, module health, configuration, wiring, or the controller's ability to access the device.

Replacing the module without examining the associated diagnostics may leave the real problem unresolved.

For safety PLCs, follow the approved safety diagnostic and authorization procedures. Do not bypass safety functions to clear an alarm.

11. Fault/Alarm History in VFDs

VFDs commonly record protective trips, warnings, and operating events.

Examples include:

  • Overcurrent.
  • DC bus overvoltage.
  • DC bus undervoltage.
  • Motor overload.
  • Drive overtemperature.
  • Motor or drive thermal warning.
  • Ground fault.
  • Input phase loss.
  • Output phase or motor-circuit fault.
  • Communication loss.
  • External interlock or enable loss.
  • Speed feedback fault, where applicable.

What to record

  • Exact fault code and description.
  • Drive model and firmware.
  • Timestamp.
  • Operating speed and frequency.
  • Current and voltage values, if available.
  • Acceleration or deceleration condition.
  • Load and process state.
  • Relevant parameter values.
  • Previous and subsequent alarms.
  • Reset history.
  • Diagnostic findings and corrective action.

An overcurrent trip can result from excessive load, mechanical binding, a motor-circuit problem, incorrect configuration, or a drive fault. The code alone does not identify which explanation is correct.

12. Fault/Alarm History in Servo Systems

Servo systems may record faults involving the drive, motor, feedback device, power circuit, communication, or motion control.

Examples include:

  • Encoder communication error.
  • Position deviation beyond a limit.
  • Excessive motor current.
  • Drive overtemperature.
  • DC bus abnormality.
  • Motor overload.
  • Brake or release-sequence fault.
  • Motion command or synchronization error.
  • Feedback cable interruption.
  • Servo enable or interlock condition not satisfied.

Investigation approach

  1. Record the exact alarm code and affected axis.
  2. Capture the operating mode and motion condition.
  3. Review preceding and following events.
  4. Inspect relevant drive and controller diagnostics.
  5. Evaluate feedback, wiring, supply, mechanical load, and configuration as appropriate.
  6. Confirm the actual fault through safe, approved tests.
  7. Record the corrective action and required motion verification.

Do not change tuning parameters or repeatedly reset a servo drive merely to see whether the alarm disappears. That is experimentation without a controlled hypothesis, and it can create additional faults or hazards.

13. Fault/Alarm History in CNC Machines

CNC alarm histories may contain several categories:

  • CNC system alarms.
  • Servo axis alarms.
  • Spindle drive alarms.
  • Encoder and feedback alarms.
  • Tool changer alarms.
  • Lubrication and hydraulic alarms.
  • Pneumatic pressure alarms.
  • Machine-builder PLC alarms.
  • Safety interlock alarms.
  • Communication errors.
  • Program and operating-condition errors.

Important distinction

A CNC control alarm and a machine-builder PLC alarm may describe different parts of the same event.

For example, a machine-builder PLC may report that an axis is not ready, while the CNC diagnostic history contains the underlying servo alarm.

A proper investigation correlates the two histories rather than treating the messages as unrelated faults.

Record the control model, alarm number, relevant axis or subsystem, operating conditions, and applicable diagnostic evidence.

14. Fault/Alarm History in Industrial Robots

Robot controllers may record alarms involving:

  • Servo drives and motors.
  • Encoder feedback.
  • Motion and position deviation.
  • Controller power or temperature.
  • Safety circuits.
  • Enabling devices.
  • Fence and gate conditions.
  • Tool and peripheral communication.
  • Fieldbus and remote I/O.
  • Program execution and motion commands.

A robot may display a motion or program error after an earlier safety, communication, or servo fault.

Review the chronological history to determine whether the program error is the initiating problem or a consequence of the earlier event.

For safety-related events, use the approved robot and cell safety procedures. Do not bypass gates, interlocks, enabling devices, or safety-rated functions to reproduce or clear a fault.

15. Fault/Alarm History in HMI and SCADA

HMI and SCADA systems commonly record process alarms, operator acknowledgements, communication failures, tag-quality changes, and application events.

Examples include:

  • High or low process limits.
  • Loss of PLC communication.
  • Bad or stale tag quality.
  • Alarm acknowledgement.
  • Operator mode changes.
  • Data acquisition interruption.
  • Historian write failure.
  • Application exceptions.
  • Server redundancy or failover events.

Important distinction: process fault vs display fault

Suppose an HMI displays a pressure value that remains unchanged.

Possible explanations include:

  • Actual pressure has remained stable.
  • The sensor signal has stopped updating.
  • PLC communication has been lost.
  • The SCADA tag has stale quality.
  • The historian or display application is malfunctioning.

The displayed number alone cannot distinguish these conditions. Check tag quality, source-device status, timestamps, and independent process evidence.

16. Fault/Alarm History in Industrial Networks

Industrial network diagnostics may be essential when PLCs, drives, remote I/O, robots, and SCADA exchange data over Ethernet or fieldbus systems.

Potential events include:

  • Link down or link up.
  • Device disconnected.
  • Communication timeout.
  • Connection reset.
  • Duplicate IP address.
  • Device configuration mismatch.
  • Packet loss or excessive errors.
  • Port errors.
  • Network topology change.
  • Excessive connection loading.
  • Device restart.
  • Time synchronization failure.

Investigation requirements

Record the affected device, network path, timestamp, connection state, switch-port diagnostics where available, and other alarms occurring in the same period.

A device-disconnected message does not automatically mean the device itself is defective. The cause may be its power supply, cable, switch port, network configuration, or another element of the communication path.

17. Alarm Floods and Alarm Cascades

An alarm flood occurs when many alarms appear in a short period, making it difficult for operators to identify the most important conditions.

An alarm cascade occurs when one initiating event causes multiple related alarms.

Examples:

  • Loss of a shared 24 V DC supply causes many sensor and I/O alarms.
  • A network switch failure causes multiple remote devices to report communication faults.
  • A cooling-water supply failure triggers several temperature alarms.
  • A machine stop generates secondary sequence timeout alarms.

How to investigate

  • Identify the earliest credible abnormal event.
  • Group alarms by equipment, subsystem, and time.
  • Determine whether they share a common dependency.
  • Distinguish initiating events from consequential events.
  • Review alarm priority and suppression logic.
  • Preserve the original event records.
  • Avoid counting every message as a separate breakdown.

Alarm suppression, shelving, and deadband configuration must follow the site's alarm-management procedures. Suppression should not conceal critical hazards or prevent required operator action.

18. Timestamp Accuracy and Event Ordering

Fault histories are useful only if their event times can be trusted.

Potential timestamp problems include:

  • PLC and SCADA clocks not synchronized.
  • CNC controller clocks using a different time basis.
  • Time zones configured inconsistently.
  • Manual clock adjustments.
  • Communication delays.
  • Buffered events uploaded after communication is restored.
  • Low timestamp resolution.
  • Events recorded only when polled rather than at the actual transition.

For example, two controllers may record related faults with timestamps that differ by several seconds because their clocks are not synchronized.

Good practice

  • Use a defined time source where supported.
  • Record the timestamp source.
  • Synchronize devices according to the site's engineering standards.
  • Preserve original timestamps when importing records.
  • Distinguish event time from server receipt time.
  • Account for clock offsets during cross-system analysis.
  • Avoid asserting a precise event sequence when timestamp uncertainty is larger than the observed time difference.

19. Intermittent Fault Investigation

Intermittent faults may disappear before a technician reaches the machine. Alarm history is therefore especially valuable.

Record:

  • Frequency and time of occurrence.
  • Whether the alarm repeats under the same operating conditions.
  • Relevant sequence step, axis position, load, or speed.
  • Preceding warnings and state changes.
  • Duration of the fault.
  • Whether the alarm cleared automatically.
  • Whether a reset or power cycle was required.
  • Relevant environmental and mechanical conditions.
  • Whether the fault correlates with movement, vibration, temperature, or electrical loading.

A fault that occurs only when a robot axis moves may suggest a different diagnostic direction from a fault that occurs immediately at power-up.

If the failure cannot be reproduced, document the evidence and limitations. A successful short test does not prove that an intermittent problem has been eliminated.

20. Alarm Frequency, Duration, and Recurrence Analysis

Alarm history can support several useful measures.

20.1 Alarm count

Count activations of a defined alarm within a period, using the chosen counting rules.

A single continuously active alarm should not be counted as dozens of independent failures merely because it appears in multiple data extracts.

20.2 Alarm duration

\[ \text{Alarm duration}=\text{Clear time}-\text{Activation time} \]

This measures how long the alarm remained active. It does not necessarily equal machine downtime.

20.3 Recurrence rate

A simple recurrence measure is:

\[ \text{Recurrence rate}= \frac{\text{Repeated qualifying events}}{\text{Defined operating exposure}} \]

The denominator may be operating hours, production cycles, or another suitable exposure measure.

20.4 Alarm-to-breakdown relationship

An alarm may:

  • Cause a breakdown.
  • Warn of an impending breakdown.
  • Appear as a consequence of a breakdown.
  • Occur without any production impact.
  • Be generated by a diagnostic or communication problem.

Linking alarm events to breakdown records helps distinguish these cases.

21. Alarm Management and Nuisance Alarms

A nuisance alarm is an alarm that provides little useful operational information because it is excessively frequent, poorly configured, duplicated, or not actionable under the intended operating conditions.

Examples include:

  • Repeated alarms caused by a noisy input signal.
  • A threshold alarm that activates under normal startup conditions.
  • Duplicate alarms for the same underlying event.
  • Alarms that immediately clear and reactivate without useful context.
  • An alarm that does not tell the operator what response is required.

A high alarm count does not automatically indicate poor equipment reliability. It may indicate poor alarm configuration, excessive signal noise, repeated status transitions, or a genuine recurring failure.

Improvement may involve correcting the physical cause, adjusting approved alarm thresholds, adding appropriate deadbands, improving descriptions, or revising alarm priorities.

Never modify alarm logic solely to make a dashboard look better. Changes must preserve required protection and operator response.

22. Using Fault History During Troubleshooting

A practical investigation can follow this sequence:

Equipment stops or behaves abnormally
        ↓
Record the symptom and equipment identity
        ↓
Open the relevant fault/alarm history
        ↓
Identify the earliest credible related event
        ↓
Correlate events across PLC, drive, CNC, robot, and SCADA
        ↓
Check the actual equipment and operating conditions
        ↓
Test possible causes using approved procedures
        ↓
Correct the confirmed defect
        ↓
Verify the required function
        ↓
Record the findings and link the maintenance records

The objective is to use event history to narrow the investigation, then confirm the diagnosis with suitable technical evidence.

23. Fault/Alarm History in SQL and Power BI

For a connected industrial system, alarm history can be collected from PLCs, SCADA, CNC systems, robots, drives, and network devices and stored in a central event database.

A conceptual architecture is:

PLC / CNC / Robot / VFD / Network Devices
        ↓
Diagnostic and Alarm Events
        ↓
SCADA / Node-RED / Event Collection
        ↓
SQL Alarm History Database
        ↓
Validation and Event Correlation
        ↓
Power BI Alarm and Reliability Dashboard
        ↓
Maintenance Investigation and Improvement

Suggested SQL fields

Field Purpose
Event_ID Unique event record
Equipment_ID Asset identifier
Source_Device Controller or device that generated the event
Alarm_Code Exact alarm or diagnostic code
Alarm_Text Description recorded by the source
Event_Type Activation, clearance, acknowledgement, or reset
Event_Time Source event timestamp
Receipt_Time Time the collection system received the event
Severity Approved alarm priority
Active_Status Current or final status, where available
Tag_or_Address Relevant PLC tag, I/O address, or device reference
Operating_State Equipment state when the event occurred
Related_Event_ID Linked event or correlation reference
Breakdown_ID Linked breakdown report
Data_Quality Valid, estimated, incomplete, or another approved status

Important implementation rules

  • Preserve the original alarm code and text.
  • Store activation and clearance events distinctly when available.
  • Do not assume every alarm is a separate failure.
  • Do not discard events simply because they clear quickly.
  • Retain source timestamps and receipt timestamps separately.
  • Handle duplicates without losing the original evidence.
  • Treat missing telemetry as missing data, not as proof that no alarms occurred.
  • Maintain traceability when alarm classifications or event groupings are changed.

24. Alarm Dashboard Design

A useful maintenance dashboard should help engineers find important events rather than merely display the largest possible alarm count.

Recommended views include:

  • Most frequent alarms by equipment.
  • Alarm frequency over time.
  • Longest active alarm durations.
  • Alarms preceding major breakdowns.
  • Repeated alarms after a repair.
  • Alarm distribution by subsystem.
  • Alarm events associated with production downtime.
  • Communication faults by device or network area.
  • Alarm floods grouped by time and common equipment dependencies.
  • Unresolved high-priority alarms.
  • Alarm-to-breakdown correlation.

Interpretation cautions

A frequently occurring alarm may be harmless or poorly configured. A rare alarm may indicate a severe failure.

Rank alarms using operational consequences, criticality, recurrence, duration, and evidence rather than frequency alone.

25. Practical Example: Diagnosing a Machine Stop

A machine stops, and the HMI displays a sequence timeout.

The alarm history shows:

  1. A remote input device briefly lost communication.
  2. A feedback input became unavailable.
  3. The sequence timed out while waiting for confirmation.
  4. The machine stopped.

A superficial diagnosis might conclude that the sequence timer is too short. A more disciplined investigation checks the remote device, network connection, device power, and input update behavior first.

If the communication failure is confirmed as the initiating event, increasing the sequence timer would merely delay the alarm and could conceal the real problem.

The failure report should record the confirmed cause, the repair, and the evidence that demonstrates the original problem has been addressed.

26. Common Fault/Alarm History Mistakes

  • Reading only the final alarm on the HMI.
  • Treating every alarm as a separate equipment failure.
  • Assuming the first displayed alarm is always the root cause.
  • Resetting the controller before preserving relevant diagnostics.
  • Ignoring alarms that cleared automatically.
  • Replacing components based only on alarm text.
  • Failing to record the controller model or firmware version.
  • Comparing timestamps from unsynchronized systems without accounting for clock offsets.
  • Confusing alarm duration with downtime duration.
  • Treating missing event data as evidence that no fault occurred.
  • Ignoring recurring alarms after repairs.
  • Suppressing alarms without evaluating operational and safety consequences.
  • Changing thresholds or timers without an engineering justification.
  • Closing an investigation without verifying the repaired function.

27. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is fault/alarm history?

It is the chronological record of equipment faults, alarms, warnings, state transitions, and related diagnostic events used for troubleshooting and reliability analysis.

Q2. What is the difference between an alarm and a fault?

An alarm notifies the operator of a defined condition. A fault indicates an abnormal condition affecting equipment function or operation. Exact definitions depend on the equipment and application.

Q3. Does the first alarm always indicate the root cause?

No. It may be the first detected event, but timestamp accuracy, detection delays, and system dependencies must be considered.

Q4. Why should alarms be recorded before resetting equipment?

Resetting or power-cycling equipment can remove valuable diagnostic context or alter the fault state. Preserve relevant evidence when safe and practical.

Q5. What is a first-out fault?

It is the first fault identified by a defined diagnostic or protection mechanism. It helps narrow the investigation but does not automatically prove the physical root cause.

Q6. Why can one failure generate multiple alarms?

A primary failure can affect dependent devices and process sequences, producing consequential alarms such as feedback loss, timeouts, and machine-stop notifications.

Q7. How does alarm history help with intermittent faults?

It preserves event timing, recurrence, operating context, and diagnostic information even when the fault clears before the technician arrives.

Q8. What is an alarm flood?

A rapid accumulation of alarms that can overwhelm operators and make the important initiating condition difficult to identify.

Q9. Is alarm duration the same as downtime?

No. An alarm may remain active without stopping production, and production may remain unavailable after an alarm clears.

Q10. How can alarm history support predictive maintenance?

Repeated warnings, increasing event frequency, abnormal operating trends, and recurring diagnostic patterns may provide early indications of deterioration. Such indications require validation and do not guarantee that a failure will occur.

28. Practical Fault/Alarm History Checklist

  • Correct equipment and source device identified.
  • Exact alarm codes and descriptions recorded.
  • Relevant event timestamps preserved.
  • Activation, clearance, acknowledgement, and reset distinguished where available.
  • Operating state and process context recorded.
  • Earliest credible related event identified.
  • PLC, drive, CNC, robot, HMI, and network events correlated as needed.
  • Diagnostic evidence preserved before resets where safe.
  • Alarm severity and operational consequences assessed.
  • Alarm duration distinguished from actual downtime.
  • Repeated events checked against previous breakdowns.
  • Root cause confirmed through investigation rather than inferred from alarm text.
  • Corrective work and functional verification documented.
  • Relevant breakdown, failure, work-order, and CAPA records linked.
  • Any alarm configuration changes follow approved engineering and safety procedures.
