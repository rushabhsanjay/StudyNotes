1. Purpose and Scope

Emergency recovery is the controlled process of bringing industrial equipment, production systems, or automation infrastructure from an abnormal or hazardous condition to a safe, stable, and verified state.

It covers situations in which normal operating procedures are insufficient, equipment behavior is unpredictable, production is interrupted, critical utilities fail, or personnel and machinery may be exposed to danger.

For industrial maintenance, emergency recovery has two distinct objectives:

  1. Immediate safety and stabilization: Protect personnel, control hazards, prevent escalation, and establish a safe machine state.
  2. Technical recovery: Identify the failure, restore the required functions, verify safety and quality, and authorize return to service.

Production recovery must never take priority over personnel safety or required machine protection.

Emergency recovery may involve:

  • PLC, HMI, CNC, robot, servo, and VFD failures.
  • Machine crashes, unexpected motion, and mechanical jams.
  • Electrical supply failure and control-power loss.
  • Industrial network outages and communication failures.
  • Safety circuit trips and protective device activation.
  • Pneumatic, hydraulic, cooling, lubrication, and other utility failures.
  • Smoke, overheating, electrical arcing, or fire.
  • Data corruption, failed downloads, and loss of critical configuration.
  • Cybersecurity incidents affecting operational technology.
  • Production line stoppages caused by interdependent equipment failures.

2. Emergency, Fault, Breakdown, and Recovery

These terms describe different conditions.

Term Meaning Example
Fault An abnormal condition detected by a device or system Drive overcurrent alarm
Breakdown Equipment can no longer perform its required function Conveyor motor fails to run
Emergency A condition requiring immediate action to control a threat Robot moves unexpectedly toward a person
Emergency stop A protective function intended to stop hazardous motion or operation E-stop activated after a machine hazard
Recovery Actions that restore a safe and functional state Diagnosing a failed drive and validating its replacement
Restart Resuming machine operation after stopping Restarting a conveyor after fault clearance
Return to service Formal release after required checks and authorization Production resumes after maintenance validation

An alarm does not automatically constitute an emergency. Conversely, an emergency can exist without an alarm. A machine with an unexpected movement or an electrical burning smell requires appropriate action even if its controller reports no fault.

3. Emergency Recovery Priorities

The priority order is:

  1. Protect human life and prevent injury.
  2. Activate the required emergency response and protective functions.
  3. Control hazardous energy and prevent unexpected movement.
  4. Prevent secondary equipment damage and escalation.
  5. Preserve evidence and establish the machine's condition.
  6. Diagnose and recover the failed system.
  7. Validate operation and authorize return to service.
  8. Record the incident and implement corrective actions.

Never repeatedly reset a fault, bypass an interlock, or force a machine to run simply because production is waiting. That approach converts a recoverable breakdown into a potentially much worse incident.

4. Emergency Classification

Classification helps determine the response level, required personnel, and escalation path.

Level Typical condition Response
Level 1: Local equipment fault A contained device fault with no immediate hazard Authorized maintenance troubleshooting
Level 2: Machine breakdown A major machine or station cannot operate Maintenance and controls support
Level 3: Line or utility disruption Multiple machines or essential services are affected Coordinated response involving production and maintenance
Level 4: Safety or environmental emergency Injury risk, fire, serious electrical hazard, hazardous release Site emergency procedures and designated emergency responders
Level 5: Site-wide or OT infrastructure incident Major power loss, widespread control failure, or suspected cyber incident Incident command and relevant technical specialists

These levels are illustrative. The site's formal emergency plan, risk assessment, and escalation matrix take precedence.

5. Immediate Response Procedure

The exact response depends on the hazard. A general framework is:

Recognize the abnormal condition
              ↓
Protect personnel and raise the alarm
              ↓
Use the appropriate emergency response
              ↓
Establish and verify a safe state
              ↓
Control hazardous energy where required
              ↓
Notify responsible personnel
              ↓
Assess damage and preserve evidence
              ↓
Diagnose and execute the approved recovery
              ↓
Verify safety and machine functionality
              ↓
Obtain authorization to return to service
              ↓
Record the incident and corrective actions

5.1 Initial actions

  • Stop work if an immediate hazard exists.
  • Warn nearby personnel and keep people away from danger.
  • Activate the designated emergency response when required.
  • Use the machine's emergency-stop function when appropriate to the situation.
  • Do not enter hazardous zones to inspect equipment while motion or stored energy remains possible.
  • Do not reset protective devices without identifying the reason for activation.
  • Notify the control room, supervisor, maintenance lead, or emergency response team according to site procedure.

5.2 Establishing a safe state

Depending on the machine, this may require:

  • Electrical isolation and lockout/tagout.
  • Verification of absence of electrical energy by an authorized person using the approved procedure.
  • Depressurization of pneumatic and hydraulic systems.
  • Blocking or securing gravity-loaded mechanisms.
  • Discharging stored electrical energy according to manufacturer instructions.
  • Preventing robot, servo, spindle, conveyor, or actuator movement.
  • Confirming that restart commands cannot initiate hazardous motion.

An emergency stop is not automatically an energy-isolation device. It may stop motion without isolating the electrical supply or eliminating stored energy.

6. Emergency Stop and Safety-System Recovery

Safety devices include emergency-stop buttons, guard switches, light curtains, safety scanners, interlocks, safety relays, and safety PLCs.

6.1 When a safety function activates

  1. Determine whether an actual hazard or unsafe condition remains.
  2. Keep personnel outside the hazardous area.
  3. Identify the activated protective device or safety diagnostic.
  4. Inspect the cause using the approved procedure.
  5. Correct the underlying fault.
  6. Verify the safety circuit and protective device.
  7. Reset the safety function only when permitted and safe.
  8. Perform the prescribed functional tests before releasing the equipment.

6.2 Reset is not restart

A safety reset acknowledges that the conditions for resetting have been satisfied. It must not be treated as proof that the machine is safe to restart.

Where required by the risk assessment, the operator must perform a separate deliberate start action from an appropriate location after confirming that the hazardous area is clear.

Never jumper a safety input, defeat a guard switch, disable a safety relay, or bypass a light curtain to restore production.

7. Emergency Recovery Roles

Role Responsibility
Operator Recognizes abnormal conditions, follows the machine SOP, reports symptoms
Shift supervisor Coordinates the response and controls production-side communication
Maintenance technician Performs authorized inspection and equipment recovery
Controls engineer Diagnoses PLC, HMI, motion, CNC, and control-system faults
Electrical specialist Investigates electrical faults and verifies electrical safety
Safety specialist Supports safety assessment and validation where required
OT/network specialist Investigates industrial network and infrastructure incidents
Emergency response team Handles site emergencies within its authorized scope
Production or quality representative Verifies process readiness and product acceptance
Authorized release owner Approves return to service under site procedures

One person may hold multiple roles at a particular site, but required competencies and authorization must still be met.

8. PLC Emergency Recovery

PLC-related emergencies can involve controller failure, corrupted logic, lost communication, power-supply faults, I/O failures, or an unexpected machine sequence.

8.1 Initial assessment

  • Record the controller's status indicators and diagnostic messages.
  • Determine whether the CPU is in RUN, STOP, faulted, or another platform-specific state.
  • Check controller and I/O power status.
  • Review the controller's diagnostic buffer or fault history.
  • Identify whether the fault affects one machine or multiple stations.
  • Verify network and remote I/O status.
  • Preserve the current project and available diagnostic evidence before making changes.

8.2 Recovery precautions

  • Do not download a PLC project without confirming the correct machine and approved version.
  • Do not clear faults before recording important diagnostic information.
  • Do not force I/O or variables without explicit authorization and risk controls.
  • Do not assume that placing a controller in RUN guarantees safe operation.
  • Verify the actual machine sequence and relevant interlocks after recovery.
  • Confirm that outputs, actuators, and communication-dependent functions behave as expected.

8.3 Example

A machine stops because the PLC loses communication with a remote I/O rack.

A sound response is to establish a safe state, record the controller and network diagnostics, verify the rack's power and communication path, and identify the cause. After correcting the issue, verify I/O status and machine interlocks before running the machine through the approved restart procedure.

Replacing the PLC or downloading the entire program would be premature if the actual problem is a failed network cable or remote I/O power supply.

9. HMI Emergency Recovery

HMI failures can remove operator visibility or control without necessarily stopping the underlying PLC.

Possible causes include:

  • Panel power failure.
  • Damaged display or touch interface.
  • Corrupted application.
  • Communication failure with the PLC.
  • Failed storage or operating system.
  • Incorrect IP address or communication configuration.
  • Authentication or licensing issues.

Recovery procedure

  1. Determine whether the machine is in a safe condition despite the HMI failure.
  2. Confirm whether the PLC and machine control functions remain healthy.
  3. Do not operate blindly if essential status, alarm, or command feedback is unavailable.
  4. Verify panel power, diagnostics, and communication status.
  5. Recover the HMI using the approved application and configuration.
  6. Validate PLC communication, alarms, setpoints, commands, and access controls.
  7. Confirm that displayed values match actual controller data.
  8. Obtain release authorization before resuming operation.

A functioning PLC does not automatically make continued operation acceptable if the operator has lost essential safety-related or process-critical information.

10. VFD, Servo, and Motor Emergency Recovery

Motor-control failures can involve overcurrent, overload, overtemperature, DC-bus faults, encoder faults, earth faults, communication loss, or mechanical overload.

10.1 Immediate precautions

  • Identify the fault code and operating conditions.
  • Stop repeated reset attempts until the cause is understood.
  • Assess unexpected movement, braking, and stored energy.
  • Isolate electrical energy when required.
  • Check motor, cable, drive, and mechanical load condition using approved procedures.
  • Verify whether the fault is electrical, mechanical, thermal, or configuration-related.

10.2 Typical faults and checks

Fault condition Possible causes Recovery focus
Overcurrent Short circuit, acceleration load, incorrect settings, mechanical jam Inspect wiring, load, and drive diagnostics
Overtemperature Blocked cooling, high ambient temperature, overload Verify cooling and loading
DC-bus overvoltage Excessive regeneration, deceleration settings, supply issues Check braking configuration and application requirements
Encoder fault Damaged cable, feedback device fault, configuration mismatch Verify feedback wiring and compatibility
Motor overload Excessive load, incorrect motor data, mechanical binding Inspect the load and motor protection settings
Communication loss Network failure, device power loss, configuration error Check communication path and configured fault response
Servo following error Mechanical obstruction, feedback issue, tuning or limit problem Stop motion and inspect the axis system

Only qualified personnel should perform electrical measurements, internal drive inspection, or work involving hazardous electrical energy.

11. CNC and Robot Emergency Recovery

11.1 CNC recovery

A CNC machine may stop because of a spindle fault, axis fault, tool changer jam, lubrication alarm, loss of reference position, or control-system failure.

Recovery should include:

  • Record the alarm and the machine state.
  • Determine whether the tool, workpiece, spindle, or axis is in an unsafe position.
  • Prevent unintended movement.
  • Follow the machine-builder procedure for tool changer, spindle, and axis recovery.
  • Preserve the current program, offsets, tool data, and relevant machine parameters.
  • Verify reference positions and required compensation data.
  • Test the recovery sequence under controlled conditions.

Do not arbitrarily move axes, change machine parameters, or alter tool offsets to clear a recovery alarm.

11.2 Robot recovery

Robot emergencies can involve collision detection, servo faults, mastering issues, external-axis faults, communication loss, or safety-system activation.

  • Establish the safe state and keep personnel outside the hazard zone.
  • Record the controller alarms and robot position.
  • Determine whether a collision or mechanical damage occurred.
  • Follow the robot manufacturer's recovery procedure.
  • Verify mastering, TCP, frames, payload, and external-axis configuration if affected.
  • Validate safety functions before motion.
  • Perform controlled movement tests before automatic operation.

A collision alarm must not be treated as a routine nuisance fault until the cause and potential damage have been assessed.

12. Industrial Network and OT Infrastructure Recovery

Industrial network failures can stop multiple PLCs, drives, robots, HMIs, and supervisory systems simultaneously.

12.1 Symptoms

  • Multiple devices report communication loss.
  • Remote I/O becomes unavailable.
  • HMI data freezes or becomes stale.
  • PLC-to-PLC communication fails.
  • Devices repeatedly connect and disconnect.
  • Network redundancy fails to recover.
  • SCADA loses process data.

12.2 Recovery steps

  1. Determine the extent of the outage.
  2. Identify the affected network segment and critical devices.
  3. Preserve switch, firewall, controller, and network diagnostics.
  4. Check power, link status, cabling, and approved network monitoring.
  5. Verify recent configuration changes.
  6. Restore approved network settings if required.
  7. Confirm communication recovery and device identity.
  8. Validate redundancy and critical control paths.
  9. Verify that machine data is current rather than merely displayed.
  10. Release affected equipment under the appropriate authorization process.

Do not factory-reset a managed industrial switch during an incident unless the recovery plan specifically requires it. Doing so can remove VLANs, port assignments, redundancy settings, and security controls.

12.3 Suspected cybersecurity incident

If unusual controller behavior, unauthorized changes, malware indicators, or suspicious network activity are detected:

  • Follow the site's OT cybersecurity incident procedure.
  • Notify the designated OT security and incident-response personnel.
  • Avoid connecting unapproved laptops, USB drives, or diagnostic devices.
  • Preserve logs and relevant evidence.
  • Do not independently disconnect critical infrastructure unless directed by the approved response plan or required to control an immediate hazard.
  • Restore systems from trusted, validated configurations under the authorized recovery process.

Cybersecurity recovery must account for operational safety, production dependencies, and the possibility that compromised configurations may remain present after connectivity returns.

13. Utility and Infrastructure Failures

Machine operation may depend on utilities beyond the control cabinet.

Utility Potential effect Recovery checks
Electrical supply Controller resets, drive trips, production stoppage Supply stability, protection devices, power-quality diagnostics
Compressed air Actuators fail to move, clamps lose function Pressure, leakage, air preparation, stored-energy hazards
Hydraulic supply Loss of clamping, lifting, or positioning Pressure, pump status, leakage, mechanical support
Cooling Overtemperature in drives, spindles, or process equipment Flow, temperature, pumps, filters
Lubrication Excessive wear, spindle or axis alarms Level, pressure, flow, lubrication system status
Industrial network Loss of control or supervision Switches, cabling, network configuration
UPS Loss of control-system continuity UPS alarms, battery condition, load, bypass status
Exhaust or ventilation Unsafe environmental conditions or process interruption Required airflow and site-specific safety limits

Never assume that an actuator is safe because the utility supply has been switched off. Accumulators, trapped pressure, suspended loads, and other stored-energy sources may remain hazardous.

14. Power Failure and Controlled Restart

Power restoration is not always equivalent to machine recovery.

After an outage, check:

  • The cause and stability of the incoming supply.
  • Electrical protection and power-quality alarms.
  • PLC, HMI, drive, CNC, and robot startup status.
  • Retained data and configuration integrity.
  • Axis reference or homing requirements.
  • Communication status across the machine and line.
  • Safety circuits and protective devices.
  • Machine sequence and product traceability state.
  • The status of workpieces, tools, fixtures, and partially completed cycles.

Restart sequencing must follow the approved procedure. Do not energize all equipment simultaneously if the system requires staged startup or utility stabilization.

For a production line, confirm the readiness of upstream and downstream stations before restarting. A single station may be healthy while the line remains unsafe or unable to handle the next product.

15. Recovery from a Machine Crash or Mechanical Jam

A crash or jam may cause hidden damage even when the machine powers up.

Recovery procedure

  1. Stop operation and secure the area.
  2. Apply required energy isolation and stored-energy controls.
  3. Record the machine state and relevant alarms.
  4. Inspect the affected mechanical components and fixtures.
  5. Check sensors, limit switches, encoders, couplings, and mounting arrangements as applicable.
  6. Verify alignment, tool condition, and calibration where required.
  7. Check for bent components, damaged cables, or displaced guards.
  8. Repair or replace damaged parts.
  9. Verify the control sequence and relevant parameters.
  10. Perform controlled tests and confirm process quality before release.

Never use controller resets as a substitute for checking the physical machine after a collision.

16. Emergency Recovery and Parameter Restoration

Parameter restoration is one possible recovery action, not the default solution to every fault.

Use it when evidence indicates that configuration is missing, corrupted, incompatible, or incorrectly modified.

Before restoring parameters:

  • Identify the correct device and machine.
  • Preserve the current configuration and diagnostics where possible.
  • Verify the approved backup.
  • Confirm hardware and firmware compatibility.
  • Determine the exact restoration scope.
  • Assess effects on safety, motion, process quality, and communication.
  • Define rollback and validation requirements.

After restoration, compare the configuration with the approved baseline and complete the required functional and safety checks.

If the cause is a failed sensor, damaged cable, mechanical obstruction, or unstable power supply, restoring unrelated parameters will not fix the underlying failure.

17. Recovery of Production State and Traceability

A machine can be technically healthy while production data remains inconsistent.

Potential issues include:

  • A product partly processed during the fault.
  • A PLC sequence that lost its step state.
  • An HMI recipe that differs from the approved production order.
  • A robot cycle interrupted before completing a transfer.
  • A CNC program stopped before its completion point.
  • A torque or measurement result not recorded.
  • A product identifier or serial number not synchronized with the tracking system.

Required checks

  • Identify products and workpieces affected by the incident.
  • Determine whether the interrupted cycle can be safely resumed.
  • Verify the actual physical position and processing state.
  • Confirm traceability data and required test results.
  • Segregate potentially nonconforming products according to the quality procedure.
  • Obtain production and quality approval where required.

Never assume that a controller's cycle-complete bit, counter, or database entry proves that the physical process completed correctly.

18. Recovery Time and Escalation

Emergency recovery should include clear escalation thresholds.

Escalate when:

  • Personnel safety is uncertain.
  • A safety device or safety function cannot be validated.
  • The fault repeatedly returns after an approved corrective action.
  • The machine has suffered a collision or significant mechanical damage.
  • The approved backup is unavailable or incompatible.
  • The failure affects multiple machines or essential utilities.
  • The problem requires specialized OEM software or protected parameters.
  • A cybersecurity incident is suspected.
  • Product quality or traceability cannot be established.
  • Recovery exceeds the site's authorized troubleshooting scope.

Time management

A recovery plan may define time limits for initial assessment, escalation, diagnosis, and decision-making. These limits should prevent unproductive troubleshooting loops rather than pressure technicians into unsafe actions.

A practical rule is to escalate when the available evidence no longer supports a safe, controlled next step.

19. Emergency Recovery Documentation

Every significant recovery should leave an auditable record.

19.1 Minimum information

  • Date and time of the incident.
  • Machine, station, and asset identification.
  • Reported symptom and operating conditions.
  • Alarm codes and diagnostic information.
  • Safety actions and isolation requirements.
  • Affected components and production impact.
  • Tests performed and findings.
  • Root cause, if established.
  • Temporary and permanent corrective actions.
  • Parts replaced and parameter changes.
  • Backup or software revision used.
  • Functional and safety validation results.
  • Production or quality verification where applicable.
  • Authorization and time of return to service.

19.2 Example maintenance record

Field Example
Asset Conveyor drive CV-03
Incident Drive trip during production
Initial response Machine stopped and area secured
Diagnosis Drive reported an overload fault
Investigation Mechanical load and motor data checked
Corrective action Identified cause corrected under approved procedure
Verification Controlled operation and interlock checks completed
Release Authorized supervisor approved return to service
Follow-up Recurring-fault trend reviewed

This is an illustrative record, not a claim about an actual machine incident.

20. Root Cause Analysis and Corrective Action

Restoring operation resolves the immediate interruption. Root cause analysis aims to prevent recurrence.

A useful investigation separates the event into:

  • Symptom: What was observed?
  • Failure mode: What function failed?
  • Physical or technical cause: What component or mechanism caused the failure?
  • Contributing factors: What made the event more likely or severe?
  • Systemic cause: Why did existing design, maintenance, operating, or management controls fail to prevent it?
  • Corrective action: What removes or controls the identified cause?
  • Effectiveness verification: How will recurrence prevention be demonstrated?

Example

A PLC loses communication with a remote I/O rack.

  • Symptom: Several stations stop simultaneously.
  • Failure mode: Remote I/O communication is interrupted.
  • Technical cause: A network connection is intermittently failing.
  • Contributing factors: Cable routing, connector condition, or environmental exposure may be involved.
  • Corrective action: Repair the identified connection issue and verify the communication path.
  • Preventive action: Improve cable protection or inspection practices if the investigation supports that action.
  • Effectiveness check: Monitor communication faults and confirm that the problem does not recur under comparable operating conditions.

Avoid declaring the root cause to be “operator error” or “communication failure” without identifying the mechanism and the controls that should have prevented or detected it.

21. Emergency Recovery Readiness

Emergency recovery is more effective when the necessary resources are prepared before an incident.

21.1 Readiness requirements

  • Current emergency contact and escalation lists.
  • Machine-specific emergency procedures.
  • Accurate electrical and pneumatic drawings.
  • Approved PLC, HMI, CNC, robot, and drive backups.
  • Device models, firmware, and engineering software records.
  • Critical spare-parts inventory.
  • Suitable and authorized diagnostic tools.
  • Access to OEM recovery procedures.
  • Tested backup and restoration methods.
  • Documented safety isolation points.
  • Defined recovery roles and release authority.
  • Periodic drills for critical failure scenarios.

21.2 Recovery drills

A drill can test whether maintenance personnel can locate the correct backup, identify the affected equipment, retrieve diagnostic information, and follow the recovery procedure.

A tabletop exercise or simulated fault is often preferable to deliberately disrupting a live production machine. Any practical drill on equipment must be authorized and designed so it does not introduce unacceptable risk.

22. Emergency Recovery KPIs

KPI What it measures

Repeat incident rate Frequency of recurring incidents

Recovery validation compliance Whether required verification was completed before release

MTTR can mean mean time to repair, restore, or recover depending on the organization's definition. Record the exact definition before comparing results between machines or reporting periods.

23. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Resetting alarms without recording diagnostics.
  • Repeatedly cycling power without identifying the cause.
  • Bypassing safety devices to resume production.
  • Restoring an unverified backup.
  • Downloading the wrong PLC or HMI project.
  • Changing multiple parameters simultaneously without a controlled test plan.
  • Moving a robot or CNC axis without understanding its physical state.
  • Assuming a healthy controller means the entire machine is safe.
  • Ignoring interrupted-cycle and traceability issues.
  • Releasing equipment before required safety and quality checks.
  • Failing to document temporary repairs.
  • Closing the work order without addressing recurrence risk.

24. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is emergency recovery in industrial automation?

It is the controlled process of establishing a safe state, diagnosing an abnormal condition, restoring equipment functions, validating the result, and authorizing return to service.

Q2. What is the first priority during a machine emergency?

Protect personnel and control the hazard. Production recovery begins only after the required safety conditions have been established.

Q3. Is an emergency stop equivalent to lockout/tagout?

No. An emergency stop activates a protective stopping function, whereas lockout/tagout controls hazardous energy during maintenance when required. An emergency stop may leave electrical or stored energy present.

Q4. Why should fault history be recorded before resetting a controller?

Resetting can remove or obscure useful diagnostic information needed to identify the actual cause and prevent recurrence.

Q5. What should happen after a robot collision?

Secure the area, control hazardous energy, inspect for mechanical and electrical damage, verify mastering and calibration where affected, and complete the prescribed functional and safety validation before release.

Q6. When should parameter restoration be used?

When the investigation indicates that configuration has been lost, corrupted, incorrectly modified, or made incompatible. It should not replace diagnosis of unrelated hardware or mechanical faults.

Q7. Why can a machine remain unsafe after a successful restart?

Protective functions, calibration, interlocks, process settings, or physical components may still be defective. Successful startup proves only that some startup conditions were satisfied.

Q8. What is the difference between recovery and root cause elimination?

Recovery restores the required function. Root cause elimination addresses the underlying cause or systemic weakness so the failure is less likely to recur.

Q9. Why is production traceability part of recovery?

Interrupted cycles may leave products partly processed, untested, or incorrectly recorded. Their actual processing status must be established before acceptance.

Q10. What authorizes return to service?

Completion of required technical, safety, and process checks, followed by the approval specified in the site's release procedure.

25. Final Emergency Recovery Checklist

  • Emergency condition classified and appropriate response activated.
  • Personnel protected and affected area controlled.
  • Required emergency stop or protective functions used appropriately.
  • Hazardous energy controlled and safe state verified.
  • Responsible personnel notified and escalation requirements met.
  • Alarms, logs, and equipment condition documented.
  • Failure cause investigated to the extent necessary for safe recovery.
  • Approved repair or restoration procedure followed.
  • Configuration, calibration, and dependencies verified where affected.
  • Safety functions tested as required.
  • Machine operation and production quality validated.
  • Traceability and interrupted-cycle status checked.
  • Return-to-service authorization obtained.
  • Maintenance record completed.
  • Corrective and preventive actions assigned where required.
  • Recovery readiness improvements identified.
