1. Definition and Purpose

A Bill of Materials (BOM) is a structured, controlled list of the materials, components, assemblies, and other items required to build, install, operate, repair, or maintain a machine or system.

In industrial automation, a BOM may cover the complete electrical panel, PLC system, HMI, drives, sensors, safety devices, robots, CNC equipment, mechanical assemblies, pneumatic circuits, cables, connectors, and approved software or license items.

A BOM answers practical questions:

  • What components are installed in the machine?
  • What exact part number and model does each component use?
  • How many units are required?
  • Where is each item installed?
  • Which supplier or manufacturer provides it?
  • Which alternative or successor part is approved?
  • Which items should be stocked as critical spares?
  • What changes are required when a component becomes obsolete?
  • Can maintenance restore the equipment without reverse-engineering the entire machine?

For handover, the BOM must accurately represent the agreed and installed configuration. A list copied from the original quotation may not be sufficient because substitutions, design changes, and commissioning modifications may have occurred.

2. Why a BOM Is Important

2.1 Engineering and Installation

  • Establishes the component requirements for machine construction and installation.
  • Links components to electrical drawings, mechanical drawings, panel layouts, and equipment tags.
  • Supports verification that selected components meet the design requirements.
  • Identifies dependencies between assemblies and individual components.

2.2 Procurement and Cost Control

  • Provides accurate descriptions and part numbers for purchase orders.
  • Helps prevent incorrect or incompatible parts from being ordered.
  • Supports material planning, vendor comparison, and project cost estimation.
  • Identifies long-lead-time components that could delay a project or repair.

2.3 Maintenance and Breakdown Recovery

  • Helps technicians identify the exact failed component.
  • Reduces troubleshooting and procurement delays.
  • Supports planned maintenance and spare-parts management.
  • Identifies obsolete components and suitable replacement strategies.

2.4 Traceability and Change Management

  • Records the manufacturer, model, revision, and applicable serial or lot information.
  • Connects engineering changes to affected equipment.
  • Supports audit evidence and lifecycle management.
  • Helps establish which components were installed before and after a modification.

2.5 Handover

A useful handover BOM should reflect the installed configuration, identify critical parts, point to relevant drawings and manuals, and make it possible for authorized personnel to obtain compatible replacements.

3. Types of BOM

Different BOMs serve different purposes. They should not be treated as interchangeable merely because they contain similar rows.

BOM type Purpose Typical contents
Engineering BOM (EBOM) Represents the engineering-designed product or system Components and assemblies from engineering design
Manufacturing BOM (MBOM) Supports manufacturing and assembly Production assemblies, components, and manufacturing requirements
Service BOM Supports field service and repair Serviceable assemblies and replaceable components
Spare-parts BOM Supports maintenance inventory Recommended spares, quantities, and replacement parts
Electrical BOM Lists electrical and automation hardware PLCs, modules, breakers, contactors, relays, terminals, cables
Mechanical BOM Lists mechanical hardware and assemblies Frames, bearings, gears, fasteners, actuators, tooling
Pneumatic/hydraulic BOM Lists fluid-power components Valves, cylinders, pumps, filters, fittings, hoses
Software/configuration BOM Records relevant software and digital dependencies Software products, versions, licenses, firmware, device options
Project BOM Supports project purchasing and installation Planned materials and project-specific equipment
As-built BOM Records the approved installed configuration Actual installed components and approved substitutions

A machine may require several of these views. For example, the engineering BOM may include every design component, while the service BOM groups replaceable assemblies in a way that is useful during breakdowns.

4. BOM Versus Other Engineering Documents

Document Main question answered
BOM Which parts and quantities make up the system?
Parts list Which parts are identified in a particular drawing, assembly, or document?
Equipment list Which equipment items exist in the project or plant?
Asset register Which assets are managed for ownership, maintenance, lifecycle, and financial purposes?
Spare-parts list Which replacement items should be available for maintenance?
I/O list Which signals are exchanged between field devices and controllers?
IP list Which network devices use which network addresses and settings?
Electrical drawings How are electrical components connected?
Panel layout Where are components physically mounted?
Manuals How must equipment be installed, configured, operated, and maintained?

These documents complement one another. A BOM does not replace the electrical drawings, and an asset register does not necessarily contain the detailed component-level information required to replace a PLC module.

5. BOM Hierarchy and Structure

5.1 Hierarchical BOM

A complex machine is often represented as a hierarchy of assemblies and components.

Machine
  → Automation System
    → Control Panel
      → PLC Assembly
        → PLC CPU
        → Digital Input Module
        → Digital Output Module
        → Analog Input Module
      → 24 V DC Power Supply
      → Industrial Ethernet Switch
    → Operator Station
      → HMI Panel
    → Drive System
      → VFD
      → Motor
      → Motor Cable
    → Safety System
      → Safety Controller
      → Safety I/O
      → Guard Switches

The hierarchy makes it possible to understand which components belong to which subsystem. It also supports quantity calculations, service replacement, and change-impact analysis.

5.2 BOM Levels

Level Description Example
Level 0 Complete machine or product Robotic assembly machine
Level 1 Major subsystem Electrical control system
Level 2 Assembly Main control panel
Level 3 Subassembly PLC rack assembly
Level 4 Individual component Digital input module
Level 5 Component detail, where needed Connector or replaceable accessory

Not every BOM needs five levels. The hierarchy should be deep enough to support the intended engineering, procurement, manufacturing, or maintenance purpose.

5.3 Parent-Child Relationships

A parent item contains or groups child items.

For example:

  • Parent: Main control panel.
  • Child: PLC assembly.
  • Grandchild: PLC CPU and I/O modules.

Quantities may be defined as quantity per parent assembly. If a machine contains two identical panels and each panel requires three modules, the total requirement is:

\[ Q\_{\text{total}} = Q\_{\text{per assembly}} \times N\_{\text{assemblies}} \]
\[ Q\_{\text{total}} = 3 \times 2 = 6 \]

This calculation assumes both panels use the same configuration and that the stated quantity applies to each panel.

6. Essential BOM Fields

6.1 Identification and Description

Field Purpose
BOM item number Identifies a row or item position within the BOM
Item ID/material number Unique identifier in the organization's material system
Item description Clear description of the item
Parent assembly Identifies the assembly containing the item
Equipment tag Connects the item to the installed equipment
Reference designator Identifies a component on a drawing, such as K1 or F1
Assembly level Identifies the component's position in the hierarchy

The BOM item number and material number are not necessarily the same. One identifies the item's position in a particular BOM; the other identifies the material in the organization's inventory or ERP system.

6.2 Manufacturer and Ordering Information

Field Purpose
Manufacturer Original equipment or component manufacturer
Manufacturer part number Exact manufacturer's product identifier
Order code Full ordering code, including relevant options
Supplier/vendor Approved source of supply
Supplier part number Vendor's own identifier, if different
Approved alternate Reviewed alternative part number
Lifecycle status Active, obsolete, discontinued, or other defined status
Lead time Expected procurement time
Country of origin Recorded where required by procurement or compliance rules

The exact manufacturer part number is particularly important for PLC modules, drives, safety devices, power supplies, sensors, and communication equipment. A product family name alone may not uniquely identify the installed part.

6.3 Technical and Installation Information

Field Purpose
Quantity Number of items required
Unit of measure Each, metre, kilogram, set, or other unit
Location Panel, cabinet, machine, axis, or assembly location
Drawing reference Drawing number and relevant sheet
Electrical rating Voltage, current, power, and relevant ratings
Signal/interface Electrical signal, communication interface, or connector type
Dimensions Relevant physical dimensions
Firmware or revision Hardware, firmware, or product revision where applicable
Serial or lot number Traceability for individually tracked or batch-tracked items
Criticality Operational or safety consequence of item failure
Spare status Whether a spare is required or stocked
Notes Relevant compatibility, installation, or purchasing constraints

Not all fields apply to every component. For example, a cable requires length, type, and routing information, while a PLC CPU may require firmware compatibility and communication specifications.

9. Correct Part Identification

9.1 Why Exact Identification Matters

Two components may look nearly identical but have different electrical ratings, communication capabilities, firmware requirements, dimensions, or safety certifications.

A replacement that physically fits can still be electrically, functionally, or safety-wise incompatible. Machines are remarkably unimpressed by visual similarity.

9.2 PLC Components

Verify:

  • Exact CPU and module order codes.
  • Supported rack, base, backplane, and bus arrangement.
  • Digital input or output channel count and electrical type.
  • Analog input/output signal ranges and resolution.
  • Required power supply and current capacity.
  • Supported communication protocols and interfaces.
  • Firmware compatibility with the project configuration.
  • Required terminal connectors and accessories.
  • Product lifecycle and successor information.

9.3 Drives and Servo Systems

Verify:

  • Input supply voltage and phase configuration.
  • Rated motor power and current.
  • Overload and duty requirements.
  • Motor type and feedback compatibility.
  • Encoder or resolver interface.
  • Communication protocol and option modules.
  • Braking resistor, reactor, filter, and accessory requirements.
  • Firmware and configuration compatibility.
  • Safety functions and relevant certification requirements.
  • Cable type, length, connector, and shielding requirements.

A drive should not be selected solely by motor kilowatts. Current, overload duty, supply conditions, environment, and application requirements matter too.

9.4 Sensors and Instruments

Verify:

  • Sensing principle and target compatibility.
  • Supply voltage and output type.
  • PNP/NPN or relay output arrangement.
  • Switching distance or measurement range.
  • Connector, cable, and mounting dimensions.
  • Environmental protection and temperature rating.
  • Analog signal range and accuracy, where applicable.
  • Response time and switching frequency, where relevant.
  • Required certifications or safety rating.

9.5 Circuit Protection and Switching Devices

Verify:

  • Rated operational voltage and current.
  • Breaking capacity and protective coordination requirements.
  • Trip characteristics and settings.
  • Pole count and switching function.
  • Coil voltage and frequency for contactors or relays.
  • Auxiliary contacts and required accessories.
  • Applicable standards and panel requirements.

Never substitute a circuit breaker, fuse, contactor, or safety device purely because its dimensions match the original.

10. BOM for Mechanical, Pneumatic, and Hydraulic Systems

A complete industrial BOM is not limited to electrical components.

10.1 Mechanical BOM

Common items include:

  • Frames, brackets, plates, and structural assemblies.
  • Bearings, shafts, gears, couplings, and belts.
  • Linear guides, ball screws, and actuator assemblies.
  • Fasteners, washers, spacers, and locking devices.
  • Tooling, fixtures, grippers, and machine-specific attachments.
  • Guards, covers, and mounting hardware.

Relevant information may include dimensions, material grade, tolerance, surface treatment, drawing number, and assembly location.

10.2 Pneumatic BOM

Common items include:

  • Cylinders and grippers.
  • Directional control valves.
  • Pressure regulators and filters.
  • Pressure switches and transmitters.
  • Flow controls, silencers, and fittings.
  • Tubing, hoses, and manifolds.

Record port sizes, operating pressure range, valve function, coil voltage, connector type, seal compatibility, and required accessories.

10.3 Hydraulic BOM

Common items include:

  • Pumps, motors, and reservoirs.
  • Directional, pressure-control, and flow-control valves.
  • Cylinders and hydraulic actuators.
  • Filters, coolers, hoses, and fittings.
  • Pressure transmitters, gauges, and switches.

Record pressure and flow ratings, fluid compatibility, mounting interfaces, port configuration, and relevant replacement-kit information.

11. Installed BOM Versus Spare-Parts BOM

These serve different purposes.

Installed BOM: Describes components fitted to the machine or system.

Spare-parts BOM: Identifies components recommended or required for replacement and maintenance.

One installed component may require several spare items. Conversely, one spare module may be compatible with multiple installed machines if engineering confirms that compatibility.

11.1 Spare-Priority Classification

A site may classify spares using a risk-based system such as the following:

Category Typical consideration Example
Critical Failure causes major production loss, unacceptable risk, or long recovery time Unique critical controller or safety component
High Failure significantly affects production or requires urgent replacement Essential drive or proprietary communication module
Medium Failure affects a subsystem but recovery or substitution is manageable Common sensor or standard relay
Low Low operational impact and easy availability Standard non-critical hardware

This is an illustrative classification. Use the plant's approved criticality method.

11.2 Spare-Quantity Planning

Spare quantities should be based on risk, failure history, installed population, repair time, supplier lead time, obsolescence, and cost.

Useful inputs include:

  • Number of installed units.
  • Historical failure rate.
  • Consequence of failure.
  • Supplier lead time.
  • Availability of repair or exchange service.
  • Compatibility of one spare with multiple machines.
  • Storage conditions and shelf life.
  • Product lifecycle status.

There is no universal rule that every component requires one spare. A critical proprietary component with a long lead time may justify stock, while a low-cost standard item with immediate local availability may not.

11.3 Inventory Parameters

Parameter Meaning
Installed quantity Number of items fitted to the equipment
Spare quantity Number of replacement items held or recommended
Minimum stock Lowest permitted inventory level
Maximum stock Target upper inventory level
Reorder point Inventory threshold that triggers replenishment
Lead time Time from order placement to availability
Repairable item Item that may be repaired and returned to service
Exchange item Item supplied through a replacement or exchange arrangement
Shelf life Time an item remains suitable for use under specified conditions

A simple reorder-point concept is:

\[ \text{Reorder Point} = \text{Expected Demand During Lead Time} + \text{Safety Stock} \]

The calculation method must match the organization's inventory policy and demand assumptions.

12. Obsolescence and Replacement Management

Industrial automation products can become obsolete while the machines they control remain operational for many years.

A BOM should help maintenance identify lifecycle risks before a breakdown forces an emergency purchase.

12.1 Lifecycle Status

Possible statuses include:

  • Active and available.
  • Active with limited availability.
  • Manufacturer-discontinued.
  • Last-time-buy period.
  • Obsolete with an official successor.
  • Obsolete without a direct replacement.
  • Repair-only or exchange-only.
  • Compatibility not yet verified.

Use current manufacturer information or approved supplier documentation to confirm lifecycle status.

12.2 Successor Part Assessment

Before approving a replacement, compare:

  1. Electrical and mechanical compatibility.
  2. Functional capability and performance.
  3. Communication protocol and network compatibility.
  4. Firmware, configuration, and engineering-software requirements.
  5. Connector, wiring, and mounting requirements.
  6. Safety certification and validated safety behavior where relevant.
  7. Availability of programming tools and technical support.
  8. Effects on existing backups, spare stock, and maintenance procedures.
  9. Required testing, documentation updates, and approvals.

A successor part number is not proof of a drop-in replacement. It may require a configuration change, wiring modification, software migration, or full engineering validation.

12.3 Obsolescence Register

Equipment tag Installed part Lifecycle status Replacement strategy Action
PLC-01 Existing PLC CPU To be verified Evaluate supported successor Check manufacturer lifecycle notice
VFD-03 Existing drive Limited availability Verify compatible replacement Assess motor and control compatibility
HMI-02 Existing operator panel Discontinued Plan platform migration Review screen and communication changes
SEN-17 Existing sensor Active Maintain standard stock Confirm current order code

This example is a planning template, not a statement about the lifecycle status of any real product.

13. Approved Alternatives and Substitutions

A BOM may list approved alternatives where equivalent products are acceptable.

An approved alternative must be evaluated against the application, not simply against its description or price.

Consider:

  • Electrical and mechanical ratings.
  • Functional characteristics.
  • Environmental and operating limits.
  • Interface and protocol compatibility.
  • Certifications and safety requirements.
  • Manufacturer support and lifecycle.
  • Configuration, installation, and maintenance implications.
  • Effects on the validated machine design.

Record the original part number, proposed alternative, comparison results, approving authority, applicable machine or assembly, and any restrictions.

Safety-related components, protection devices, and validated control components must not be substituted without the required engineering review and approvals. An alternative that is suitable for ordinary control may be unsuitable for a safety function.

14. Software, Firmware, and License BOM

Modern machines depend on software and configuration data as well as physical hardware.

A software/configuration BOM can record:

  • PLC engineering software and version.
  • PLC project version or controlled backup identifier.
  • HMI development software and runtime version.
  • CNC or robot programming tools and relevant options.
  • Drive configuration tools and parameter backups.
  • Firmware versions of PLCs, HMIs, drives, network devices, and remote I/O.
  • Required communication drivers and libraries.
  • Runtime licenses, device options, and licensed features.
  • Operating-system and database dependencies where relevant.
  • Configuration files, backup locations, and recovery instructions.
  • License ownership, transferability, renewal conditions, and authorized access arrangements.

14.1 Software BOM Example

Item Software or dependency Version record Purpose
SW-001 PLC engineering software Exact installed version PLC programming and diagnostics
SW-002 HMI development software Exact installed version HMI application maintenance
SW-003 Drive configuration tool Exact installed version Drive parameter backup and setup
SW-004 Robot programming environment Installed version and options Robot program maintenance
SW-005 Runtime license License identifier or controlled record Enables required runtime functionality

Do not place passwords, private keys, or license activation secrets in an ordinary BOM. Store sensitive credentials in an approved secure system and reference the authorized retrieval procedure.

15. BOM Reconciliation Against As-Built Documents

The BOM must agree with the actual machine and the controlled engineering records.

Compare it with:

  • As-built electrical drawings.
  • Electrical schematics and panel layouts.
  • Mechanical drawings and assembly drawings.
  • I/O lists and network/IP lists.
  • PLC, HMI, robot, and CNC hardware configurations.
  • Manufacturer manuals and order-code information.
  • Purchase orders and approved substitutions.
  • Physical inspection of installed equipment.
  • Spare-parts inventory and maintenance records.

15.1 Reconciliation Procedure

  1. Identify the machine, subsystem, and BOM revision under review.
  2. Compare each listed item with the corresponding drawing or assembly.
  3. Verify manufacturer, exact model, order code, rating, and quantity.
  4. Inspect physical labels where access is safe and authorized.
  5. Compare the recorded item with the actual controller rack, panel, machine, or assembly.
  6. Record missing, extra, replaced, damaged, or unidentified items.
  7. Resolve discrepancies through approved engineering change control.
  8. Update the BOM and affected documents.
  9. Verify the final revision and retain evidence of the review.

Physical inspection should not require opening energized equipment or bypassing guards. Follow the site's electrical safety, isolation, and access procedures.

16. Engineering Change Control

A BOM is a controlled engineering record. Changes must be traceable.

16.1 Typical Reasons for Change

  • Component replacement due to failure.
  • Manufacturer discontinuation.
  • Approved alternative part.
  • Change in quantity or assembly structure.
  • Design improvement or cost reduction.
  • Change in electrical rating or interface.
  • Machine retrofit or expansion.
  • Safety-related design change.
  • Software or firmware compatibility requirement.

16.2 Change Record Fields

Field Purpose
Change number Unique engineering change identifier
Original item Existing part number and description
New item Proposed or approved replacement
Reason Technical or operational justification
Affected assemblies Machines or subsystems requiring update
Compatibility review Recorded engineering assessment
Safety review Required assessment for safety-related changes
Approval Authorized engineering or change-control approval
Implementation date Date the change becomes effective
Stock disposition Treatment of existing inventory
Verification Required test and acceptance evidence
Document updates BOM, drawings, manuals, and other affected records

16.3 Effectivity

Effectivity defines where and when a change applies. A change may apply to:

  • All newly manufactured machines.
  • A specific machine serial-number range.
  • A particular production line or subsystem.
  • A specific retrofit campaign.
  • Spare parts issued after an approved date.

Without effectivity information, maintenance may be unable to determine which replacement part or procedure applies to a particular machine.

17. BOM Quality Checks and Common Errors

17.1 Common Errors

  • Using only a product family name instead of the exact part number.
  • Recording an incorrect quantity or unit of measure.
  • Listing planned components that were never installed.
  • Failing to record approved substitutions.
  • Using supplier descriptions that do not uniquely identify the component.
  • Omitting connectors, mounting accessories, terminal covers, or required options.
  • Ignoring firmware and configuration compatibility.
  • Treating a discontinued component's successor as automatically interchangeable.
  • Mixing installed quantities and spare quantities in one field.
  • Recording obsolete revisions without identifying their status.
  • Failing to link items to drawings, tags, or parent assemblies.
  • Keeping sensitive software credentials directly in the BOM.
  • Updating the BOM without updating affected drawings or configuration records.

17.2 Quality Checklist

  • Each item has a unique and unambiguous identifier.
  • Manufacturer and exact part number are recorded where applicable.
  • Descriptions contain enough detail to distinguish similar items.
  • Quantities and units of measure are correct.
  • Parent-child relationships and assembly levels are consistent.
  • Installed location and drawing references are provided where relevant.
  • Ratings, interfaces, and compatibility requirements are recorded.
  • Approved alternatives and restrictions are documented.
  • Spare recommendations are separated from installed quantities.
  • Lifecycle and obsolescence risks are reviewed.
  • Software, firmware, and licensing dependencies are addressed where applicable.
  • Changes are approved and traceable.
  • The BOM matches the installed system.
  • The document revision and approval status are clear.

18. Standards and Quality Management

The applicable standards depend on the equipment, industry, contract, and jurisdiction. Standards relevant to BOM creation and component selection may include:

Standard or framework Relevance to BOM management
ISO 9001 Document control, traceability, purchasing controls, and change management within a quality management system
IEC 60204-1 Electrical equipment of machinery and associated component-selection requirements
IEC 61439 series Low-voltage switchgear and controlgear assemblies, where applicable
IEC 61082-1 Preparation of electrotechnical documents
IEC 62443 series Industrial automation cybersecurity, including relevant system and component considerations
ISO 12100 Machinery risk assessment and risk reduction
ISO 13849-1 Safety-related parts of control systems
IEC 62061 Functional safety of machinery control systems

A BOM does not establish compliance by itself. Compliance depends on the complete design, component suitability, installation, verification, and required documentation.

19. BOM in SAP and Maintenance Systems

In an industrial organization, BOM data may be maintained in an ERP system, a computerized maintenance management system (CMMS), a product lifecycle management system (PLM), or controlled engineering files.

19.1 Common System Functions

  • Material master records identify and describe purchasable items.
  • BOM records define component relationships and quantities.
  • Equipment records identify maintainable assets.
  • Functional locations identify where equipment is installed.
  • Spare-parts records support stock management and replenishment.
  • Purchase records establish procurement history and supplier information.
  • Maintenance notifications and work orders record failures, repairs, and component replacements.

The exact functionality depends on the organization's SAP configuration or other enterprise system.

19.2 Practical Maintenance Workflow

Equipment fault identified
  → Identify failed component and equipment tag
  → Verify exact part number against BOM and installed label
  → Check stock and approved alternatives
  → Raise material request or purchase requirement
  → Replace or repair using the approved procedure
  → Record the maintenance activity and material usage
  → Update BOM and related documents if the installed configuration changed

Replacing a component with an identical approved part may require only maintenance-record updates. A change to a different model, interface, rating, or configuration may require engineering approval and updates to multiple documents.

20. BOM Handover Package

A complete BOM handover package should contain more than a single spreadsheet.

Recommended contents include:

  • Approved as-built BOM.
  • Hierarchical BOM or assembly breakdown, where applicable.
  • Exact manufacturer part numbers and technical descriptions.
  • Installed quantities and locations.
  • Approved alternate and successor-part records.
  • Critical-spares recommendations and stock information.
  • Obsolescence register for relevant components.
  • Cross-references to drawings, manuals, and equipment tags.
  • Software, firmware, configuration, and license dependency records.
  • Engineering change history and applicable effectivity.
  • Document revision register and approval status.
  • Outstanding discrepancies and agreed corrective actions.

Adapt the folder structure to the site's document-control rules and existing enterprise systems.

21. Final BOM Acceptance Checklist

  • The BOM identifies the correct machine, subsystem, and revision.
  • The listed configuration matches the installed equipment.
  • Exact part numbers, ratings, and quantities are verified.
  • Components are traceable to relevant drawings and equipment tags.
  • Approved substitutions are documented.
  • Critical spare requirements are identified and justified.
  • Obsolete and lifecycle-sensitive components are recorded.
  • Software, firmware, configuration, and licensing dependencies are addressed.
  • Safety-related and protection components have the required engineering approval.
  • Changes and effective dates are traceable.
  • BOM data is consistent with relevant ERP, CMMS, or engineering records.
  • The receiving team can identify and procure compatible replacement parts.
  • The final document has the required review, approval, and revision information.

Core principle: A useful BOM must identify the correct component, establish where and how it is used, state how many are required, and preserve enough technical and lifecycle information to support safe replacement and long-term maintenance.
