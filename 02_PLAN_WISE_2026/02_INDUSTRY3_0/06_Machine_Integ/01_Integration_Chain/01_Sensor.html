1. Purpose of Sensors in Machine Integration

A sensor detects a physical condition or process variable and converts it into a signal that a control system can interpret. In industrial automation, sensors form the interface between the physical machine and the PLC.

Sensors allow a machine to determine whether a component is present, a cylinder has reached a position, a motor is rotating, a part has reached a station, or a process variable has crossed a limit.

Common applications include:

  • Part presence detection on assembly and machining lines.
  • Cylinder extended and retracted position confirmation.
  • Conveyor position and speed monitoring.
  • Robot gripper part detection.
  • Workpiece clamping confirmation.
  • Pressure, temperature, flow, and level monitoring.
  • Machine guarding and safety-related detection.
  • Position and speed feedback from encoders.
  • Quality inspection using vision and measurement sensors.

A sensor is not simply a device that turns an electrical signal ON or OFF. It may produce a discrete switching signal, a continuous analog signal, a pulse train, a serial data stream, or a network message.

The purpose of machine integration is to ensure that the sensor, electrical interface, PLC input, control logic, mechanical arrangement, and diagnostic system work together correctly.

Each stage can introduce a different type of failure.

For example, a proximity sensor may detect a metal component correctly but fail to activate the PLC input because of incorrect PNP/NPN compatibility, a broken conductor, missing common wiring, incorrect module configuration, or an input-channel fault.

Troubleshooting should therefore follow the entire signal chain rather than assuming that the sensor itself is defective.

4. Inductive Proximity Sensors

Inductive proximity sensors detect suitable conductive targets, commonly metal objects, without physical contact.

They are widely used in manufacturing because they avoid the mechanical wear associated with contact switches.

4.1 Operating principle

An oscillator in the sensor creates an electromagnetic field near its sensing face. When a suitable metal target enters the field, the target affects the oscillator's energy. The sensor's electronics detect the change and switch the output.

4.2 Important specifications

  • Sensing distance.
  • Target material and correction factor.
  • Flush or non-flush mounting.
  • Supply voltage.
  • Output type: PNP, NPN, push-pull, or another specified interface.
  • Normally open or normally closed output behavior.
  • Switching frequency.
  • Output current rating.
  • Short-circuit and reverse-polarity protection.
  • Temperature and ingress-protection ratings.
  • Connector or cable type.

4.3 Flush and non-flush mounting

A flush-mountable sensor is designed for installation in a mounting arrangement that allows its sensing face to be positioned flush with the specified surrounding material, subject to manufacturer requirements.

A non-flush sensor generally requires additional clearance around the sensing face because nearby metal can influence its electromagnetic field.

Incorrect mounting can reduce sensing distance or cause false switching.

Always follow the manufacturer's specified clearance, spacing, and mounting instructions.

4.4 Target material

Inductive sensors do not necessarily detect every metal at the same distance.

The rated sensing distance is commonly established using a specified standard target. Other materials may produce different effective sensing distances.

For example, stainless steel, aluminium, and copper may have different correction factors depending on the sensor design.

If a sensor works with a steel test piece but misses an aluminium production part, verify the target material and effective sensing distance before replacing the sensor.

5. Photoelectric Sensors

Photoelectric sensors detect objects using emitted light and a receiving element.

They are suitable for detecting many non-metallic objects, including cardboard, plastic, glass, and selected transparent materials, depending on sensor type and configuration.

5.1 Through-beam

A separate transmitter and receiver face one another.

Transmitter  ------ Light beam ------> Receiver
                     |
                 Object blocks beam
                     |
                     v
                Output changes

Advantages:

  • Generally suitable for relatively long sensing distances.
  • Strong detection performance when correctly aligned.
  • Object reflectivity is often less important than with reflective arrangements.

Limitations:

  • Requires mounting and alignment on opposite sides.
  • Requires separate transmitter and receiver wiring or a specified paired interface.

5.2 Retro-reflective

The sensor transmits light toward a reflector and detects the returned light.

An object passing between the sensor and reflector interrupts the optical path.

Considerations include reflector alignment, object geometry, and special versions needed for transparent targets.

5.3 Diffuse-reflective

The transmitter and receiver are generally located in the same sensor housing. The sensor detects light reflected from the target.

Performance depends on factors such as:

  • Target colour and reflectivity.
  • Distance.
  • Surface angle.
  • Ambient light.
  • Background objects.
  • Sensor adjustment.

A dark or angled object may be detected less reliably than a bright, highly reflective object.

5.4 Background suppression

Some photoelectric sensors distinguish a target from objects farther behind it using optical or triangulation-based techniques.

This can improve reliability when the background might otherwise cause false detection.

Background suppression is not a guarantee against every surface or distance condition. Validate the actual target and background combination.

6. Capacitive, Ultrasonic, and Magnetic Sensors

6.1 Capacitive sensors

Capacitive sensors respond to changes in the electric field near the sensing surface.

Depending on the model, they can detect metal, non-metallic solids, liquids, powders, and other materials.

Applications include:

  • Liquid-level detection through suitable non-metallic walls.
  • Presence detection of plastic components.
  • Material detection in hoppers.
  • Detection of selected non-metallic workpieces.

Common issues include sensitivity to moisture, contamination, mounting conditions, and nearby objects. Teach or adjust the sensor according to the manufacturer's instructions.

6.2 Ultrasonic sensors

Ultrasonic sensors use sound waves and measure characteristics such as echo timing or signal behavior.

Typical applications include:

  • Distance measurement.
  • Level detection.
  • Presence detection.
  • Detection of objects whose optical properties make photoelectric sensing difficult.

Important factors include dead zone, beam geometry, target angle, sound-absorbing materials, temperature, and response time.

A soft or angled target may produce an unreliable echo.

6.3 Magnetic sensors

Magnetic sensors detect a magnetic field or a magnetically responsive target.

A common industrial example is a magnetic cylinder-position sensor installed in a pneumatic cylinder slot. It detects the magnet attached to the piston.

Typical signals include:

  • Cylinder retracted.
  • Cylinder extended.
  • Intermediate position, if the application and sensor arrangement support it.

Verify that the sensor is compatible with the cylinder and that the magnetic target reaches the required sensing zone.

7. Mechanical Limit Switches

A mechanical limit switch changes its electrical contact state when its actuator is moved by a machine component.

Typical applications include:

  • Mechanical end-of-travel detection.
  • Door-position indication.
  • Cam-operated sequence detection.
  • Position confirmation on mechanisms.

Common contact arrangements include:

  • Normally open (NO).
  • Normally closed (NC).
  • Changeover contacts.

The words normally open and normally closed describe the contact's specified normal, unactuated condition, not necessarily the machine's usual running state.

Limit switches can provide a direct mechanical indication of position, but they introduce contact wear, possible misalignment, actuator damage, and mechanical impact concerns.

A standard limit switch is not automatically suitable for a safety function. Safety-related use requires an appropriate device and validated safety design.

8. PNP, NPN, and Push-Pull Outputs

Output compatibility is one of the most important parts of sensor-to-PLC integration.

8.1 PNP output

A PNP switching output typically sources positive voltage to the load when active.

For a common 24 VDC arrangement:

+24 VDC
   |
   v
Sensor supply
   |
Sensor PNP output
   |
   v
PLC digital input
   |
Input common / 0 V reference
   |
   v

This is a conceptual arrangement. Actual terminal connections depend on the sensor's circuit, PLC input module, and manufacturer's wiring diagram.

When the PNP output is active, the PLC input receives a positive voltage relative to its reference.

8.2 NPN output

An NPN switching output typically sinks current toward 0 V when active.

+24 VDC
   |
   v
PLC input circuit
   |
   v
Sensor NPN output
   |
   v
0 VDC

This is also conceptual. The actual input module must be compatible with the output topology.

When active, the sensor provides a path for current from the PLC input circuit toward 0 V.

8.3 Push-pull output

A push-pull output can actively drive the output toward both the positive supply and the 0 V reference, subject to the device's electrical design.

Push-pull outputs can offer interface flexibility, but compatibility must still be verified. They are not universally interchangeable with every PLC input arrangement.

8.4 PNP versus NPN comparison

Characteristic PNP NPN
Typical active output behavior Sources positive voltage Sinks current toward 0 V

Critical rule: Match the sensor output topology to the PLC input circuit. Never decide compatibility from the connector pin count or wire colours alone.

9. Two-Wire and Three-Wire Sensors

9.1 Three-wire sensor

A common three-wire DC sensor has:

  • Supply positive.
  • Supply negative.
  • Switching output.

For many common DC sensor products, brown is positive supply, blue is negative supply, and black is the switching output. This is a common convention, not a substitute for the sensor's datasheet.

9.2 Two-wire sensor

A two-wire sensor is connected in series with the load or input circuit and uses the same two conductors for power and switching behavior.

Important characteristics include:

  • On-state voltage drop.
  • Off-state leakage current.
  • Minimum load requirements.
  • Compatibility with the PLC input threshold.
  • Compatibility with AC or DC supply, as applicable.

A two-wire sensor may leak enough current in its OFF state to keep a PLC input partially energized or cause an indicator to glow. This can produce false ON indications or intermittent behavior.

Use the manufacturer's compatibility information and approved interface arrangements to resolve these issues.

10. Normally Open, Normally Closed, and Signal Logic

A sensor's electrical output behavior must be distinguished from the PLC program's logical interpretation.

  • Normally open (NO): The output or contact is normally non-conducting in its defined normal state, depending on device design.
  • Normally closed (NC): The output or contact is normally conducting in its defined normal state, depending on device design.
  • Logical true/false: The state interpreted by the PLC program.

A PLC tag becoming TRUE does not automatically mean that a physical object is present. The program may invert the input or interpret an active-low signal.

Example:

Physical condition Electrical input PLC interpretation
Target absent OFF Part_Present = FALSE
Target present ON Part_Present = TRUE
Target absent ON due to wiring or sensor fault Potential false detection
Target present OFF due to misalignment or wiring fault Missed detection

This table assumes a conventional non-inverted arrangement. Actual behavior must be confirmed from the electrical design and program.

10.1 Fail-safe interpretation

In machine integration, consider what happens when a wire breaks, power is lost, a sensor fails, or an input module stops communicating.

A design should not assume that every sensor's OFF state means a safe machine condition.

For safety-related functions, the required behavior must be established by the risk assessment and implemented using suitable safety-rated devices, architecture, diagnostics, and validation.

11. PLC Digital Input Modules

A PLC digital input module receives discrete electrical signals and presents their interpreted states to the controller.

Important specifications include:

  • Rated input voltage.
  • ON-state voltage and current thresholds.
  • OFF-state voltage and current thresholds.
  • Input impedance or current consumption.
  • Input delay and filtering.
  • Channel count.
  • Common-terminal arrangement.
  • Electrical isolation.
  • Diagnostic capabilities.
  • Supported wiring configurations.
  • Compatibility with sourcing or sinking field devices.

11.1 Input thresholds

A digital input does not necessarily change state at exactly zero volts or exactly its nominal supply voltage.

The module defines valid ON and OFF regions. Between these regions, the state may be undefined or unreliable.

For a typical 24 VDC system, the module datasheet specifies the acceptable voltage and current conditions. Do not apply generic thresholds to every PLC family.

11.2 Input filtering

Input filtering suppresses short-duration electrical changes that should not be interpreted as valid transitions.

However, excessive filtering can delay detection.

For high-speed signals such as encoder pulses, ordinary filtered digital inputs may be unsuitable. Use high-speed inputs, counter modules, or the interface specifically designed for the signal.

11.3 Input common

The input common establishes the electrical reference or current path for a group of input channels.

An incorrect common connection can cause:

  • Multiple inputs to remain OFF.
  • Unexpected input states.
  • Incompatibility with PNP or NPN devices.
  • Unintended current paths.
  • Misleading results during voltage measurement.

Always verify the actual input-module circuit diagram.

12. Sensor Power Supply and Protection

Industrial sensors often use 24 VDC, but the actual rated supply range must be checked for each device.

The power supply design should account for:

  • Total sensor current.
  • PLC input current where applicable.
  • Inrush current.
  • Cable voltage drop.
  • Protection against short circuits.
  • Branch protection and distribution.
  • Noise from nearby drives and switching equipment.
  • Separation between suitable power and signal wiring.
  • Environmental temperature.

12.1 Voltage drop

Cable resistance can reduce the voltage available at a remote sensor.

A simplified DC voltage-drop relationship is:

\[ V\_{\text{drop}} = I R\_{\text{loop}} \]

Where:

  • \(V\_{\text{drop}}\) is the cable voltage drop.
  • \(I\) is the current through the circuit.
  • \(R\_{\text{loop}}\) is the total resistance of the outgoing and return conductors.

The sensor voltage is approximately:

\[ V\_{\text{sensor}} = V\_{\text{supply}} - V\_{\text{drop}} \]

Ensure that the voltage at the sensor remains within its specified operating range under the actual load conditions.

12.2 Shared supply considerations

A sensor supply may also power other field devices. If one device causes a short circuit or excessive current, other sensors may lose power.

Use an appropriate distribution and protection arrangement. Separate protection can help isolate faults, provided it is designed to suit the load and supply.

13. Wiring, Connectors, and Cable Selection

Sensor wiring must be compatible with the device, environment, and signal type.

Common considerations include:

  • Conductor cross-section.
  • Cable length.
  • Connector pinout.
  • Connector IP rating when mated.
  • Oil, coolant, and chemical resistance.
  • Temperature rating.
  • Flexing and bending requirements.
  • Vibration resistance.
  • EMC performance.
  • Shielding and grounding.
  • Routing near VFD motor cables and other interference sources.

13.1 M8 and M12 connectors

Industrial sensors commonly use M8 or M12 connectors. The connector size alone does not define its pinout or signal assignment.

Check:

  • Pin count.
  • Sensor output type.
  • Connector coding.
  • Supply voltage and polarity.
  • Output pin.
  • Shield or functional-earth arrangement where applicable.
  • Mating connector compatibility.

Two sensors with the same connector size can have different pin assignments.

13.2 Cable routing

Keep sensitive sensor and analog signal wiring appropriately separated from high-noise power wiring, particularly VFD output cables, motor leads, and switching conductors.

Follow the machine's EMC design and the equipment manufacturers' requirements for routing, shielding, and bonding.

Do not apply one universal shield-termination rule to every sensor. The correct arrangement depends on signal type, cable construction, grounding architecture, and manufacturer guidance.

14. Analog Sensors and Transmitters

Analog sensors produce a signal that varies with the measured process variable.

Examples include:

  • Pressure transmitters.
  • Temperature transmitters.
  • Position sensors.
  • Flow transmitters.
  • Level transmitters.
  • Load cells with appropriate signal conditioning.

The PLC must have a compatible analog input channel or a suitable signal-conditioning interface.

14.1 Common analog interfaces

Interface Typical use Important consideration
0–10 V Voltage-based measurement Sensitive to voltage drop, grounding, and interference
1–5 V Selected instrumentation interfaces Verify input range and reference
4–20 mA Industrial process measurement Loop supply, burden, polarity, and fault interpretation
Resistance or RTD Temperature measurement Requires compatible input and wiring configuration
Thermocouple Temperature measurement Requires appropriate thermocouple input, extension wiring, and cold-junction compensation
Pulse or frequency Speed and rate measurement Requires adequate input frequency capability

14.2 Why 4–20 mA is common

A 4–20 mA signal can support transmission over long cables and provides a useful live-zero convention.

For a conventional 4–20 mA measurement:

  • 4 mA represents the configured lower-range value.
  • 20 mA represents the configured upper-range value.
  • Values outside the normal operating range may indicate an overrange, underrange, or fault condition, depending on the instrument and configuration.

The 4 mA live zero helps distinguish a valid lower-range measurement from some zero-current fault conditions. It does not guarantee that every fault will be detected.

14.3 Analog scaling

For a linear transmitter:

\[ PV = LRV + \frac{I-I\_{\min}}{I\_{\max}-I\_{\min}}(URV-LRV) \]

Where:

  • \(PV\) is the calculated process value.
  • \(LRV\) is the configured lower-range value.
  • \(URV\) is the configured upper-range value.
  • \(I\) is the measured current.
  • \(I\_{\min}\) is the signal current corresponding to the lower range.
  • \(I\_{\max}\) is the signal current corresponding to the upper range.

For a 4–20 mA transmitter configured for 0–100 bar, a measured current of 12 mA corresponds to:

\[ PV = 0 + \frac{12-4}{20-4}(100-0) \]
\[ PV = 50\ \text{bar} \]

This result assumes linear scaling and a correctly configured 4–20 mA input.

14.4 Analog troubleshooting

If the displayed process value is incorrect, check:

  • Sensor configuration and range.
  • Loop supply voltage.
  • Current-loop wiring and polarity.
  • PLC analog input configuration.
  • Raw input value.
  • Engineering-unit scaling.
  • Input channel selection.
  • Grounding and shielding.
  • Open circuit or short circuit.
  • Transmitter fault diagnostics.

Do not adjust scaling to compensate for a wiring fault without identifying the actual cause.

15. Encoder and High-Speed Sensor Signals

Encoders provide position, speed, or motion information through pulses or encoded data.

Common output types include:

  • Single-ended digital pulses.
  • Differential signals such as suitable RS-422-based interfaces.
  • Incremental A/B signals, sometimes with an index pulse.
  • Absolute position interfaces.
  • Networked encoder data.

A standard PLC digital input may not be fast enough to count all pulses accurately.

Check:

  • Maximum pulse frequency.
  • Input filter settings.
  • Counter capability.
  • Electrical interface compatibility.
  • Cable length and shielding.
  • Differential versus single-ended input requirements.
  • Encoder supply voltage.
  • Resolution and direction interpretation.

For an incremental encoder, A/B phase relationships can provide direction information when interpreted by suitable hardware or software.

For a high-speed motion application, use a high-speed counter, motion controller, or compatible drive interface where required.

16. IO-Link and Smart Sensors

IO-Link is a point-to-point communication technology used to connect compatible sensors and actuators to an IO-Link master.

It supports more than simple ON/OFF switching. Depending on the device, the system may exchange process values, parameters, identification data, and diagnostic information.

A typical arrangement is:

Smart Sensor
     |
     | IO-Link connection
     v
IO-Link Master
     |
     | Industrial network
     v
PLC
     |
     v
Machine control logic

Potential benefits include:

  • Remote parameterization.
  • Device identification.
  • Diagnostic information.
  • Access to process values.
  • Simplified replacement and parameter restoration when supported.
  • Better visibility of certain sensor faults.

IO-Link is not the same as ordinary Ethernet. The sensor connects to an IO-Link master through a compatible port, and the master communicates with the PLC using its supported network interface.

17. Safety-Related Sensors

Safety-related sensors detect conditions relevant to reducing machine risk.

Examples include:

  • Safety interlock switches.
  • Safety-rated non-contact switches.
  • Safety light curtains.
  • Safety laser scanners.
  • Two-hand control devices.
  • Safety-rated position or speed sensing arrangements.

These devices must be selected according to the machine risk assessment and the required safety function.

Important concepts include:

  • Required Performance Level (PL) under ISO 13849-1 where applicable.
  • Safety Integrity Level (SIL) under the relevant IEC 62061 or IEC 61508 framework.
  • Diagnostic coverage.
  • Fault detection.
  • Redundancy where required.
  • Common-cause failure considerations.
  • Safe-state definition.
  • Validation of the complete safety function.

A normal proximity sensor or ordinary PLC input is not automatically suitable for a safety function.

Likewise, a safety-rated sensor alone does not make the entire function safety-rated. The complete chain, including logic, outputs, actuators, diagnostics, and validation, must meet the applicable requirements.

Never bypass a safety sensor to restore production without an approved safe method and the necessary authorization.

18. Sensor Selection for Machine Integration

Selecting a sensor involves more than choosing its sensing distance.

Evaluate the following factors:

  1. Target: Material, dimensions, shape, colour, reflectivity, and surface condition.
  2. Sensing principle: Inductive, capacitive, photoelectric, ultrasonic, magnetic, mechanical, or another suitable method.
  3. Operating distance: Rated and effective sensing distance with the actual target.
  4. Response time: Required switching or measurement speed.
  5. Output interface: PNP, NPN, analog, pulse, serial, or networked.
  6. Supply voltage: Actual operating range and power requirements.
  7. PLC compatibility: Input thresholds, current, wiring topology, and channel capability.
  8. Environment: Dust, coolant, oil, moisture, vibration, temperature, and washdown conditions.
  9. Mounting: Mechanical clearance, alignment, sensing-face protection, and adjustment access.
  10. Cable and connector: Length, flexibility, pinout, and environmental suitability.
  11. Diagnostics: Output indicators, fault outputs, communication diagnostics, or teach functions.
  12. Maintainability: Replacement availability, spare strategy, standardization, and documentation.
  13. Safety requirements: Whether the device participates in a safety function.
  14. Lifecycle cost: Purchase price, replacement time, downtime risk, and availability of compatible alternatives.

18.1 Example: Part-presence sensor on a conveyor

Suppose a conveyor must detect a steel component before a pneumatic stopper is activated.

An inductive sensor may be suitable if:

  • The target is within its effective sensing distance.
  • The mounting geometry is correct.
  • The sensor is mechanically protected.
  • The output is compatible with the PLC input.
  • The sensor responds quickly enough for the conveyor speed.
  • The control sequence handles a missing or invalid detection.

The design must also account for target variation, vibration, metal chips, coolant, and the possibility that the sensor detects nearby machine metal instead of the intended part.

Good signal names describe the function clearly.

Examples:

  • Part_Present
  • Cylinder_Extended
  • Cylinder_Retracted
  • Clamp_Pressure_OK
  • Conveyor_At_Position
  • Guard_Closed
  • Oil_Temperature

For important machine conditions, document the physical meaning of ON and OFF. This avoids confusion between raw electrical states and process states.

20. Commissioning and Validation

Sensor commissioning verifies the complete sensor-to-PLC path.

20.1 Pre-power checks

  • Confirm the correct sensor model and output type.
  • Check mounting, alignment, and target clearance.
  • Verify connector pinout and cable identification.
  • Check the supply voltage and polarity.
  • Verify input-module compatibility.
  • Confirm the wiring against the schematic.
  • Check protective measures and cable routing.
  • Ensure that safety-related work follows the approved procedure.

20.2 Powered checks

With the machine in an appropriate safe condition:

  1. Verify the sensor supply voltage.
  2. Observe the sensor's output indicator where provided.
  3. Activate and deactivate the target.
  4. Measure the output using a suitable method.
  5. Observe the PLC input-channel status.
  6. Verify the corresponding PLC tag.
  7. Confirm the expected state in the control logic.
  8. Test the intended machine response under the approved commissioning procedure.
  9. Record results and any deviations.

Do not force PLC outputs or operate hazardous motion without following the machine's commissioning and safety procedures.

20.3 Acceptance criteria

A sensor is ready for service when:

  • It detects the intended target reliably.
  • Its electrical interface is compatible.
  • The PLC input changes state correctly.
  • The program interprets the signal correctly.
  • The required response time is met.
  • Environmental and mounting requirements are satisfied.
  • Fault behavior is understood and documented.
  • Required safety validation is completed where applicable.

21. Sensor Troubleshooting

Troubleshooting should distinguish between sensing problems, electrical-interface problems, PLC input problems, and logic problems.

21.1 Sensor indicator OFF

Possible causes:

  • No supply voltage.
  • Incorrect pinout.
  • Broken cable.
  • Incorrect polarity.
  • Target outside the sensing zone.
  • Wrong sensing principle for the target.
  • Sensor damage.

Checks:

  • Measure the supply voltage at the sensor.
  • Verify the connector pinout.
  • Confirm the target and mounting geometry.
  • Inspect cable and connector condition.
  • Compare the device with the manufacturer's datasheet.

21.2 Sensor indicator ON but PLC input OFF

Possible causes:

  • PNP/NPN mismatch.
  • Incorrect input common.
  • Output wired to the wrong terminal.
  • Broken return path.
  • PLC input module fault.
  • Incorrect channel configuration.
  • Output voltage or current outside the module's valid input range.

Checks:

  • Verify the actual sensor output topology.
  • Measure the output relative to the correct reference.
  • Check the input common and module wiring.
  • Verify the PLC input terminal.
  • Observe the input-channel indicator.
  • Compare measured conditions with the module thresholds.

21.3 PLC input ON but machine sequence does not proceed

Possible causes:

  • Wrong PLC tag mapping.
  • Incorrect logic condition.
  • Missing interlock or permissive.
  • Timer not complete.
  • Incorrect operating mode.
  • Another required sensor is not active.
  • A fault or safety condition prevents operation.

Checks:

  • Confirm the raw input address.
  • Verify the tag-to-address mapping.
  • Monitor the relevant program conditions.
  • Check sequence state and interlocks.
  • Review active alarms and fault diagnostics.

Do not bypass interlocks simply because the sensor appears to be working.

21.4 Intermittent sensor signal

Possible causes:

  • Marginal sensing distance.
  • Vibration or loose mounting.
  • Damaged flexing cable.
  • Connector contact problems.
  • Electrical interference.
  • Unstable supply voltage.
  • Excessive input noise.
  • Target variation.
  • Contamination or moisture.

Investigate the physical and electrical causes before increasing software filtering. Filtering may hide a symptom while leaving the underlying problem unresolved.

21.5 False detection

Possible causes:

  • Sensor detecting an unintended object.
  • Background interference in an optical arrangement.
  • Metal mounting structure affecting an inductive sensor.
  • Capacitive sensitivity set too high.
  • Leakage current from a two-wire sensor.
  • Incorrect PLC logic polarity.
  • Crosstalk or wiring faults.

Correct the cause rather than relying solely on PLC logic to mask an unreliable sensor.

22. Preventive Maintenance

Sensor reliability depends on installation quality, environmental conditions, and periodic inspection.

A maintenance plan should include:

  • Inspect sensor mounting and mechanical damage.
  • Check for loose connectors and damaged cables.
  • Remove contamination where permitted by the manufacturer.
  • Verify alignment and sensing distance.
  • Inspect sensor indicators and diagnostic status.
  • Check supply voltage where faults are suspected.
  • Review intermittent PLC input alarms.
  • Confirm that replacement sensors match the electrical interface.
  • Verify cable and connector suitability after replacements.
  • Update the spare-parts list and drawings after changes.
  • Revalidate safety-related functions after relevant maintenance.

Do not alter sensitivity, teach values, or safety parameters without following the approved procedure.

23. Practical Integration Exercise

Design the sensor-to-PLC interface for a station containing:

  • An inductive part-presence sensor.
  • A magnetic cylinder-extended sensor.
  • A cylinder-retracted sensor.
  • A pressure transmitter with a 4–20 mA output.
  • A safety interlock for an access guard.

For each device, document:

  1. Sensing principle and intended function.
  2. Supply voltage and output type.
  3. Cable and connector specification.
  4. PLC input module and channel.
  5. Terminal references and wire numbers.
  6. PLC tag and expected electrical state.
  7. Normal and abnormal conditions.
  8. Required response time.
  9. Fault diagnostics and troubleshooting method.
  10. Commissioning and acceptance tests.
  11. Spare-parts and replacement requirements.
  12. Safety classification and validation requirements where applicable.

For the guard interlock, identify the required safety function from the risk assessment. Do not treat an ordinary PLC input as a substitute for the required safety architecture.

24. Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is the role of a sensor in machine integration?

It converts a physical condition or process variable into a signal that the control system can interpret.

2. What is the difference between an inductive and a capacitive sensor?

An inductive sensor detects suitable conductive targets through electromagnetic interaction. A capacitive sensor detects changes in capacitance and can detect a broader range of materials, depending on its design.

3. What is the difference between PNP and NPN sensors?

A typical PNP output sources positive voltage when active. A typical NPN output sinks current toward 0 V when active. The PLC input must be compatible with the selected output topology.

4. What is the difference between a two-wire and three-wire DC sensor?

A three-wire sensor commonly has separate positive supply, negative supply, and output connections. A two-wire sensor uses the same two conductors for supply and switching behavior.

5. Why can a sensor indicator be ON while the PLC input remains OFF?

Possible causes include incompatible output topology, incorrect common wiring, a broken conductor, incorrect terminal mapping, or a faulty input channel.

6. Why is a 4–20 mA signal often preferred for industrial measurement?

It supports robust current-loop measurement over suitable cable lengths and provides a live-zero convention that can help distinguish a valid lower-range reading from some faults.

7. What is the purpose of PLC input filtering?

It suppresses short-duration signal changes. Excessive filtering, however, can delay detection or prevent accurate counting of fast signals.

8. Why might a sensor detect a test piece but fail on the actual component?

The actual target may have different material properties, geometry, reflectivity, orientation, or effective sensing distance.

9. What is the difference between a raw PLC input and a process tag?

The raw input represents the electrical state interpreted by the input module. A process tag represents the program's named interpretation of that signal and may include inversion or other logic.

10. Can a normal proximity sensor be used as a safety device?

Not automatically. A safety function requires suitable devices, architecture, diagnostics, and validation according to the applicable risk assessment and standards.

11. What is IO-Link?

It is a point-to-point communication technology for connecting compatible sensors and actuators to an IO-Link master, enabling process data, parameters, and diagnostics where supported.

12. What should be checked before replacing a faulty sensor?

Verify the sensing principle, supply voltage, output type, pinout, sensing distance, environmental rating, connector, and PLC input compatibility.

13. Why can long sensor cables cause problems?

They can introduce voltage drop, interference, capacitance-related effects, and signal-integrity issues depending on the cable and signal type.

14. What should be done if a sensor signal is intermittent?

Check mounting, target geometry, cables, connectors, supply stability, electrical interference, input filtering, and the sensor's operating limits before changing the control logic.

15. What is the most important rule in sensor commissioning?

Verify the complete path from physical detection through the electrical interface and PLC input to the intended machine response.

25. Final Engineering Principles

  • Select the sensor based on the real target, environment, response time, and application.
  • Verify electrical compatibility between the sensor output and PLC input.
  • Distinguish electrical states from logical process states.
  • Confirm supply voltage, polarity, pinout, and input common.
  • Use suitable input hardware for analog, high-speed, and networked signals.
  • Treat safety-related sensing as part of a complete validated safety function.
  • Diagnose the complete signal chain before replacing components.
  • Document device tags, cable IDs, wire numbers, terminal references, and PLC addresses.
  • Commission the sensor under the approved machine-safety procedure.
  • Maintain sensor configurations and as-built documentation after modifications.
