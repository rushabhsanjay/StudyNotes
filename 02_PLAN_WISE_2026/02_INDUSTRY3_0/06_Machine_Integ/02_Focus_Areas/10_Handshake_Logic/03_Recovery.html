1. Purpose of recovery in industrial automation

Recovery is the controlled process of returning a machine or integrated production system to an acceptable operating condition after a fault, interruption, timeout, emergency stop, power loss, or incomplete operation.

In industrial automation, clearing a fault does not necessarily mean the machine is ready to restart. The controller must establish the actual machine state, resolve incomplete transactions, verify required conditions, and prevent unexpected movement.

For example, if a robot loses communication with a CNC machine during part loading, the robot may not know whether the component was transferred successfully. Simply resetting the communication fault and repeating the loading command could cause a collision, duplicate operation, or damaged component.

Recovery logic must therefore answer four questions:

  • What caused the interruption?
  • What is the actual physical and control state of the machine?
  • Which actions are safe and valid from that state?
  • What conditions must be satisfied before operation can resume?

Core principle: Reset clears a defined fault or latched condition. Recovery establishes a valid operating state. Restart intentionally initiates operation. These are separate actions.

2. Reset, recovery, and restart

Action Purpose Example
Fault acknowledgement Confirms that an operator has recognized an alarm Operator acknowledges a communication alarm
Fault reset Clears a fault latch when its reset conditions are satisfied Drive fault is reset after its cause is removed
State recovery Reconciles machine state after an interruption Robot and CNC determine whether a part transfer completed
Safety reset Resets a safety-related function according to its validated design A guard-related safety function is reset after the required conditions are met
Restart Initiates or resumes a defined operating sequence Operator issues a new cycle-start command
Homing or referencing Establishes a required coordinate reference Servo axis performs its reference procedure
Reinitialization Restores software or communication state PLC rebuilds the communication interface after reconnection

These actions are not interchangeable. An alarm acknowledgement must not silently clear a fault that requires investigation. A reset must not automatically initiate hazardous motion. Homing must not be performed until its prerequisites and movement risks are understood.

3. Typical causes requiring recovery

Recovery may be needed after:

  • PLC-to-PLC communication loss.
  • Request-and-acknowledge timeout.
  • Robot or CNC fault.
  • Drive fault or loss of readiness.
  • Sensor disagreement.
  • Failed pneumatic or hydraulic movement.
  • Incomplete part transfer.
  • Interrupted automatic cycle.
  • Power failure or controller restart.
  • Safety-system activation.
  • Operator-initiated stop.
  • Incorrect recipe or product data.
  • Loss of position reference.
  • A failed quality test.
  • An interrupted tool change or fixture operation.

Different faults require different recovery paths. A drive overload, an unresolved robot position, and an uncertain part-transfer outcome should not all be handled by one generic reset routine.

4. Recovery architecture

Fault or interruption detected
             ↓
Record fault and current state
             ↓
Place equipment in the required condition
             ↓
Identify the fault and its cause
             ↓
Verify the actual physical state
             ↓
Resolve incomplete operations and transactions
             ↓
Check reset prerequisites
             ↓
Reset permitted fault conditions
             ↓
Re-establish machine and communication readiness
             ↓
Operator or authorized sequence initiates restart
             ↓
Resume from a defined, validated state

This is a conceptual architecture. The actual sequence depends on the machine, hazard assessment, equipment, and approved recovery procedure.

A recovery design should preserve enough diagnostic information to determine what happened before the fault. Clearing state variables indiscriminately may remove the evidence needed to resolve the problem.

5. Recovery state machine

A state machine makes recovery behavior explicit and prevents the program from jumping directly from a fault to automatic production.

Typical recovery states include:

State Name Purpose
0 NORMAL Normal automatic operation
80 FAULT_DETECTED Capture the fault and active sequence state
81 CONTROLLED_HOLD Maintain the required machine condition
82 DIAGNOSIS_REQUIRED Identify or verify the fault cause
83 STATE_RECONCILIATION Establish the actual state of the machine and active transaction
84 RESET_READY Confirm that reset prerequisites are satisfied
85 WAIT_RESTART Wait for the permitted restart action
86 REINITIALIZE Restore required controller, device, or communication state
90 RECOVERY_FAULT Handle unsuccessful or incomplete recovery

A conceptual flow is:

NORMAL
   ↓ Fault
FAULT_DETECTED
   ↓
CONTROLLED_HOLD
   ↓
DIAGNOSIS_REQUIRED
   ↓ Cause resolved
STATE_RECONCILIATION
   ↓ State confirmed
RESET_READY
   ↓ Reset permitted
WAIT_RESTART
   ↓ Restart command
REINITIALIZE / VALIDATED RESUME
   ↓ Conditions satisfied
NORMAL

Not every machine needs all these states. The important requirement is that each transition has defined conditions and that an unresolved fault cannot be bypassed by an ordinary start command.

6. Fault latching and fault clearing

A fault latch preserves a detected fault until the specified clearing conditions are met.

A simple example is a handshake timeout:

IF RequestActive
   AND NOT Acknowledge
   AND AckTimer.Q THEN

    AckTimeoutFault := TRUE;
END_IF;

In a real PLC application, the fault would usually be integrated into a structured state machine with a consistent timer invocation, event capture, and reset strategy.

The fault should not be cleared merely because Acknowledge becomes true after the timeout. That late response may belong to the interrupted transaction or may indicate that the original operation is already underway.

A reset condition might require:

  • The initiating cause has been removed.
  • Relevant devices report valid status.
  • Communication is healthy.
  • The active transaction has been resolved.
  • Required process conditions are satisfied.
  • The controller is in an allowed recovery state.
  • The operator has performed the specified reset action, if required.

Fault clearing should be deliberate and traceable.

7. Physical-state verification

A PLC's remembered sequence state may not match the actual physical condition after an interruption.

For example, the PLC may have recorded STEP_30 = LOAD_PART, but power loss occurred while the robot was moving. After restart, the robot's actual position, the part location, and the fixture state must be checked before the sequence resumes.

Typical physical checks include:

  • Part presence at the source, gripper, fixture, and destination.
  • Robot position and motion state.
  • CNC door and fixture state.
  • Clamp and cylinder sensor states.
  • Conveyor position and workpiece location.
  • Drive readiness and axis reference status.
  • Tool and workpiece identity.
  • Safety-system status and required operating mode.
  • Pneumatic and hydraulic conditions.

A sensor signal alone may not prove that the physical situation is correct. Use the appropriate combination of sensors, controller diagnostics, inspection, and validated procedures.

8. Recovery after communication loss

Suppose Machine A sends Transfer_Request to Machine B. Communication fails before Machine A receives the acknowledgement.

When communication returns, Machine A must not assume that Machine B never received the request.

A robust recovery sequence should:

  1. Detect that communication is healthy again.
  2. Validate the received data and connection status.
  3. Determine whether the previous transaction is still active.
  4. Read or reconcile the receiving machine's transaction state where supported.
  5. Determine whether the operation was not started, is in progress, completed, or has an uncertain outcome.
  6. Resolve any incomplete transfer or processing action.
  7. Clear the old transaction only according to the interface contract.
  8. Permit a new request after both machines reach the agreed state.

A transaction identifier, sequence number, or explicit status exchange can help distinguish an old transaction from a new one.

If the outcome cannot be established reliably, the system should remain in a defined recovery condition until the uncertainty is resolved. Repeating the last command blindly is not a recovery strategy.

9. Recovery after a timeout

A timeout indicates that an expected event did not occur within its permitted time.

The correct recovery depends on which event timed out.

Timeout Investigation Possible recovery
Acknowledgement timeout Check receiver state, communication, and request validity Reconcile transaction state and retry only if permitted
Operation timeout Check the physical process and device diagnostics Resolve the mechanical or process cause before resuming
Sensor-transition timeout Inspect sensor, actuator, wiring, and supply conditions Restore the defined initial or safe condition
Drive-ready timeout Inspect drive fault status, enable conditions, and control configuration Correct the cause and follow the approved drive reset procedure
Communication timeout Check connection diagnostics and data validity Re-establish communication and reconcile pending transactions
Completion timeout Determine whether the operation is still active or already completed Confirm physical and transaction state before clearing the fault

A late response should be handled explicitly. The controller must determine whether it belongs to the old transaction and whether the corresponding operation is still valid.

10. Recovery after a robot or CNC interruption

Robot and CNC integration introduces additional state concerns because the physical position and active operation matter.

A recovery procedure may need to verify:

  • Robot program and current execution state.
  • Robot mastering or position-reference validity where applicable.
  • CNC cycle state and active program.
  • Tool, workpiece, and fixture conditions.
  • Gripper status and part retention.
  • Shared-zone occupancy.
  • Door and access conditions.
  • Active machine-to-machine handshake states.
  • Whether a part is partially loaded, clamped, machined, or unloaded.

Do not assume that resuming a robot program from its last recorded instruction is equivalent to safely resuming the physical operation. The interrupted instruction may have only partially executed.

A machine builder's approved recovery procedure should define which steps may be resumed, which must be repeated, and which require manual inspection or a controlled return to a known state.

11. Recovery after drive or servo faults

Drive faults may invalidate assumptions about motion, readiness, or position.

Before restarting, determine:

  • The actual drive fault and its cause.
  • Whether the axis is stationary.
  • Whether a load can move under gravity or stored energy.
  • Whether a brake is applied and functioning as required.
  • Whether the position reference remains valid.
  • Whether the drive's safety-related functions are active.
  • Whether the axis needs a reference or homing procedure.
  • Whether the machine can safely resume the interrupted motion.

Resetting a drive fault does not necessarily restore a valid coordinate reference or guarantee that the axis is ready for automatic operation.

Do not command homing, jogging, or automatic movement without verifying the applicable interlocks, operating mode, access conditions, and manufacturer instructions.

12. Recovery after a safety-system event

Safety-related recovery must follow the validated safety design.

Typical requirements include:

  • The cause of the safety demand has been removed or appropriately addressed.
  • The relevant protective devices and safety system satisfy their reset conditions.
  • The hazard area is in the required condition.
  • The reset action occurs at the specified location and under the defined conditions.
  • Reset does not itself initiate hazardous movement.
  • A separate start action is performed where required.
  • The machine's normal control system does not bypass the safety-related conditions.

An emergency-stop reset does not automatically authorize restarting production. Guard closure does not necessarily prove that the area is clear. A standard PLC bit such as Safety_OK must not substitute for the required safety-related function.

Where hazardous energy remains, the approved isolation and maintenance procedures must be followed. Recovery logic is not a substitute for lockout/tagout or equivalent energy-control requirements.

13. Automatic recovery versus operator-assisted recovery

Not every fault should be recovered automatically.

Condition Automatic recovery consideration Operator involvement
Temporary communication interruption May be appropriate if transaction state can be verified Required if state remains uncertain
Non-hazardous transient device fault Depends on manufacturer guidance and fault behavior May be required by the fault specification
Part-transfer timeout Only if physical state and transaction outcome can be confirmed Often needed if part location is uncertain
Drive fault Depends on the fault type and manufacturer requirements Required when investigation or manual intervention is specified
Robot position uncertainty Automatic recovery only through an approved validated method Often required to verify position and workspace conditions
Safety-system demand Follow the validated safety reset and restart sequence Reset and restart permissions depend on the design
Failed quality test A controlled re-test may be allowed by the quality process Required if disposition or traceability rules demand it
Unknown machine state after power loss Automatic recovery only when state reconstruction is reliable Required when uncertainty cannot be resolved automatically

Automatic recovery should have explicit limits, maximum attempts where appropriate, diagnostic logging, and a defined fallback state.

Repeatedly resetting a fault until production resumes can hide an underlying problem and create more serious failures.

14. PLC programming principles for recovery

A maintainable recovery implementation should separate several responsibilities:

  • Fault detection.
  • Fault recording and latching.
  • Physical-state verification.
  • Transaction reconciliation.
  • Reset-permission logic.
  • Restart-permission logic.
  • Device initialization.
  • Sequence resumption.
  • Recovery-failure handling.

Conceptual logic:

CASE RecoveryState OF

    80: (* FAULT DETECTED *)
        RecordFault := TRUE;
        RecoveryState := 81;

    81: (* VERIFY CONDITIONS *)
        IF FaultCauseRemoved
           AND DevicesStatusValid
           AND CommHealthy THEN

            RecoveryState := 83;
        END_IF;

    83: (* RECONCILE STATE *)
        IF PhysicalStateConfirmed
           AND TransactionResolved THEN

            RecoveryState := 84;
        END_IF;

    84: (* RESET READY *)
        IF ResetCommand
           AND ResetPermitted THEN

            RecoveryState := 85;
        END_IF;

    85: (* WAIT FOR RESTART *)
        IF RestartCommand
           AND RestartPermitted THEN

            RecoveryState := 86;
        END_IF;

    86: (* REINITIALIZE *)
        IF InitializationComplete
           AND AllRequiredConditionsValid THEN

            RecoveryState := 0;
        END_IF;

END_CASE;

This is an instructional skeleton, not a complete production program. It omits platform-specific initialization, fault latching, timeout handling, state retention, edge detection, reset sequencing, and detailed transitions for failed recovery.

The real implementation must also define what happens when any prerequisite becomes false during recovery. It must not progress simply because a single Boolean signal is true.

15. Preventing unexpected restart

Unexpected restart is a major recovery-design concern.

Common causes include:

  • Power restoration automatically enabling a previous run command.
  • A retained start bit remaining true.
  • A communication partner restoring an old command.
  • An HMI command being held active.
  • A sequence resuming from a stale step number.
  • Reset logic automatically generating a start condition.
  • A drive becoming ready while the PLC still has an active motion command.

Design measures may include:

  • Requiring a new intentional start command after the defined recovery conditions are satisfied.
  • Clearing or validating stale commands.
  • Reinitializing handshake states.
  • Checking the actual machine state before resuming.
  • Separating reset and restart permissions.
  • Verifying the required safety conditions through the validated safety system.
  • Preventing automatic continuation where the outcome of an interrupted operation is uncertain.

The exact restart behavior must be defined through the risk assessment, control-system design, and applicable standards.

16. Recovery diagnostics and event logging

A recovery system should provide enough information for a technician to identify why the machine stopped and what remains unresolved.

Useful records include:

Record Purpose
Fault identifier Identifies the detected condition
Fault timestamp Establishes when the event occurred
Sequence state Shows which operation was active
Expected signal Identifies the missing response or condition
Actual signal state Helps identify the discrepancy
Communication status Distinguishes link failure from process-state issues
Transaction identifier Links the fault to the relevant operation
Reset event Records when the fault was reset
Restart event Records when operation was intentionally resumed
Recovery result Indicates successful recovery or continued fault
Operator or maintenance record Supports traceability where required

Retain the first fault and the sequence of relevant events. A cascade of secondary alarms can obscure the original cause if the diagnostic design records only the latest message.

17. Commissioning and recovery testing checklist

Fault handling

  • Verify each relevant fault is detected and recorded.
  • Confirm the machine enters the required condition.
  • Verify that fault information remains available for diagnosis.
  • Test reset conditions and fault-clear behavior.
  • Confirm that a late response does not silently clear an unresolved fault.

State reconciliation

  • Test communication loss during each important handshake phase.
  • Test power interruption during an active operation where the approved test plan permits it.
  • Verify physical state after interrupted movement.
  • Confirm part location and transaction status before resuming.
  • Verify stale commands and retained states are handled correctly.
  • Confirm uncertain operations are not repeated automatically.

Reset and restart

  • Verify reset does not itself initiate hazardous motion.
  • Verify restart requires the defined conditions and intentional command.
  • Confirm safety-related reset and restart behavior matches the validated design.
  • Test failed recovery prerequisites.
  • Confirm maximum retry behavior and recovery-failure handling.
  • Verify diagnostic logs and event timestamps.

Documentation

  • Maintain fault and recovery-state definitions.
  • Document operator and maintenance recovery procedures.
  • Record approved device-specific reset procedures.
  • Document transaction-reconciliation rules.
  • Record commissioning results and approved program versions.

18. Interview questions and answers

1. What is machine recovery?

Recovery is the controlled process of restoring a valid operating state after a fault or interruption, including verification of physical state, resolution of incomplete operations, and satisfaction of restart conditions.

2. What is the difference between reset and restart?

Reset clears a defined fault or latched condition when permitted. Restart initiates or resumes operation after all required conditions are satisfied.

3. Why should recovery use a state machine?

A state machine makes recovery steps explicit, prevents invalid transitions, and provides a clear way to diagnose where recovery has stopped.

4. What should happen if communication returns after a transfer timeout?

The controllers should validate communication, reconcile the active transaction, and establish the actual physical state before deciding whether to resume or repeat the operation.

5. Why is physical-state verification necessary?

The recorded PLC sequence state may not match the actual robot, fixture, axis, or workpiece position after an interruption.

6. Can every fault be reset automatically?

No. Some faults require physical inspection, operator intervention, a controlled homing procedure, or a manufacturer-specific recovery procedure.

7. Why must reset and restart be separate?

Separating them prevents clearing a fault from inadvertently initiating machine movement and provides a deliberate point to verify operating conditions.

8. What is transaction reconciliation?

It is the process of determining whether an interrupted operation was not started, remains active, completed, or has an uncertain outcome.

9. What is the danger of retaining a start command through a power interruption?

The machine may begin moving unexpectedly when power or communication returns if the retained command is accepted without the required restart checks.

10. How should recovery be tested?

Test relevant faults at different sequence stages, verify the required machine condition, confirm reset and restart rules, and ensure incomplete transactions cannot be repeated or skipped incorrectly.

19. Key engineering principles

  • Separate fault acknowledgement, fault reset, recovery, and restart.
  • Capture the fault and active sequence state before clearing information.
  • Verify the physical machine condition, not only PLC memory.
  • Reconcile incomplete transactions after communication loss.
  • Use fault-specific recovery paths.
  • Prevent stale commands and acknowledgements from triggering unintended actions.
  • Require deliberate restart after the specified prerequisites are satisfied.
  • Follow validated safety-system procedures for safety-related events.
  • Define limits for automatic recovery and retries.
  • Record recovery results for troubleshooting and maintenance.

Reliable recovery means returning to a known, verified state rather than merely making the alarm disappear. That distinction protects equipment, product quality, production traceability, and people.
