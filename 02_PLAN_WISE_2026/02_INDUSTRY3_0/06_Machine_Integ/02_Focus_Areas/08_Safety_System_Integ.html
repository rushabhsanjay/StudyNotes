1. Purpose of safety-system integration

Safety-system integration ensures that machines, robots, CNCs, conveyors, fixtures, drives, and operators are protected through coordinated safety functions.

In an integrated production system, hazards rarely belong to only one machine. A robot may load a CNC, a conveyor may transfer a component between stations, and a fixture may clamp the part while an operator accesses another area. A safety event in one area can affect several connected machines.

Safety-system integration establishes how these systems detect hazardous conditions, stop or prevent hazardous motion, coordinate safety states, and permit controlled recovery.

The main objectives are:

  • Prevent or reduce exposure to identified hazards.
  • Ensure emergency stops and protective devices perform their intended functions.
  • Coordinate safety-related behavior between connected machines.
  • Prevent unexpected startup or restart after a safety event.
  • Ensure that faults in safety-related control components are detected or controlled as required by the design.
  • Establish clear reset, recovery, and restart procedures.
  • Verify that the integrated system meets the required safety performance.

Critical principle: Standard PLC logic, ordinary network communication, HMI indications, and normal machine interlocks must not be treated as safety-rated functions unless the complete implementation has been specifically designed, validated, and approved for that purpose.

2. What is a machine safety system?

A machine safety system is the combination of components and logic responsible for implementing defined safety functions.

Typical components include:

  • Emergency-stop devices.
  • Guard-door switches and interlocks.
  • Light curtains and safety laser scanners.
  • Safety mats and pressure-sensitive devices.
  • Safety relays.
  • Safety PLCs or safety controllers.
  • Safety-rated input and output modules.
  • Safety contactors and feedback circuits.
  • Safe drive functions.
  • Safety communication networks.
  • Safety-related wiring, diagnostics, and configuration.

The safety system must be designed around the hazards and the required risk reduction. Adding a safety relay or a safety PLC does not automatically make the complete machine safe.

The standard PLC may receive a status indicating that a safety circuit is healthy or that a protective device is active. That status is useful for sequencing and diagnostics, but the safety function must not depend on an ordinary PLC bit or a non-safety-rated network message when the risk assessment requires a safety-rated control path.

4. Essential safety terminology

Term Meaning Engineering significance
Hazard A potential source of injury or harm Identifies what must be controlled
Risk assessment Evaluation of hazards, exposure, and possible harm Determines required risk reduction
Safety function A defined function that reduces a specified risk Must have explicit triggering conditions and required behavior
PL Performance Level under ISO 13849-1 Expresses the required or achieved capability of a safety-related control system
SIL Safety Integrity Level under IEC 62061 and related standards Specifies integrity requirements within the applicable functional-safety framework
SRP/CS Safety-related parts of control systems Covers control-system components involved in implementing safety functions
Safe state The defined state that reduces the relevant hazard Must be determined for the specific machine and hazard
Reset Action that permits a safety function or system to leave a latched condition when its requirements are satisfied Must not itself initiate hazardous motion
Restart Action that starts or resumes machine operation Must follow the defined operating and safety conditions
Validation Evidence that the implemented safety functions meet their requirements Confirms the complete design, installation, and behavior

PL and SIL are related concepts but are not interchangeable labels. Use the applicable standard and assessment method for the system being designed.

5. Risk assessment and safety-function specification

Safety integration begins with identifying hazards, not selecting hardware.

Typical hazards include:

  • Robot movement inside a shared workspace.
  • Unexpected CNC axis or spindle motion.
  • Conveyor movement during operator access.
  • Crushing or trapping at fixtures and clamps.
  • Ejection of tools, workpieces, or machine components.
  • Unexpected restart after a power interruption.
  • Stored pneumatic, hydraulic, electrical, or mechanical energy.
  • Access to hazardous areas during automatic operation.
  • Collision between coordinated machines.
  • Maintenance work performed while hazardous energy remains available.

A safety-function specification should define:

  1. The hazard being addressed.
  2. The event or condition that activates the safety function.
  3. The required response.
  4. The time or stopping-performance requirement, where applicable.
  5. The safe state or acceptable risk-reduction state.
  6. The conditions required before reset.
  7. The conditions required before restart.
  8. The required performance level or integrity level.
  9. The devices and logic implementing the function.
  10. The tests needed to validate the function.

The specification must be derived from the risk assessment and applicable standards, not copied blindly from another machine.

6. Emergency-stop integration

An emergency stop is intended to respond to an emergency by bringing the machine or relevant equipment to a defined safe condition.

In an integrated cell, an emergency-stop event may need to affect more than the machine where the button is installed.

For example, if a robot transfers parts into a CNC, the risk assessment may require coordinated stopping of the robot and CNC when a particular emergency-stop device is activated.

The required response depends on the machine layout, hazards, operating modes, and applicable standards.

Important design considerations

  • Define the scope of every emergency-stop device.
  • Determine which machines and hazardous movements must respond.
  • Use the appropriate safety-rated control architecture.
  • Verify stopping behavior and stopping time where required.
  • Ensure that resetting an emergency stop does not automatically restart hazardous motion.
  • Identify any stored energy that remains hazardous after stopping.
  • Validate the complete response across connected machines.

Emergency-stop functions are supplementary protective measures. They do not replace guarding, interlocking, safe operating procedures, or energy isolation for maintenance.

7. Stop categories and safe stopping

IEC 60204-1 defines stop categories relevant to machine electrical equipment.

Stop category General behavior Typical consideration
Category 0 Stop by immediate removal of power to the machine actuators, with uncontrolled stopping where applicable Motion may coast or stop through mechanical effects
Category 1 Controlled stop with power available during the stopping process, followed by removal of power to the actuators Allows a controlled deceleration before power removal
Category 2 Controlled stop with power remaining available to the actuators Not equivalent to a safety-rated removal of torque

The appropriate category must be determined from the hazard analysis and machine design. The category number does not, by itself, establish the required PL or SIL.

A safe stop must account for actual stopping behavior. Removing motor torque may not immediately stop a high-inertia axis, vertical load, spindle, or rotating tool.

8. Guard doors and access interlocks

Guard interlocks prevent or control access to hazardous areas.

Common devices include:

  • Mechanical safety switches.
  • Coded non-contact safety switches.
  • Guard-locking interlocks.
  • Trapped-key systems.
  • Safety-rated position monitoring.

A guard interlock may initiate a stop when a door opens. A guard-locking system may keep the door locked until hazardous motion has stopped or another defined safe condition is met.

Guard integration requirements

  • Determine whether simple interlocking is sufficient or guard locking is required.
  • Assess bypass and defeat risks.
  • Confirm that opening the guard produces the specified response.
  • Verify that closing the guard does not automatically restart hazardous motion unless the complete design explicitly permits the intended behavior and meets applicable requirements.
  • Verify guard-lock release conditions.
  • Test the complete stopping and access sequence.
  • Provide a documented method for trapped-person and recovery scenarios where applicable.

A standard PLC indication such as Door_Closed = TRUE is not a substitute for the required safety-rated guard-interlocking function.

9. Light curtains and safety laser scanners

These devices detect entry into a protected area using different sensing principles.

Device Typical use Main considerations
Safety light curtain Protecting access to a machine opening Resolution, reach-through, reach-over, mounting distance, and stopping time
Safety laser scanner Monitoring a defined floor area or protective field Field configuration, approach direction, response time, environmental conditions, and mounting
Safety mat Detecting presence on a protected floor area Coverage, damage, placement, and the defined safety response
Area-scanning safety device Monitoring access to a hazardous zone Protective field geometry, response time, and validated configuration

Safety distance

Protective-device placement must account for how far a person can travel toward the hazard during the total response and stopping time.

A simplified relationship is:

\[ S = K \times T + C \]

Where:

  • \(S\) is the required separation distance.
  • \(K\) is an approach-speed parameter defined by the applicable standard and scenario.
  • \(T\) is the total response and stopping time.
  • \(C\) is an additional distance term defined by the applicable standard and protective-device geometry.

This is a conceptual form, not a universal installation formula. The correct calculation, units, parameters, and assumptions must follow the applicable standard and the specific protective-device arrangement.

The total time may include sensor response, safety-controller processing, communication delay, output response, drive response, and mechanical stopping time, as applicable.

10. Safety PLC versus standard PLC

A standard PLC is generally designed for normal machine control. A safety PLC is designed and certified for defined safety-related applications when implemented within its specified limits.

Feature Standard PLC Safety PLC
Primary purpose Machine sequencing and process control Safety-related control functions
Typical logic Normal control logic Safety application logic
Fault detection Depends on standard controller design Safety mechanisms designed to meet specified integrity requirements
Safety certification Not normally certified for safety functions Certified for specified safety applications and conditions
Safety I/O Standard I/O unless specifically rated Safety-rated I/O as supported by the system
Validation Normal functional verification Safety validation plus applicable functional verification
Use in safety function Only where specifically justified and compliant with the required design Used within its certified limits and validated architecture

A safety PLC does not make every connected signal or output safety-rated. The complete safety function, including sensors, logic, outputs, communication, actuators, configuration, and diagnostics, must meet the required design and validation criteria.

11. Safety communication between machines

Integrated machines may exchange safety information using safety-rated communication mechanisms.

Examples include supported implementations of:

  • PROFIsafe.
  • CIP Safety.
  • FSoE, or Safety over EtherCAT.
  • Other certified safety communication mechanisms supported by the equipment.

These technologies must be supported by the actual controllers, devices, firmware, configuration, and system architecture.

Ordinary PROFINET, EtherNet/IP, EtherCAT, TCP/IP, or other standard communication does not become safety-rated merely because it carries a Boolean variable named Safety_OK.

Safety communication mechanisms incorporate defined measures for detecting relevant communication faults, but they still require correct configuration, compatible equipment, and validation.

Typical safety-related exchange

Safety controller A
       ↓
Safety-rated communication
       ↓
Safety controller B
       ↓
Validated safety logic
       ↓
Defined safe response

The exact architecture may be peer-to-peer, controller-to-device, or based on a supported safety network arrangement.

12. Shared safety zones and machine coordination

A production cell may contain multiple safety zones, each protecting a different area or machine.

For example:

  • Robot operating zone.
  • CNC loading zone.
  • Conveyor transfer zone.
  • Operator loading zone.
  • Tool-change or maintenance zone.
  • Shared robot-and-machine workspace.

The integration must define which events affect each zone and how connected machines respond.

A zone matrix is useful for documenting the required behavior.

Event Robot CNC Conveyor Operator access
Robot-zone guard opens Defined safety response Response depends on the risk assessment Response depends on shared hazards Access governed by guard design
CNC access guard opens Stop or inhibit if required by shared hazards Defined safety response Response depends on shared hazards Access governed by guard design
Shared emergency stop activates Defined coordinated response Defined coordinated response Defined coordinated response Hazardous motion stopped as specified
Safety communication fails Defined safe response Defined safe response where affected Defined safe response where affected Access remains governed by the safety design
Maintenance isolation applied Must remain in the required safe condition Must remain in the required safe condition Must remain in the required safe condition Authorized maintenance procedure applies

This table is only a planning example. The actual response for each event must come from the documented safety-function specification.

Do not assume that all machines must always stop together. Conversely, do not allow one machine to continue if doing so creates a hazard for a person or another machine.

13. Reset, acknowledgement, and restart

These actions must be treated as separate functions.

  • Acknowledgement: Confirms that an alarm or event has been recognized.
  • Reset: Clears or releases a defined latched condition when its reset requirements are met.
  • Restart: Initiates or resumes machine operation.

A safe sequence typically requires:

  1. The initiating condition has been removed.
  2. The protective devices and safety system satisfy their defined conditions.
  3. The operator has an appropriate view of the hazard area, or the design uses another validated means of ensuring safety.
  4. Reset is performed at the intended location and under the specified conditions.
  5. The reset does not itself initiate hazardous movement.
  6. A separate, intentional start command is issued when required.
  7. The machine starts only when all relevant operating and safety conditions are satisfied.

A reset button should not be used as a shortcut to restart a machine. The design must account for blind spots, multiple access points, trapped-person hazards, and remote restart risks.

14. Safe drive functions

Modern drives may support safety functions that control motor-related hazards.

Common examples include:

Function Meaning Engineering consideration
STO Safe Torque Off Prevents the drive from producing torque as specified by its safety design
SS1 Safe Stop 1 Initiates a controlled stop followed by the specified safe torque-off behavior
SS2 Safe Stop 2 Initiates a controlled stop followed by the specified safe operating state
SLS Safely-Limited Speed Keeps speed within a defined safety limit under the specified conditions
SOS Safe Operating Stop Monitors a defined standstill condition while the applicable drive safety function remains active
SBC Safe Brake Control Controls a supported brake-related safety function
Safe direction or position functions Monitors defined movement constraints Requires compatible drive functions and correct application configuration

These functions are available only on supported equipment and are subject to manufacturer-specific implementation limits.

STO does not necessarily stop motion immediately. A motor or load may continue moving because of inertia, gravity, stored energy, or external forces. A vertical axis may require a properly designed brake and additional protective measures.

The selected function must match the hazard and the risk assessment.

15. Pneumatic and hydraulic safety integration

Electrical safety control does not automatically eliminate hazards from pneumatic or hydraulic energy.

Potential hazards include:

  • Unexpected cylinder movement.
  • Pressure retained in an isolated section.
  • Gravity-driven motion.
  • Accumulator energy.
  • Clamps releasing unexpectedly.
  • Loads moving after motor torque is removed.
  • Trapped pressure downstream of a valve.

The design may require monitored valves, pressure monitoring, safe exhaust or isolation arrangements, mechanical restraint, or other protective measures.

For each actuator, determine:

  • The hazardous motion or stored energy.
  • The required response to the safety demand.
  • Whether pressure should be retained, exhausted, or isolated.
  • Whether a load may fall or move after pressure changes.
  • Whether the actuator requires mechanical support.
  • How the safe state is verified.
  • How energy is isolated for maintenance.

The correct behavior is application-specific. Simply de-energizing a solenoid is not proof that the pneumatic or hydraulic system is safe.

16. Safety-related status versus standard status

Safety controllers may provide diagnostic or status information to the standard PLC and HMI.

Examples include:

  • Guard open.
  • Emergency stop active.
  • Safety system not ready.
  • Safe drive function active.
  • Safety communication fault.
  • Reset required.

These indications help operators and maintenance personnel understand why operation is inhibited.

However, the standard PLC must not replace the safety system's required response by evaluating an ordinary status bit and then attempting to perform the safety function itself.

A useful separation is:

Safety devices
       ↓
Safety controller and safety logic
       ↓
Safety-rated outputs / safe drive functions
       ↓
Required safety response

Safety controller status
       ↓
Standard PLC diagnostics
       ↓
HMI / alarms / maintenance information

The standard PLC may use the status to prevent normal sequence progression, but the validated safety function must operate independently of any non-safety-rated dependency that would invalidate the safety design.

17. Safety diagnostics and fault handling

Safety systems may detect faults such as:

  • Channel discrepancy.
  • Short circuit or cross fault.
  • Input-device failure.
  • Output-device fault.
  • Contactor feedback mismatch.
  • Safety communication timeout.
  • Configuration or signature mismatch.
  • Drive safety fault.
  • Failure to achieve the expected safe condition.

A diagnostic should identify the fault source and the required recovery procedure.

Troubleshooting must not begin by bypassing the affected safety channel. Instead:

  1. Place the machine in the required safe condition.
  2. Identify the reported diagnostic and affected safety function.
  3. Inspect the device, wiring, connectors, and power supply.
  4. Verify input and output states using the approved diagnostic tools.
  5. Check controller configuration and device compatibility.
  6. Investigate feedback discrepancies and communication faults.
  7. Correct the cause and perform the prescribed functional test.
  8. Document the result and restore the system through the approved process.

If a safety fault cannot be resolved, the machine must remain in the condition required by its safety design.

18. Safety validation and proof testing

Safety validation confirms that the complete safety functions meet their specified requirements in the actual installed system.

Validation should cover the full chain from initiating device to final response.

Safety demand
     ↓
Sensor or protective device
     ↓
Safety input and logic
     ↓
Safety communication, where applicable
     ↓
Safety output
     ↓
Actuator or safe drive function
     ↓
Measured or verified response

Validation activities may include:

  • Verifying the safety-function specification.
  • Confirming hardware and software configuration.
  • Checking wiring and device installation.
  • Testing each relevant safety demand.
  • Measuring stopping time where required.
  • Verifying guard-lock release behavior.
  • Testing reset and restart behavior.
  • Checking safety communication fault responses.
  • Verifying actuator and feedback behavior.
  • Confirming that modifications have not invalidated the safety design.
  • Recording results, deviations, and corrective actions.

A successful PLC online test is not sufficient evidence that a safety function is validated.

19. Standards and compliance framework

ISO 13849-2 Validation of safety-related control systems
IEC 62061 Functional safety of machinery safety-related control systems
IEC 60204-1 Electrical equipment of machines

The applicable editions and local legal requirements must be checked for each project. A standards list does not establish compliance by itself.

20. Common integration failures

Symptom Possible cause Required investigation
Machine does not restart after a guard closes Reset condition not satisfied or restart intentionally requires operator action Inspect the documented reset and restart sequence
Safety PLC reports communication fault Incorrect configuration, incompatible devices, network issue, or timeout Inspect safety-network diagnostics and configuration
One machine stops but another continues Incorrect zone coordination or intentionally independent safety functions Verify the safety-function and zone matrix
STO activates but motion continues briefly Mechanical inertia or stored energy Verify stopping behavior and required protective measures
Safety relay will not reset Open input channel, discrepancy, feedback fault, or reset conditions unmet Inspect device status, wiring, and feedback circuit
Guard opens but hazardous motion persists too long Stopping time or protective-device placement is inadequate Measure and assess the complete stopping response
HMI reports safety fault but safety controller shows healthy Incorrect mapping, stale data, or diagnostic interpretation error Trace the status path and communication validity
Safety system trips intermittently Wiring faults, vibration, environmental conditions, device faults, or communication problems Use diagnostic logs and controlled testing
Machine restarts unexpectedly after power recovery Incorrect restart behavior or inadequate restart prevention Investigate the validated control design immediately
Hydraulic actuator moves after electrical power is removed Stored pressure, gravity, or unsuitable valve behavior Assess energy isolation and actuator-specific safeguards

21. Commissioning checklist

Design and documentation

  • Complete the applicable risk assessment.
  • Define each safety function and its required response.
  • Determine required PL or SIL using the applicable methodology.
  • Document safety zones and interactions between machines.
  • Verify the selected safety devices and controller compatibility.
  • Document reset, restart, recovery, and energy-isolation requirements.

Installation and configuration

  • Verify safety-device placement and mounting.
  • Check wiring, connectors, and approved electrical diagrams.
  • Verify safety input/output configuration.
  • Confirm safety communication configuration where applicable.
  • Verify drive safety parameters and supported functions.
  • Record approved safety-controller and device configurations.

Functional validation

  • Test emergency-stop devices and their defined scope.
  • Test guards, guard locking, and protective devices.
  • Verify the required response across connected machines.
  • Confirm that reset does not itself initiate hazardous motion.
  • Test restart prevention after safety demands and power recovery.
  • Verify stopping behavior and time where required.
  • Test relevant safety communication and device faults.
  • Verify pneumatic, hydraulic, and stored-energy behavior.
  • Record the results and resolve deviations before release.

Maintenance and change control

  • Establish approved periodic inspection and testing requirements.
  • Maintain safety-related backups and configuration records.
  • Document modifications and their impact on validation.
  • Ensure maintenance personnel follow the approved isolation procedures.
  • Revalidate affected safety functions after relevant changes.

22. Interview questions and answers

1. What is safety-system integration?

It is the coordinated design and implementation of safety functions across connected machines, controllers, protective devices, drives, and actuators.

2. What is the difference between a standard PLC and a safety PLC?

A standard PLC handles normal control, while a safety PLC is designed and certified for specified safety-related applications. The complete implementation must still meet its required safety performance and be validated.

3. Can a normal PLC bit be used as an emergency-stop signal?

A normal PLC bit must not be relied upon to implement a safety function unless the complete architecture is specifically designed and validated to meet the applicable requirements. Standard logic alone is not a substitute for a safety-rated control path.

4. What is the difference between reset and restart?

Reset clears or releases a defined latched condition when its requirements are met. Restart initiates or resumes machine operation. Reset must not inadvertently cause hazardous movement.

5. What is the difference between PL and SIL?

PL is a performance-level framework under ISO 13849, while SIL is a safety-integrity framework used under IEC 62061 and other applicable functional-safety standards. The required method depends on the applicable design framework.

6. Does STO stop a motor immediately?

No. STO prevents torque generation according to its specified function, but inertia, gravity, or stored energy may continue to produce motion.

7. Why is safety-zone coordination important?

An event in one zone can create hazards in another. The system must coordinate the required response based on the risk assessment rather than assuming all machines are independent.

8. Why must a safety function be validated after integration?

Individual components may work correctly while the complete system still contains configuration errors, unsuitable timing, incorrect zone logic, or unexpected interactions.

9. What is the purpose of safety-rated communication?

It enables safety-related information to be exchanged using a supported and certified communication mechanism with defined fault-detection measures. The complete configuration must still be correct and validated.

10. What should be done when a safety channel fault occurs?

Keep the machine in the required safe condition, diagnose the actual cause, repair the fault, and perform the prescribed functional test. Do not bypass the safety channel to restore production.

11. Why is stopping time important for light curtains and safety scanners?

The protective device must be positioned so that a person cannot reach the hazard before the machine reaches the required safe condition. The assessment must use the applicable calculation method and measured or justified response times.

12. Does closing a guard mean the machine may automatically restart?

Not automatically. Restart behavior must follow the machine's risk assessment, applicable requirements, and validated control design. Guard closure should not be treated as universal permission to restart.

23. Key engineering principles

  • Design safety functions from hazards and risk assessment.
  • Specify the required behavior of each safety function.
  • Distinguish standard control communication from safety-rated communication.
  • Coordinate safety responses across robots, CNCs, conveyors, fixtures, and shared zones.
  • Separate alarm acknowledgement, safety reset, and machine restart.
  • Account for inertia, gravity, pneumatic pressure, hydraulic pressure, and stored energy.
  • Verify the entire sensor-to-actuator safety chain.
  • Validate the integrated machine after installation and relevant modifications.
  • Maintain configuration records, test evidence, and controlled recovery procedures.
  • Never bypass a safety device or force a safety status bit to restore production.

Safety-system integration is complete only when the combined machine behaves as specified under normal operation, safety demands, faults, recovery, and restart conditions.
