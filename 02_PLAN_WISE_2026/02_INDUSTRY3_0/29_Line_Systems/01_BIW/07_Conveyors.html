1. Purpose and Role of Conveyors in BIW

A conveyor is a mechanical system used to transport bodies, sheet-metal assemblies, pallets, fixtures or components between production operations. In Body-in-White (BIW) manufacturing, conveyors connect welding stations, robots, fixtures, inspection points, buffers and transfer systems into a coordinated production line.

A conveyor is not merely a motor and a belt or chain. It is a complete machine system containing mechanical structures, drive components, sensors, controls, safety devices and interfaces with upstream and downstream equipment.

Typical BIW conveyor functions include:

  • Transporting body shells between welding stations.
  • Moving pallets and carriers carrying components.
  • Positioning a body accurately for robot or fixture operations.
  • Accumulating work-in-progress between stations.
  • Synchronizing transfers with production cycles.
  • Supporting model or variant routing.
  • Preventing collisions between moving carriers.
  • Reporting equipment status, faults and production counts.
  • Supporting controlled stops, recovery and maintenance.

The key distinction is that transporting a part and accurately positioning it for a process are different functions. A conveyor may move a body to a station, while a separate locating and clamping system establishes the final process position.

3. Main Conveyor Types Used in Industry

BIW plants may use several conveyor technologies within one production system. The correct technology depends on the load, path, floor layout, required positioning accuracy, cycle time, environment and maintenance requirements.

3.1 Chain conveyors

Chain conveyors move loads using one or more driven chains. The load may rest directly on the chain arrangement or travel on a carrier attached to the chain.

Typical applications include heavy pallets, body carriers and industrial transfer systems.

Advantages:

  • Suitable for many heavy-duty applications.
  • Can be designed for long production routes.
  • Can support robust carrier movement.
  • May integrate with stops, lifters and transfer units.

Limitations and failure modes:

  • Chain elongation and wear.
  • Sprocket wear or misalignment.
  • Lubrication requirements.
  • Debris accumulation.
  • High mechanical loads during jams.
  • Noise and vibration from wear or poor alignment.

3.2 Roller conveyors

Roller conveyors use powered or gravity-driven rollers to move pallets or suitable loads.

Powered rollers may use individual motors, belts, chains or other transmission arrangements. Gravity rollers rely on a slope and suitable load characteristics.

Typical applications include pallet movement, accumulation and transfers between process areas.

Key maintenance points include roller bearings, shaft condition, drive belts or chains, alignment, accumulation logic and the condition of the load-contact surfaces.

3.3 Belt conveyors

Belt conveyors transport material on a continuous belt supported by rollers or a bed.

They are common for suitable lighter loads, components and logistics applications. Heavy BIW body transfer requires a system specifically designed for the relevant load and process.

Common faults include belt tracking problems, belt damage, insufficient tension, pulley wear, material buildup and drive overload.

3.4 Skid and pallet conveyors

Skid or pallet conveyors move a carrier that supports the body or component. The carrier provides a standardized interface with the conveyor and process stations.

The skid or pallet may include:

  • Locating features.
  • Part-support structures.
  • Identification tags.
  • Clamping or retention features.
  • Reference surfaces.
  • Model-specific tooling.

The carrier becomes part of the production system. Damage or dimensional wear on its locating features can affect process quality even when the conveyor itself moves correctly.

3.5 Overhead conveyors

Overhead conveyors transport loads along a route above the production floor. They can reduce floor-space usage and support transfer between production areas.

Maintenance considerations include suspended-load security, track condition, hanger integrity, drive components, clearances, lubrication and the risks associated with loads above people or equipment.

3.6 Lift-and-carry and transfer systems

Lift-and-carry systems raise a load, move it to another position and lower it. Transfer systems may use transverse conveyors, shuttle units, lifters, pushers or other mechanisms to move loads between lines or parallel paths.

These systems frequently combine several actuators and position sensors. Their sequence must ensure that the load is supported and that the transfer path is clear before movement begins.

3.7 Skillet and power-and-free systems

A skillet system typically transports a vehicle body or carrier on a platform that may also support work at an ergonomic height or in a defined orientation, depending on the design.

A power-and-free conveyor uses a powered mechanism to move carriers while allowing designed accumulation or independent carrier control through its track and engagement arrangement.

Actual operating principles vary by design. Maintenance staff should use the equipment drawings and manufacturer documentation rather than assuming that every system with the same name has identical mechanics.

4. Conveyor Mechanical Components

Understanding the mechanical load path is essential for diagnosing conveyor faults. A motor can run normally while a damaged coupling, gearbox, chain, bearing or transfer mechanism prevents the load from moving correctly.

4.1 Motor, gearbox and transmission

The motor converts electrical energy into rotational motion. A gearbox changes the speed and torque available at the output shaft. Couplings, shafts, sprockets, chains, belts or rollers transmit that motion to the conveyor.

For an idealized gearbox:

\[ n\_{\text{out}} = \frac{n\_{\text{motor}}}{i} \]

Where \(i\) is the gearbox reduction ratio.

For example, a motor running at 1,500 rpm with a reduction ratio of 30:1 gives an ideal output speed of 50 rpm, before considering actual operating effects.

Approximate output torque is:

\[ T\_{\text{out}} \approx T\_{\text{in}} \times i \times \eta \]

Where \(\eta\) is the transmission efficiency.

A higher reduction ratio generally reduces output speed while increasing available output torque, subject to the motor, gearbox and transmission ratings.

4.2 Chains and sprockets

Inspect for:

  • Chain elongation and uneven wear.
  • Damaged links, rollers or connecting elements.
  • Sprocket tooth wear.
  • Incorrect chain tension.
  • Poor lubrication where lubrication is required.
  • Misalignment between sprockets.
  • Abnormal vibration, noise or temperature.
  • Debris or foreign objects trapped in the mechanism.

Excessive tension is not a valid universal remedy for chain problems. Incorrect tension can increase bearing loads, accelerate wear and damage the drive system.

4.3 Bearings and rollers

Common symptoms include noise, vibration, excessive temperature, rough rotation, increased drive current and localized mechanical resistance.

Check the specified lubrication method, bearing condition, shaft alignment, mounting and contamination. A seized roller may cause a local transport fault even when the main drive appears healthy.

4.4 Stops, lifters and transfer mechanisms

Stops control carrier movement at defined points. Lifters raise or lower carriers. Transfer mechanisms move loads between conveyor paths or levels.

These mechanisms may use pneumatic cylinders, hydraulic actuators, electric actuators or servo-controlled axes.

Their diagnostic checks should include:

  • Mechanical obstruction.
  • Actuator travel and end-position feedback.
  • Air or hydraulic pressure where applicable.
  • Valve or drive status.
  • Mounting and alignment.
  • Carrier support and retention.
  • Sequence interlocks.
  • Clearance from neighboring equipment.

A sensor reporting the expected actuator position does not, by itself, prove that the load is correctly supported or positioned.

5. Conveyor Motors and Drive Control

Conveyors can use direct-on-line motors, reversing starters, soft starters, variable-frequency drives (VFDs), servo drives or other drive arrangements.

5.1 Direct-on-line motor control

A direct-on-line starter applies the supply to the motor through its switching and protection arrangement. Depending on the application, it may provide simple fixed-speed movement.

Important components may include a contactor, overload protection, short-circuit protection and associated control wiring.

Typical faults include overload trips, supply issues, contactor problems, motor winding faults and mechanical overload.

5.2 VFD-controlled conveyors

A VFD varies the frequency and voltage supplied to a suitable motor to control speed and support controlled acceleration and deceleration.

Common parameters and signals include:

  • Run command.
  • Forward or reverse command, where supported.
  • Speed reference.
  • Actual output frequency or speed estimate.
  • Drive-ready status.
  • Running status.
  • Fault status and fault code.
  • Acceleration and deceleration settings.
  • Current, torque or load information where available.

5.3 Common drive faults

Symptom Possible causes Diagnostic focus
Motor does not start Missing command, permissive, supply or drive-ready state Review PLC command, drive status and electrical supply
Motor trips during movement Excessive load, obstruction, acceleration demand or motor/drive issue Inspect mechanics and drive diagnostics
Conveyor moves too slowly Incorrect reference, parameter setting, load condition or mechanical resistance Compare command, feedback and mechanical condition
Conveyor stops unexpectedly Sequence interlock, drive fault, communication issue or protective action Identify the first fault and its timestamp
Motor runs but load does not move Coupling, chain, belt, gearbox or transmission failure Inspect the mechanical power path under safe conditions
Direction is incorrect Incorrect direction command or drive configuration Verify the approved design and wiring/configuration

Do not increase the current limit, torque limit or overload setting merely to keep a jammed conveyor running. First determine why the load demand has increased.

6. Conveyor Sensors and Position Feedback

Conveyors depend on sensors to detect load presence, carrier position, movement, transfer completion and equipment status.

6.1 Common sensors

Sensor or device Typical purpose
Inductive proximity sensor Detects a suitable metal target or carrier feature
Photoelectric sensor Detects a target through optical sensing
Mechanical limit switch Detects a mechanically actuated position
Magnetic cylinder sensor Detects a suitable magnetic piston position
Encoder Measures rotary or linear movement through the applicable feedback arrangement
RFID reader Identifies a tagged carrier or pallet
Barcode reader Reads a label or marked identifier
Pressure switch or transmitter Monitors pneumatic or hydraulic pressure
Motor feedback device Provides speed or position feedback when fitted

6.2 Sensor placement

A sensor should be positioned to detect the intended physical condition, not merely a convenient nearby metal surface.

For example, a carrier-position sensor should reliably detect the intended carrier feature under normal tolerances, vibration and operating conditions. Its mounting must also protect it from impact, debris and cable damage.

6.3 Typical conveyor signals

  • CONVEYOR_RUN_CMD
  • CONVEYOR_RUNNING
  • CONVEYOR_FAULT
  • CARRIER_PRESENT
  • POSITION_REACHED
  • TRANSFER_READY
  • TRANSFER_COMPLETE
  • DRIVE_READY
  • DOWNSTREAM_READY

These are illustrative tag names. Actual tag naming and signal polarity depend on the installed controls.

7. Conveyor PLC Sequence and Interlocks

The PLC coordinates the conveyor with upstream and downstream machines, positioning equipment, identification systems and production requirements.

A typical sequence may involve:

  1. Confirm the conveyor and required equipment are available.
  2. Confirm the intended transfer path is clear.
  3. Confirm that the receiving station can accept the load.
  4. Start the conveyor or transfer mechanism.
  5. Monitor movement and relevant feedback.
  6. Stop or position the carrier at the intended location.
  7. Confirm the required position or transfer state.
  8. Complete the handoff and update the sequence state.
  9. Report faults or timeouts if the expected transition does not occur.

The actual sequence may be more complex and can include multiple drives, transfer devices and coordinated motion.

7.1 Upstream and downstream coordination

A conveyor should not transfer a carrier merely because the upstream machine has completed its operation. The receiving equipment must also be ready under the designed control conditions.

A simplified handshake might include:

Upstream Transfer Request
        ↓
Downstream Ready Confirmation
        ↓
Transfer Path and Interlocks Verified
        ↓
Conveyor / Transfer Motion Enabled
        ↓
Carrier Reaches Required Position
        ↓
Position Feedback Confirmed
        ↓
Transfer Complete
        ↓
Both Stations Update Sequence State

7.2 Interlocks versus safety functions

A normal PLC interlock may prevent motion when a downstream station is not ready, a positioning device is not retracted or a carrier is not correctly identified.

A safety function addresses an identified hazard and must be implemented using the required safety-related architecture.

A normal PLC permissive is not automatically a safety function. The control design must clearly distinguish process sequencing from safety-related protection.

8. Conveyor Positioning, Stopping and Accuracy

A conveyor may need to stop a carrier within a defined position tolerance so that a fixture, robot, welding gun or inspection system can perform its operation.

Positioning accuracy depends on the complete system, including the conveyor drive, mechanical transmission, carrier, sensors, stopping method and final locating mechanism.

8.1 Factors affecting stopping position

  • Conveyor speed.
  • Load mass and distribution.
  • Drive and braking characteristics.
  • Mechanical backlash and compliance.
  • Chain, belt or roller wear.
  • Sensor location and repeatability.
  • PLC and communication delays.
  • Carrier geometry and mechanical stops.
  • Brake condition, where fitted.
  • Variation in friction and operating conditions.

A simplified stopping-distance relationship for constant deceleration is:

\[ d = \frac{v^2}{2a} \]

Where \(v\) is the initial speed and \(a\) is the magnitude of deceleration.

This relationship is an idealized model. Real stopping distance may also include control delay, drive response, load variation and mechanical behavior.

8.2 Conveyor stop versus precision location

The conveyor may bring a carrier into a stopping zone, after which locating pins, stops, lifters or clamps establish the final position.

This separation is important in BIW because robot paths and welding quality may require repeatable geometry beyond what a general transport mechanism can guarantee.

Do not compensate for a worn locating mechanism by repeatedly changing conveyor stop parameters without investigating the source of the positional error.

9. Conveyor Capacity, Throughput and Buffering

Conveyor design must support the required production rate while accounting for transfer time, accumulation, station cycle time and downtime.

9.1 Cycle time and throughput

For a simple system where one carrier completes a transfer every \(t\) seconds, the theoretical transfer rate is:

\[ Q = \frac{3600}{t} \]

Where \(Q\) is the theoretical number of transfers per hour and \(t\) is the transfer interval in seconds.

For a 20-second transfer interval:

\[ Q = \frac{3600}{20} = 180 \text{ transfers/hour} \]

This is only the theoretical rate for that transfer interval. Actual production throughput may be lower because of station cycle time, downtime, blocked transfers, faults, changeovers and quality holds.

9.2 Buffer zones

A buffer temporarily holds carriers or work-in-progress between operations. It can help isolate short-duration cycle variations and reduce the impact of brief stoppages.

However, a buffer cannot compensate indefinitely for a downstream process whose average capacity is lower than the upstream production rate.

9.3 Accumulation control

Accumulation logic must consider:

  • Carrier presence in each zone.
  • Whether the downstream zone is occupied.
  • Whether the next station can accept a carrier.
  • Carrier spacing and collision prevention.
  • Transfer-device position.
  • Product identity and routing where required.
  • Recovery behavior after a fault.
  • Any safety-related separation or protective requirements.

The exact behavior depends on the conveyor architecture. Do not assume every conveyor allows carriers to accumulate safely against one another.

10. Common Conveyor Faults and Troubleshooting

10.1 Fault matrix

Symptom Possible causes Diagnostic approach
Conveyor does not start Missing permissive, drive fault, safety condition or command issue Review sequence state, drive diagnostics and safety status through approved procedures
Motor runs but carrier does not move Broken transmission, chain or belt failure, coupling damage or jam Inspect the mechanical power path under safe isolation
Conveyor stops at the wrong position Sensor displacement, braking variation, drive setting or mechanical wear Compare commanded position, feedback and actual stopping point
Carrier is detected but does not transfer Downstream not ready, transfer interlock missing or sequence fault Review handshake states and the first unsatisfied condition
Repeated overload trips Mechanical resistance, excess load, poor alignment or drive/motor problem Check load conditions, current trends and mechanical movement
Abnormal chain noise Poor lubrication, wear, misalignment or damaged links Inspect chain and sprockets under the approved maintenance procedure
Conveyor moves intermittently Loose wiring, unstable input, drive fault or mechanical binding Correlate timestamps with PLC, drive and sensor diagnostics
Carrier collision or near-collision Faulty zone logic, incorrect position feedback or failed coordination Investigate the sequence, sensor states and event history before restarting
Transfer lift fails to extend Low pressure, actuator fault, obstruction, valve problem or missing permissive Verify the approved actuator sequence and relevant diagnostics
Conveyor faults after maintenance Incorrect sensor mounting, disturbed wiring, wrong parameter or mechanical misalignment Compare the restored condition with drawings and approved settings

10.2 Troubleshooting procedure

  1. Establish the safe condition. Determine whether motion is ongoing or stored energy is present. Follow the site-specific isolation and access procedure before entering hazardous areas.
  2. Capture the fault. Record the alarm, time, station, carrier and operating mode.
  3. Identify the first abnormal event. Later alarms may be consequences of the original fault.
  4. Check the control state. Determine whether the PLC issued a run or transfer command and whether the required permissives were satisfied.
  5. Check drive and electrical diagnostics. Review drive status, supply condition and relevant fault records.
  6. Check sensors and feedback. Compare actual physical conditions with the sensor states and PLC tags.
  7. Inspect mechanical components. Under the approved isolation procedure, examine the transmission, carrier, stops, rollers, bearings and obstructions.
  8. Correct the confirmed cause. Avoid speculative parameter changes and unnecessary part replacement.
  9. Test the repair. Verify motion, stopping position, interlocks and handoff behavior using the approved procedure.
  10. Document the result. Record the cause, action, verification and any follow-up maintenance required.

10.3 First-out fault analysis

A conveyor stop can generate many alarms in a short time. For example, the conveyor stops, the carrier never reaches its position sensor, the transfer sequence times out and the downstream station reports a missing carrier.

The missing-position alarm may be a consequence rather than the root cause.

Use event timestamps, PLC sequence states and drive diagnostics to identify the first abnormal event. This often reduces unnecessary component replacement and repeated restarts.

11. Conveyor Preventive Maintenance

Conveyor maintenance must follow the manufacturer's requirements and the plant's approved maintenance plan. The appropriate interval depends on duty cycle, load, environment, design and fault history.

11.1 Mechanical inspection

  • Inspect frames, anchors, supports and guards for damage or looseness.
  • Inspect chains, belts, sprockets, rollers and couplings.
  • Check chain tension and wear using the manufacturer's criteria.
  • Inspect gearbox condition, lubrication and leakage.
  • Check bearings, shafts and drive mounting.
  • Inspect carriers, skids, pallets and locating features.
  • Remove accumulated debris using the approved method.
  • Check stops, lifters, transfer mechanisms and mechanical limits.
  • Inspect abnormal noise, vibration, heat and movement.
  • Verify mechanical alignment where required.

11.2 Electrical and automation inspection

  • Check motor, drive and control-panel diagnostics.
  • Inspect cables, connectors and electrical enclosures.
  • Verify sensor mounting and detection.
  • Check encoder or position-feedback diagnostics where fitted.
  • Review recurring PLC alarms and timeouts.
  • Verify relevant network connections and remote I/O health.
  • Confirm approved drive parameters and configuration backups.
  • Verify conveyor-to-station handshake behavior.
  • Confirm the status of required safety devices through approved tests.
  • Record changes, defects and corrective actions.

11.3 Condition-based maintenance indicators

Indicator Potential meaning
Increasing motor current for comparable loads Rising friction, overload or mechanical deterioration
Increasing gearbox or bearing temperature Lubrication, load, alignment or component issue
Growing chain elongation Wear and possible loss of correct engagement
More frequent stopping-position variation Sensor, brake, drive, transmission or locating issue
Repeated transfer timeouts Mechanical resistance, sensor faults or sequence instability
Increasing vibration Looseness, imbalance, misalignment or bearing deterioration
Repeated carrier identification failures Tag or reader damage, mounting change or read-zone issue

These indicators are diagnostic clues, not definitive diagnoses. Compare like-for-like operating conditions and confirm the actual cause.

12. Conveyor Safety and Hazard Control

Conveyors introduce hazards from moving chains, rollers, belts, carriers, transfer mechanisms, suspended loads and stored energy. The risk depends on the actual equipment design and operating conditions.

12.1 Common hazards

  • Entanglement at chains, sprockets, rollers and rotating shafts.
  • Crushing between carriers and fixed structures.
  • Shearing or trapping at transfer units, lifters and stops.
  • Unexpected startup during fault recovery or maintenance.
  • Stored pneumatic or hydraulic energy.
  • Gravity-driven movement or falling suspended loads.
  • Electrical hazards from drives and power circuits.
  • Unexpected movement caused by automatic sequences or remote commands.
  • Robot or adjacent machine movement during conveyor intervention.
  • Pinch points created by moving skids, pallets or fixtures.

12.2 Essential safety practices

  • Identify the relevant hazards and follow the approved risk controls.
  • Use guards and protective devices as designed.
  • Apply the site's lockout/tagout or equivalent energy-isolation procedure before hazardous maintenance.
  • Isolate electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic and other relevant energy sources.
  • Release or safely restrain stored energy according to the approved procedure.
  • Secure loads and mechanisms that could move under gravity.
  • Verify the safe condition before entering the hazardous area.
  • Use designated access and recovery procedures.
  • Never rely solely on an HMI stop command or a standard PLC status bit as proof of isolation.
  • Never bypass safety devices to recover production.
  • Verify the required protective functions after maintenance before returning equipment to service.

A stop command is not the same as energy isolation. Some drives, actuators and transfer mechanisms can move unexpectedly if their energy sources or stored energy are not controlled.

12.3 Emergency stop and safe torque off

An emergency-stop function is intended to bring equipment to a defined safe state in response to an emergency, according to the system design. It does not necessarily disconnect every energy source.

Safe Torque Off (STO) is a drive safety function that prevents the drive from producing torque as specified by its design. STO does not inherently provide electrical isolation, mechanically restrain a load or guarantee that a gravity-loaded mechanism cannot move.

The required safety behavior depends on the risk assessment and safety design. Maintenance must follow the approved procedure rather than assuming that one safety function covers every hazard.

13. Conveyor Changeover and Carrier Management

BIW lines may handle different body variants, carrier types and production routes. Changeover can involve fixture adjustments, carrier identification, mechanical guides, sensor positions or control parameters.

13.1 Changeover checks

  • Confirm the intended product variant and carrier type.
  • Verify that the carrier and tooling are compatible with the station.
  • Check that required locating features and mechanical stops are in the correct configuration.
  • Verify sensor positions and target compatibility.
  • Confirm identification and routing data.
  • Verify approved PLC or drive settings where a change is required.
  • Confirm downstream readiness and the correct transfer path.
  • Complete the prescribed functional checks before production release.

13.2 Carrier condition

A damaged carrier can produce repeated problems across multiple stations. Important checks include:

  • Carrier frame distortion.
  • Worn locating pins and rest surfaces.
  • Damaged wheels, rollers or chain engagement points.
  • Loose identification tags or brackets.
  • Bent sensor targets.
  • Incorrect or missing retention features.
  • Contamination affecting locating or transfer surfaces.

Carrier-related defects should be tracked by carrier identity where possible. This helps distinguish a station fault from a recurring problem associated with a particular carrier.

14. Conveyor Performance and Reliability Metrics

Conveyor performance should be assessed using measures that relate to production requirements and equipment reliability.

Metric Meaning
Availability Proportion of the defined time the equipment is available for its intended function
Transfer cycle time Time required for a defined transfer cycle
Throughput Number of accepted units transferred or processed per unit of time
Mean time between failures (MTBF) Average operating time between defined failures
Mean time to repair (MTTR) Average time required to restore function after a defined failure
Position repeatability Consistency of achieved position across repeated cycles
Jam frequency Frequency of defined obstruction or movement-related events
No-transfer frequency Frequency of expected transfers that do not complete
Fault recurrence Frequency with which a previously observed failure returns

Metric definitions must be consistent. For example, downtime attributed to the conveyor should be distinguished from downtime caused by a downstream station that is not ready.

14.1 Example reliability analysis

Suppose a conveyor experiences six defined failures over 120 operating hours. If those operating hours and failure definitions are consistent, the estimated MTBF is:

\[ MTBF = \frac{120}{6} = 20\text{ operating hours} \]

If the total repair time for those failures is three hours:

\[ MTTR = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5\text{ hours} \]

These values are only meaningful when the data uses consistent definitions and accurately recorded operating and repair times.

14.2 Using data for maintenance decisions

Review:

  • Repeated drive trips under similar loads.
  • Rising transfer timeouts.
  • Position errors by station or carrier.
  • Breakdown duration and recurring causes.
  • Sensor and communication fault patterns.
  • Mechanical wear against inspection history.
  • Spare-part consumption and replacement frequency.

The objective is to identify and eliminate recurring causes, not simply to accumulate alarm counts.

15. Conveyor Spares and Obsolescence

Critical conveyor spares should be selected based on failure consequences, replacement lead time, installed population, commonality and repair options.

Potential critical spares include:

  • Approved motor and gearbox assemblies.
  • Drive units and compatible control components.
  • Sensors and encoder devices.
  • Chain links, connecting elements and sprockets.
  • Bearings, rollers, belts and couplings.
  • Pneumatic valves and compatible actuators.
  • Transfer mechanism components.
  • Remote I/O and communication modules.
  • Approved cables, connectors and identification devices.

15.1 Spare-part control

For each critical spare, document:

  • Manufacturer and exact part number.
  • Installed machine and equipment tag.
  • Quantity installed and known commonality.
  • Approved replacement or successor.
  • Required mechanical and electrical compatibility.
  • Configuration or parameter requirements.
  • Stock quantity and lead time.
  • Repairability and vendor details.
  • Obsolescence and migration status.

A replacement drive or motor should not be considered compatible merely because its rating or physical size appears similar. Verify the electrical, mechanical, control and safety requirements.

16. Conveyor Fault Case Studies

Case A: Motor runs, but the carrier remains stationary

Possible causes: Failed coupling, broken chain, slipping transmission, gearbox damage or a mechanical obstruction.

Investigation: Confirm the fault state, isolate the equipment as required, and inspect the drive-to-load power path. Determine whether the motor's rotation reaches the intended conveyor mechanism.

Corrective action: Repair the confirmed failure, inspect associated components for secondary damage and perform the approved functional test.

Case B: Carrier repeatedly stops short of the station

Possible causes: Position-sensor movement, inconsistent braking, mechanical resistance, drive settings or worn locating components.

Investigation: Compare the expected stopping point with actual carrier position and relevant feedback. Check whether the error is consistent, load-dependent or associated with a specific carrier.

Corrective action: Correct the identified sensor, mechanical, drive or locating issue. Validate repeatability using the defined acceptance criteria.

Case C: Conveyor stops because the next station is not ready

Possible causes: Downstream cycle incomplete, fixture not in the required position, missing confirmation, communication failure or a legitimate process hold.

Investigation: Check the transfer request, downstream-ready signal, required permissives and sequence state. Determine whether the downstream condition is genuinely unmet or its signal is incorrect.

Corrective action: Resolve the actual downstream or communication fault. Do not remove the interlock merely to make the conveyor move.

Case D: Overload trip occurs repeatedly after maintenance

Possible causes: Incorrect chain tension, misalignment, a trapped obstruction, incorrect drive settings or a disturbed mechanical connection.

Investigation: Compare the post-maintenance configuration with approved documentation, review drive diagnostics and inspect the mechanical load path under safe isolation.

Corrective action: Restore the approved mechanical and electrical condition, then verify operation at the intended load. Do not repeatedly reset the drive without investigating the reason for the overload.

Case E: Conveyor appears mechanically healthy but transfer times out

Possible causes: Sensor not detecting the carrier, incorrect PLC mapping, communication delay, an incomplete handshake or a sequence condition that remains false.

Investigation: Trace the transfer request and feedback through the physical sensor, PLC input, logic and receiving station. Identify the first signal or state that fails to transition as expected.

Corrective action: Correct the confirmed signal or sequence fault and verify the full handoff, including recovery behavior.

18. Practical Study Exercise

Choose one conveyor or transfer mechanism from a BIW station and prepare a technical record.

  • Identify the conveyor type and its purpose.
  • Trace the mechanical power path from motor to load.
  • Identify the motor, gearbox, drive and transmission components.
  • Record the relevant sensors, position feedback and PLC tags.
  • Document the start conditions and required interlocks.
  • Trace the transfer handshake with upstream and downstream equipment.
  • Identify the expected stopping or positioning method.
  • Record common drive, sensor and mechanical faults.
  • Review the approved energy-isolation procedure and relevant hazards.
  • Identify critical spares and configuration backups.
  • Prepare a preventive maintenance checklist.
  • Document one historical breakdown using symptom, evidence, root cause, corrective action and verification.
  • Define how the conveyor is functionally tested before returning to production.

Use the actual installed equipment, drawings, PLC program, drive documentation and maintenance records. Do not alter production parameters or test safety functions outside the approved procedure.

19. Technical Review Questions

Q1. What is the difference between a conveyor and a fixture?

A conveyor transports a load. A fixture locates, supports and restrains a component for a process. A BIW station may use both.

Q2. Why might a conveyor motor run while the carrier remains stationary?

The mechanical transmission may have failed or become disconnected, or an obstruction may prevent movement.

Q3. Why can a conveyor stop in the wrong position?

Possible causes include sensor displacement, braking variation, mechanical wear, transmission backlash or incorrect drive configuration.

Q4. What is the purpose of a downstream-ready signal?

It indicates that the receiving equipment has reached the defined condition required for the intended transfer. It does not replace other required interlocks or safety functions.

Q5. Why should the first abnormal event be identified during troubleshooting?

Subsequent alarms may be consequences of the original fault. Finding the first abnormal event helps focus the investigation on the actual cause.

Q6. Why is a buffer useful, and what are its limits?

A buffer absorbs short-term production variations, but it cannot indefinitely compensate for a downstream process with insufficient average capacity.

Q7. What is the difference between conveyor stopping and precision positioning?

Stopping brings the load to a target region. A separate locating system may establish the final repeatable position needed for robot operations or welding.

Q8. Why should overload settings not be increased without investigation?

An overload may indicate a jam, excessive friction, misalignment or another mechanical or electrical fault. Increasing limits can damage equipment or create additional hazards.

Q9. What is MTBF?

Mean time between failures is the average operating time between defined failure events, calculated from consistently recorded operating time and failure data.

Q10. Why must safety isolation be considered before mechanical inspection?

Conveyors can move unexpectedly because of electrical power, stored pneumatic or hydraulic energy, gravity or automatic control commands. The approved procedure must establish a safe condition before hazardous intervention.

20. Final Acceptance Checklist

  • BIW conveyor purpose and architecture understood.
  • Chain, roller, belt, skid, overhead and transfer systems distinguished.
  • Motor, gearbox, drive and mechanical transmission understood.
  • Conveyor sensors and position feedback covered.
  • PLC sequencing and station handshakes understood.
  • Stopping distance, positioning and repeatability covered.
  • Throughput, cycle time and buffering understood.
  • Common faults and systematic troubleshooting covered.
  • Preventive and condition-based maintenance documented.
  • Conveyor hazards, isolation and safety functions understood.
  • Carrier management and changeover requirements covered.
  • Reliability metrics and fault trends understood.
  • Critical spares and obsolescence requirements documented.
  • Machine knowledge base structure prepared.
  • Practical study and functional acceptance procedure defined.

Core principle: Conveyor reliability depends on the complete system: mechanical condition, drive performance, sensor feedback, PLC sequencing, upstream/downstream coordination and safety controls. Diagnose the failed stage using evidence, correct the root cause and verify the complete transfer before releasing the equipment.
