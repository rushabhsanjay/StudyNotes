1. Purpose of a nutrunner in BIW

A nutrunner is a powered fastening tool or system used to tighten threaded fasteners to specified process requirements. In automotive manufacturing, controlled fastening is important because joints must provide the required clamping force without damaging threads, distorting components, or creating an unreliable assembly.

In a Body-in-White (BIW) line, nutrunners may be used for bolted joints on fixtures, subassemblies, tooling, brackets, and other components, depending on the station design. Other automotive assembly operations may use them for joints that directly affect vehicle quality and safety.

A nutrunner system may control torque, angle, rundown behavior, fastening sequence, and other process parameters. Advanced systems record each fastening result against a part or vehicle identifier.

Critical distinction: achieving a target torque does not automatically prove that a joint has the correct clamp load or is acceptable. Friction, lubrication, thread condition, fastener geometry, joint stiffness, and the tightening method all affect the result.

2. Nutrunner system architecture

A typical controlled fastening system consists of a tool, controller, power supply, socket or spindle arrangement, PLC interface, and quality-data interface.

Fastener and joint
        ↓
Socket / spindle / gearbox
        ↓
Nutrunner motor and torque measurement
        ↓
Nutrunner controller
        ↓
PLC handshake and fastening sequence
        ↓
Result evaluation
        ↓
Pass / Fail / Rework / Station hold
        ↓

Depending on the installation, the system may use a handheld electric tool, a fixed spindle, a multi-spindle tightening unit, a pneumatic tool, or a servo-controlled fastening head.

5. Tightening fundamentals

5.1 Torque

Torque is the rotational moment applied to a fastener.

\[ T=F \times r \]

Where:

  • \(T\) is torque in newton-metres (N·m).
  • \(F\) is the applied force in newtons (N).
  • \(r\) is the perpendicular moment arm in metres (m).

For a rotating tool, torque measurement may be performed by a transducer or estimated through a tool-specific method. The displayed value must be interpreted according to the equipment's measurement method and specifications.

5.2 Clamp load and preload

Tightening stretches the fastener and compresses the joint, creating a clamping force commonly called preload or clamp load.

A simplified engineering relationship is:

\[ T=KFd \]

Where:

  • \(T\) is tightening torque.
  • \(K\) is the nut factor, representing several friction-related effects.
  • \(F\) is the resulting preload.
  • \(d\) is a representative nominal fastener diameter.

This relationship is an approximation, not a universal calculation for production release. The nut factor can vary substantially with lubrication, coatings, surface condition, and thread geometry.

5.3 Why torque alone is insufficient

Two joints tightened to the same torque can develop different clamp loads because of differences in friction at the threads and under the fastener head or bearing surface.

Possible consequences include:

  • Insufficient clamp load and joint loosening.
  • Excessive preload and fastener yielding.
  • Thread stripping.
  • Joint distortion.
  • Damage to the fastener or mating components.
  • Inconsistent product quality despite apparently acceptable torque readings.

For critical joints, the tightening method and acceptance criteria must be established through engineering validation and the approved fastening specification.

A fastening program may combine multiple stages and acceptance windows. The correct strategy is joint-specific and should not be changed by maintenance personnel without the required engineering authorization.

7. Torque, angle, rundown and seating

A tightening cycle is not just a motor spinning until it reaches a number. The controller evaluates the fastening process from initial engagement through final tightening.

7.1 Typical fastening phases

  1. Fastener engagement: the fastener starts threading into the mating component.
  2. Free rundown: the fastener rotates with relatively low resistance before the joint surfaces contact.
  3. Seating: the fastener head or bearing surface contacts the joint.
  4. Clamp-load buildup: additional rotation stretches the fastener and increases joint compression.
  5. Final tightening: the programmed termination criteria are reached.
  6. Result evaluation: the controller checks the measured parameters against the approved limits.
  7. Result transmission: the controller reports completion and the result to the PLC or quality system.

The precise curve shape depends on fastener type, thread engagement, joint stiffness, lubrication, and the tightening strategy.

7.2 Torque-angle curve

A torque-angle curve plots torque against angular rotation during tightening.

Torque
  ↑
  │                         Final tightening
  │                       /
  │                    __/
  │                 __/
  │              __/  Clamp-load buildup
  │_____________/
  │ Free rundown   Seating
  └────────────────────────→ Angle

This is a conceptual illustration. Actual curves may look very different, and the transition between rundown and seating is not always sharp.

Curve monitoring can help identify:

  • Cross-threading.
  • Missing or incorrect fasteners.
  • Excessive rundown resistance.
  • Incomplete seating.
  • Stripped threads.
  • Abnormal joint stiffness.
  • Unexpectedly low or high tightening angle.

Not every defect produces a unique curve. Detection capability must be established by testing known good and defective joints.

8. Fastener and joint engineering

8.1 Fastener terminology

Term Meaning
Nominal diameter Designated fastener size, such as M8 or M10
Pitch Distance between adjacent thread crests
Thread engagement Length or extent of mating thread contact
Preload Tensile force developed in the fastener during tightening
Clamp load Compressive force holding the joint components together
Proof load Specified load a fastener must withstand under the applicable standard
Yield strength Stress level at which permanent deformation begins under the applicable material definition
Joint stiffness Resistance of the assembled joint to elastic deformation
Bearing surface Surface under the fastener head or nut that transmits clamping force
Prevailing torque Resistance to rotation provided by a specified locking feature
Reuse limit Engineering-defined restriction on reuse of a fastener

Fastener reuse, thread lubrication, replacement requirements, and locking features must follow the approved joint specification. A previously tightened fastener may not retain its original mechanical properties or process behavior.

8.2 Hard joints and soft joints

  • Hard joint: clamp load rises relatively quickly after seating, often over a small rotation angle.
  • Soft joint: the joint compresses or deforms more, requiring greater rotation during clamp-load buildup.

This distinction affects cycle time, angle behavior, control strategy, and the risk of overshoot.

A tightening program developed for one joint type should not be copied blindly to a different joint, even if both use the same nominal fastener size.

8.3 Common joint defects

Defect Possible cause Potential result

Over-tightening Excessive torque or incorrect recipe Fastener damage or yielding
Cross-threading Misalignment or incorrect starting engagement Damaged threads and unreliable joint
Stripped threads Excessive load, damaged threads, inadequate engagement Loss of joint integrity
Missing fastener Feeding or operator error Incomplete assembly
Wrong fastener Incorrect part supply or identification Incorrect joint performance
Incomplete seating Obstruction, trapped material, misalignment False or inadequate clamping
Loose joint after tightening Inadequate preload, settling, unsuitable process Joint movement or loosening

9. Nutrunner controller and parameter management

The controller defines the fastening program and evaluates the resulting process data. Depending on the system, it may store multiple programs, spindle configurations, torque limits, angle limits, rundown parameters, and communication settings.

Important configuration elements include:

  • Program or job number.
  • Fastener and joint identification.
  • Target torque and acceptable limits.
  • Angle limits and angle reference.
  • Rundown speed and final tightening speed.
  • Seating or snug-point detection.
  • Tightening direction.
  • Number of fastening stages.
  • Tool and transducer identification.
  • Result-handling and retry settings.
  • PLC communication parameters.
  • Software and firmware versions.

A program number is not proof that the correct program is selected. The PLC should select or verify the required program for the actual product variant and station operation.

9.1 Parameter change control

Before changing a tightening parameter:

  1. Identify the affected joint and approved engineering specification.
  2. Record the existing program and configuration revision.
  3. Establish the technical reason for the change.
  4. Obtain the required authorization.
  5. Apply the change using the approved procedure.
  6. Validate representative joints using the defined test method.
  7. Verify PLC selection, result reporting, and traceability.
  8. Record the approved revision and release status.

Do not increase torque limits merely to clear recurring NOK results. That can conceal a mechanical defect and create an unsafe or nonconforming joint.

10. PLC integration and fastening sequence

A nutrunner is commonly coordinated with the station PLC. The PLC manages station readiness and production sequencing, while the nutrunner controller performs the configured tightening operation and evaluates its results.

Typical signals include:

Signal Direction Purpose
NUTRUNNER_ENABLE PLC to controller Permit the defined fastening operation
PROGRAM_SELECT PLC to controller Select the approved fastening program
START_REQUEST PLC to controller Request a tightening cycle
TOOL_READY Controller to PLC Indicate readiness according to the interface definition
TOOL_BUSY Controller to PLC Indicate active processing
CYCLE_COMPLETE Controller to PLC Indicate cycle completion
RESULT_OK Controller to PLC Indicate an accepted result
RESULT_NOK Controller to PLC Indicate a rejected result
TOOL_FAULT Controller to PLC Indicate a tool or controller fault
RESULT_ACK PLC to controller Acknowledge a result where supported

These are example names. Actual signals, protocol behavior, and polarity must be confirmed from the installed controller documentation.

10.2 Handshake and result integrity

The PLC and nutrunner controller must agree on the start, completion, result, and acknowledgement sequence.

Important design checks:

  • A previous RESULT_OK must not be reused for a new fastener.
  • The result must correspond to the current program and fastening operation.
  • A timeout must not be treated as an accepted fastening.
  • A communication fault must be distinguished from a valid NOK result.
  • Retries must follow the approved fastening strategy.
  • Each required fastening point must be accounted for.
  • The system must prevent unintended duplicate cycles.
  • Results should be associated with the correct body or assembly identifier.

A controller reporting CYCLE_COMPLETE does not necessarily mean the fastening is acceptable. Completion and acceptance are different states.

11. Multi-spindle and sequential fastening

A multi-spindle system can tighten several fasteners using coordinated spindles. A sequential system may tighten one fastener at a time according to a defined order.

The design must account for joint deformation, fastener interaction, tool reaction forces, and the possibility that tightening one point changes the conditions at another.

Typical checks include:

  • Correct spindle-to-fastener mapping.
  • Required number of successful fastening operations.
  • Correct program assignment to each spindle.
  • Permitted sequence and synchronization.
  • Individual spindle faults and result status.
  • Handling of partial completion.
  • Prevention of product release when required fastening results are missing.

For example, if a fixture requires four fasteners, three successful results and one missing result must not be interpreted as a complete fastening set.

12. Traceability and fastening data

A modern controlled fastening system can store a record for each tightening operation.

Recommended data fields include:

Field Purpose
Vehicle or assembly ID Identifies the product
Station ID Identifies the production location
Tool or spindle ID Identifies the tightening equipment
Fastening-point ID Identifies the individual joint
Program ID Identifies the selected tightening strategy
Cycle number Distinguishes individual operations
Actual torque Records measured or reported torque
Actual angle Records measured or reported angle where available
Result status OK, NOK, aborted, invalid or another defined status
Timestamp Records when the operation occurred
Alarm or fault code Supports investigation
Operator or production context Records the applicable authorized context
Configuration revision Identifies the approved program version

The exact fields depend on the controller, customer requirements, quality system, and applicable process specification.

Traceability should preserve failed and aborted attempts as well as successful results where required. Overwriting an NOK record with a later OK result destroys information needed for defect investigation.

13. Torque measurement and tool verification

A nutrunner's displayed torque is only useful when the measurement chain and tool configuration are suitable for the application.

13.1 Important measurement concepts

  • Calibration: establishes the relationship between a measuring instrument's indication and a reference.
  • Verification: checks whether the tool performs within defined acceptance limits.
  • Repeatability: consistency of repeated measurements under the same conditions.
  • Measurement uncertainty: uncertainty associated with the measurement result.
  • Tool capability: evidence that the tool can consistently achieve the required process limits under defined conditions.
  • Joint validation: evidence that the selected tightening method produces acceptable joints.

These are different activities. Calibrating a torque transducer does not, by itself, validate the assembled joint.

13.2 Verification methods

Depending on the tool and application, approved checks may use a suitable torque analyzer, reference transducer, test joint, or other specified measurement setup.

The test arrangement must suit the tool type and tightening strategy. Dynamic tightening behavior, joint stiffness, rundown speed, and the measurement device can affect the comparison.

Use the manufacturer's procedure and site quality requirements. A single successful reading is not sufficient evidence of long-term process capability.

14.1 Recommended troubleshooting sequence

  1. Record the exact alarm, tool ID, fastening-point ID, and result.
  2. Establish the required safe state before inspecting mechanical or electrical equipment.
  3. Confirm the selected program and product variant.
  4. Check whether the fault is isolated to one tool or shared across the station.
  5. Review the torque-angle curve and controller diagnostics, where available.
  6. Inspect the fastener, joint, socket, spindle, and fixture conditions.
  7. Check power, communication, and PLC handshake if the evidence points to a control fault.
  8. Make only authorized changes and verify the complete fastening process after repair.
  9. Preserve affected product and fastening records according to the quality containment procedure.

16. Safety considerations

Nutrunner systems can create significant hazards even when the tool appears small. Fixed spindles and reaction arms can generate high reaction torque, while automated tooling may move unexpectedly during a cycle.

Key hazards include:

  • Pinching or crushing at the spindle, socket, fixture, or part.
  • Reaction torque causing sudden tool or arm movement.
  • Unexpected automatic tightening.
  • Fastener or socket ejection following component failure.
  • Electrical energy and stored energy in the controller or drive.
  • Pneumatic pressure in air-powered tools.
  • Unexpected motion during jam removal or tool replacement.
  • Sharp edges and hot surfaces around the assembly station.

Required precautions include:

  • Use guards and protective measures appropriate to the risk assessment.
  • Keep hands clear of rotating sockets and moving spindles.
  • Use the specified reaction arm or tool support where required.
  • Apply the approved isolation procedure before maintenance requiring hazardous-energy control.
  • Never bypass guard interlocks or safety functions to run a fastening cycle.
  • Verify tool and fixture stability before releasing equipment for production.
  • Follow the manufacturer's instructions for testing and servicing.

A production STOP command or an inactive start signal does not automatically establish that the equipment is safe to service.

17. Process capability and quality improvement

Fastening quality should be monitored across time, tools, spindles, fastening points, programs, and product variants.

Useful measures include:

Metric Purpose
First-pass fastening yield Percentage of fastening operations accepted on the first attempt
NOK rate Frequency of rejected tightening results
Retightening rate Frequency of additional tightening attempts
Missing-result rate Frequency of expected results not received or recorded
Tool availability Proportion of required operating time the tool is available
Mean time to repair Average time to restore the tool after a failure
Torque distribution Shows the distribution of measured torque results
Angle distribution Shows the distribution of measured angle results
Process capability Evaluates process variation against defined specification limits when the statistical assumptions are satisfied
Fastening traceability completeness Measures whether required operations are recorded against the correct product

A high pass rate does not automatically prove that a fastening process is capable. Results can cluster near a limit, drift over time, or fail to detect a particular defect.

For statistical capability indices such as \(C_p\) and \(C\_{pk}\), confirm that the data are suitable for analysis and that the process is sufficiently stable. A misleading capability number is still misleading, even when presented in a dashboard.

18. Practical exercises

Exercise 1: Investigate torque NOK

A nutrunner repeatedly reports low torque at one fastening point.

  • Identify the affected fastener and selected program.
  • Compare its tightening curve with a validated reference.
  • Check the fastener, thread condition, joint seating and socket.
  • Review tool diagnostics and prescribed verification status.
  • Determine whether the problem follows the tool, the joint, or the station position.
  • Document the root cause and verify the corrective action.

Exercise 2: Detect a missing fastening result

A station requires four successful fastening results, but the PLC receives only three.

  • Confirm the expected fastening-point list.
  • Verify the result identity and cycle counter.
  • Check whether one spindle failed to complete its operation.
  • Confirm timeout and acknowledgement behavior.
  • Verify that the station cannot release the product when required results are missing.
  • Confirm that the traceability record preserves the incomplete operation.

Exercise 3: Review an incorrect program selection

A product variant arrives at the station, but the controller selects a program intended for another joint.

  • Verify the actual part or variant identity.
  • Trace the PLC program-selection signal.
  • Check the controller's selected program and recipe revision.
  • Inspect the approved variant-to-program mapping.
  • Correct the mapping only through authorized change control.
  • Validate the sequence and required fastening results before release.

Exercise 4: Compare tool and joint faults

Several NOK results occur intermittently, but the tool passes its prescribed verification.

Investigate whether the problem depends on the fastener batch, lubrication, fixture position, joint stiffness, operator or automation sequence, or environmental conditions. Tool verification alone does not rule out a joint or process defect.

21. Competency checklist

  • Explain the architecture of a controlled nutrunner system.
  • Identify motor, gearbox, torque transducer, encoder, spindle and controller functions.
  • Understand torque, angle, preload, clamp load and joint stiffness.
  • Interpret basic tightening phases and torque-angle curves.
  • Distinguish torque control, torque-angle control and other approved strategies.
  • Trace PLC program selection, start, completion and result signals.
  • Diagnose common torque NOK, angle NOK, timeout and communication faults.
  • Understand the difference between tool verification and joint validation.
  • Maintain approved programs, backups, verification records and fastening-point maps.
  • Verify complete and correctly associated fastening results before production release.

The objective is to maintain a fastening process that produces the required joint quality, detects abnormal conditions, preserves trustworthy production records, and can be restored without uncontrolled parameter changes.
