1. Purpose of machine vision in BIW

Machine vision uses cameras, optics, lighting, image-processing software, and control-system integration to inspect parts, locate components, verify assembly conditions, and support automated manufacturing decisions.

In a Body-in-White (BIW) line, machine vision can verify whether a body panel is present, inspect weld locations, identify a component variant, measure geometric features, check fastener or clip presence, confirm fixture loading, and guide a robot to a target position.

Vision systems are particularly useful when conventional sensors can detect only a simple condition but the application requires spatial information, shape recognition, measurement, or inspection of several features in one image.

Examples of BIW applications include:

  • Presence and absence detection: confirm that a panel, bracket, reinforcement, or component is present.
  • Position and orientation: determine whether a component is correctly positioned or rotated.
  • Dimensional inspection: measure holes, gaps, edges, distances, and geometric features.
  • Weld inspection: assess visible weld features where the application and imaging method support it.
  • Assembly verification: check clips, studs, fasteners, labels, and component combinations.
  • Variant identification: distinguish vehicle models or part variants using visual features, codes, or both.
  • Robot guidance: calculate a target position or correction from image data.
  • Surface inspection: detect visible scratches, dents, contamination, or other defined defects.
  • Traceability: associate inspection results with a body ID, part ID, station, timestamp, and production result.

A crucial distinction: a camera does not automatically provide a reliable inspection merely because it produces an image. Reliability depends on lighting, optics, calibration, image quality, algorithms, validation, and the defined acceptance criteria.

System type Description Typical BIW application

The system type should be selected according to the required measurement, cycle time, environment, repeatability, and quality risk. A simple photoelectric sensor may be more reliable and economical than a camera when the task is only to detect whether an object is present.

4. Camera technology and image acquisition

4.1 Area-scan cameras

Area-scan cameras capture a two-dimensional image, typically using a global-shutter or rolling-shutter sensor.

  • Global shutter: captures the image across the sensor at effectively the same instant, useful for moving parts and motion-sensitive inspections.
  • Rolling shutter: exposes different sensor rows at different times, potentially producing geometric distortion when objects move during acquisition.

For fast-moving BIW parts, exposure strategy and motion effects must be evaluated carefully.

4.2 Line-scan cameras

A line-scan camera captures one line at a time. Movement of the material or camera builds the complete image.

The system must synchronize line acquisition with motion. Incorrect line rate or speed synchronization can stretch or compress image features.

4.3 Monochrome and color cameras

Monochrome cameras measure image intensity without separating it into color channels. They can be effective for many dimensional, edge, and contrast-based inspections.

Color cameras capture color information and are useful when color differences carry relevant information, such as identifying colored markings or components.

Color should not be used as the sole acceptance criterion unless lighting, surface variation, sensor behavior, and the actual process have been validated.

4.4 Resolution and field of view

The camera's resolution determines the number of pixels available to represent the image. Field of view (FOV) determines the physical area covered.

For an approximate planar setup:

\[ \text{Object scale}=\frac{\text{Field of view width}}{\text{Image width in pixels}} \]

Suppose the camera covers 400 mm across an image that is 2000 pixels wide.

\[ \frac{400\text{ mm}}{2000\text{ pixels}}=0.2\text{ mm/pixel} \]

A feature measuring 10 pixels across would correspond to approximately 2 mm under this simplified model.

However, 0.2 mm/pixel does not mean that the system can accurately measure to 0.2 mm. Actual accuracy depends on calibration, optics, distortion, contrast, subpixel algorithms, mechanical stability, and measurement validation.

A practical design should allocate enough pixels to the smallest feature and tolerance being inspected. The required pixel coverage depends on the application and the inspection algorithm.

5. Lighting and optics

Lighting is often one of the most important factors in vision reliability. Many intermittent vision faults are actually caused by changing reflections, poor contrast, contamination, or unstable illumination.

5.1 Common lighting arrangements

Lighting type Main effect Typical application
Ring light Illuminates around the lens General component inspection
Bar light Directional illumination over a region Edges, long features and surfaces
Backlight Creates a silhouette against a bright background Hole, gap and outline measurement
Dome light Produces diffuse illumination Reducing harsh reflections on some surfaces
Coaxial light Illuminates approximately along the optical axis Flat reflective surfaces and markings
Dark-field lighting Highlights edges, scratches or surface features under suitable geometry Selected surface-defect inspections
Structured light Projects a known pattern for depth or shape estimation 3D measurement systems

No lighting arrangement works equally well for every surface. BIW panels can be reflective, curved, oily, painted, bare metal, or contaminated with weld spatter. The illumination must be selected and validated for the actual part and defect.

5.2 Exposure, gain and focus

  • Exposure time: duration of image capture. Excessive exposure can cause motion blur; insufficient exposure can reduce signal quality.
  • Gain: amplification applied to the image signal. Higher gain can increase visible noise.
  • Focus: adjustment of optical sharpness at the target distance.
  • Aperture: controls the amount of light entering the lens and affects depth of field.
  • Depth of field: range of distances over which objects appear acceptably sharp.
  • Working distance: distance from the camera's optical reference to the inspected target.
  • Lens distortion: geometric deviation introduced by the optical system.

When an image becomes blurry, do not immediately alter the inspection threshold. First investigate focus, exposure, vibration, lens contamination, working distance, and object motion.

6. Image processing fundamentals

Industrial vision software transforms raw image pixels into measurable features and inspection results.

A typical processing pipeline may include:

  1. Image acquisition: capture the image using the required trigger, exposure, and camera settings.
  2. Preprocessing: reduce noise, normalize brightness, or apply suitable filtering.
  3. Region of interest (ROI): restrict processing to the relevant image area.
  4. Segmentation: separate a target object or feature from its background.
  5. Feature extraction: calculate edges, contours, areas, dimensions, patterns, or other useful properties.
  6. Inspection logic: compare extracted features with defined acceptance limits or trained models.
  7. Result evaluation: determine pass, fail, invalid image, or another defined status.
  8. Communication: send the result and associated data to the PLC, robot, or quality system.

The order and methods depend on the application. An inspection does not necessarily use every stage.

6.1 Common image-processing tools

Tool or method Purpose Example
Thresholding Separates pixels based on intensity or color Identify a dark hole against a bright panel
Edge detection Locates intensity transitions Find panel boundaries
Blob analysis Measures connected regions of pixels Detect a missing or present component
Pattern matching Finds a known pattern despite permitted variation Verify bracket orientation
Template matching Compares image regions with a reference pattern Check a known component feature
OCR Recognizes printed or marked characters Read a label or part code
Barcode reading Decodes a visual code Identify a body or component
Caliper or edge tools Measure distances between edges Check a gap or hole diameter
Geometric fitting Fits lines, circles, or other shapes Locate a hole center
Classification Assigns an image or region to a defined class Classify a surface appearance
Anomaly detection Identifies deviations from learned or specified normal behavior Flag unusual surface regions
3D point-cloud processing Analyzes spatial measurements Compare a component surface with a reference

Algorithm selection must match the inspection requirement. A model that classifies an image as acceptable does not automatically provide a calibrated dimensional measurement.

6.2 ROI and coordinate systems

A region of interest limits processing to the portion of the image relevant to the task. It can reduce computation and prevent unrelated features from influencing the result.

A vision system may work with several coordinate systems:

  • Pixel coordinates: location within the image.
  • Camera coordinates: location relative to the camera.
  • World coordinates: location relative to a defined physical reference.
  • Robot coordinates: location expressed in the robot's relevant coordinate frame.
  • Fixture coordinates: location relative to the station's fixture or datum system.

Conversion between these coordinate systems requires suitable calibration and transformation data. Confusing pixel coordinates with physical coordinates is a common source of incorrect robot guidance and measurement results.

7. Calibration and measurement accuracy

Calibration establishes the relationship between image data and known physical geometry or coordinates.

7.1 Types of calibration

Calibration type Purpose
Intrinsic camera calibration Estimates internal camera parameters and lens distortion
Extrinsic calibration Establishes the camera pose relative to another coordinate frame
Pixel-to-world calibration Converts image positions into physical coordinates under the defined model
Hand-eye calibration Establishes the geometric relationship between a camera and a robot
3D calibration Establishes geometric scale and coordinate relationships for depth measurements
Gauge or reference verification Checks measurement performance using a known reference artifact

Calibration and verification are different. Calibration establishes or adjusts the model; verification checks whether the system performs within its required limits.

7.2 Measurement concepts

Term Meaning
Accuracy Closeness of a measurement to the true or accepted reference value
Repeatability Agreement between repeated measurements under the same conditions
Reproducibility Agreement when relevant conditions, operators, or setups change
Resolution Smallest increment that can be distinguished or represented
Bias Systematic difference between measured and reference values
Uncertainty Quantified doubt associated with a measurement result
Gauge R&R Study of measurement variation associated with repeatability and reproducibility

A vision system can be highly repeatable but systematically wrong. For example, if the camera mount shifts, repeated measurements may remain consistent while every reported position is offset.

7.3 Measurement system validation

A robust validation process should include:

  • Defined measurement characteristics and tolerances.
  • Suitable reference artifacts or independently verified measurements.
  • Testing across the required measurement range.
  • Repeatability checks and, where applicable, reproducibility analysis.
  • Assessment of bias, linearity, and uncertainty where relevant.
  • Evaluation of temperature, vibration, surface finish, and part-position effects.
  • Defined acceptance criteria and records.

A camera's nominal pixel resolution alone is not evidence that it meets the required manufacturing tolerance.

8. 2D versus 3D vision

Feature 2D vision 3D vision
Main output Image-plane intensity or color information Spatial geometry or depth information
Common measurements Planar dimensions, edges, codes and patterns Height, depth, surface shape and spatial position
Dependence on surface appearance Often significant Depends on sensing technology and surface properties
Calibration Image-plane and geometric calibration as needed Spatial calibration and depth validation
Hardware complexity Often lower Often higher
BIW examples Label verification, hole detection, planar presence checks Panel geometry, robot guidance, profile comparison

Common 3D methods include structured light, laser triangulation, stereo vision, and time-of-flight sensing. Each has different operating limits, measurement uncertainty, range, and sensitivity to reflectivity and environmental conditions.

A 3D system is not automatically more accurate than a 2D system. The correct choice depends on the geometry and required acceptance criteria.

9. PLC integration and inspection handshakes

A vision system normally exchanges commands and status signals with the PLC. A well-designed handshake prevents stale results from being mistaken for the current inspection.

Typical signals include:

Signal Direction Purpose
VISION_TRIGGER PLC to vision system Request image acquisition
VISION_BUSY Vision system to PLC Indicate processing in progress
VISION_DONE Vision system to PLC Indicate inspection completion
VISION_PASS Vision system to PLC Indicate accepted result
VISION_FAIL Vision system to PLC Indicate rejected result
VISION_ERROR Vision system to PLC Indicate inspection or system error
VISION_READY Vision system to PLC Indicate readiness according to its definition
VISION_RESULT_ID Vision system to PLC or data system Associate a result with a particular inspection
VISION_ACK PLC to vision system Acknowledge receipt, where supported

These are illustrative signal names. The actual handshake must follow the device's communication interface and application design.

9.1 Example inspection sequence

PLC verifies station and part conditions
             ↓
PLC issues vision trigger
             ↓
Camera acquires image
             ↓
Vision system processes the image
             ↓
Vision returns completion and result status
             ↓
PLC validates freshness and result identity
             ↓
PLC accepts, rejects, or requests recovery
             ↓
Result is recorded against the correct part

9.2 Handshake design principles

  • Use a defined trigger and completion protocol.
  • Avoid interpreting a stale PASS signal as a new result.
  • Use a result identifier or transaction counter where appropriate.
  • Define timeout behavior.
  • Distinguish inspection failure from communication failure.
  • Define what happens when the camera is busy or unavailable.
  • Clear or acknowledge results according to the interface specification.
  • Ensure that the result belongs to the current body or part.
  • Prevent repeated triggers from generating ambiguous or duplicate records.

9.3 Fail-safe production behavior

The correct response to a vision failure depends on the quality risk and machine design.

For example, a missing fastener might require rejection or rework, while an invalid image caused by a camera communication failure might require a controlled retry or station hold.

The system must not silently treat NO_RESULT, TIMEOUT, or CAMERA_ERROR as PASS. Safety-related responses must be implemented using the appropriate safety-rated architecture if personnel protection depends on them.

10. Robot guidance and hand-eye calibration

Vision-guided robots use image measurements to determine where a target is located relative to the robot or workpiece.

Typical applications include locating a component, correcting its position, guiding a welding operation, or compensating for variation in part presentation.

The transformation may involve:

Image coordinates
       ↓
Camera coordinate frame
       ↓
Robot or fixture reference frame
       ↓
Calculated target position
       ↓
Robot motion command

This is a conceptual coordinate chain. The exact transformations depend on camera mounting and calibration.

10.1 Eye-in-hand and eye-to-hand

  • Eye-in-hand: the camera is mounted on the robot or moving tool assembly.
  • Eye-to-hand: the camera is mounted externally, observing the robot workspace or part.

Eye-in-hand setups require a suitable relationship between the camera and robot tool or flange. Eye-to-hand setups require a suitable relationship between the camera and the robot's reference frame.

10.2 Common robot-guidance faults

  • Incorrect camera-to-robot transformation.
  • Loose camera mount.
  • Incorrect robot or fixture reference frame.
  • Wrong part identification or coordinate convention.
  • Incorrect units or axis sign.
  • Calibration target damage.
  • Image position offset caused by changed optics or working distance.
  • Robot program or tool-data revision mismatch.

When a robot repeatedly misses a target, first establish whether the error originates in image measurement, calibration, coordinate transformation, fixture positioning, or robot data. Do not compensate by blindly editing robot offsets.

11. BIW vision applications in detail

11.1 Component presence and orientation

The vision system checks whether a component exists in the expected region and whether its orientation matches the defined condition.

Examples include brackets, reinforcements, clips, studs, and panel features.

Important design considerations:

  • Define the actual feature used to determine presence.
  • Test expected variations in position, rotation, surface appearance, and lighting.
  • Consider occlusion by clamps, cables, robot tooling, or other components.
  • Validate against both correct and incorrect assemblies.
  • Define a separate response for an invalid image.

A presence check must detect the intended component, not merely a similar-looking background feature.

11.2 Dimensional inspection

Vision can measure features such as:

  • Hole diameter and center position.
  • Distance between two holes.
  • Panel edge location.
  • Gap and flushness, when the optical geometry supports the required measurement.
  • Component position relative to a reference.
  • Selected weld or assembly features.

For each characteristic, document the measurement method, reference datum, tolerance, calibration requirements, and reaction to an out-of-tolerance result.

11.3 Surface-defect inspection

Surface inspection can identify defined visual defects such as scratches, dents, stains, coating irregularities, or visible weld-related defects.

The challenge is distinguishing genuine defects from harmless changes caused by illumination, reflections, oil, dust, and normal surface texture.

A surface-inspection system should be validated with representative samples of acceptable parts and known defect types. It must also be assessed for false rejects and false accepts.

11.4 Weld inspection limitations

Machine vision may inspect visible weld features, weld presence, selected surface conditions, or geometry. However, ordinary 2D imaging cannot establish every property of a weld.

Internal weld integrity, penetration, and certain subsurface defects may require other inspection methods, such as suitable ultrasonic, radiographic, or destructive testing, depending on the requirement.

Do not equate a visually acceptable weld with proof of complete weld integrity.

12. Vision system configuration and software

A vision application may store camera settings, lighting parameters, inspection tools, ROIs, thresholds, calibration data, result logic, communication settings, and recipe-specific parameters.

Important configuration items include:

  • Camera IP address and network settings.
  • Camera exposure, gain, and acquisition mode.
  • Lens and lighting arrangement.
  • Inspection job or recipe name.
  • ROI and tool configuration.
  • Calibration data and reference coordinate systems.
  • Pass/fail limits.
  • PLC communication mapping.
  • Trigger and timeout settings.
  • Software version and firmware revision.
  • Reference images and validated test samples.

12.1 Backup and restore

A useful backup should contain the complete configuration needed to restore the validated application, including calibration and communication settings where applicable.

Recommended practices:

  1. Back up the approved working configuration.
  2. Record camera, lens, controller, and software versions.
  3. Record calibration status and reference information.
  4. Store the backup under controlled revision management.
  5. Verify that the backup can be restored to compatible hardware.
  6. Perform the required functional and measurement validation after restoration.

A screenshot of the inspection screen is not a complete backup.

13. Industrial network and communication diagnostics

Vision systems commonly communicate using Ethernet-based interfaces, industrial protocols, vendor-specific APIs, or dedicated camera interfaces.

Examples include PROFINET, EtherNet/IP, TCP/IP, and GigE Vision, depending on the equipment.

Troubleshooting should distinguish between:

  • Physical connectivity problems.
  • IP address conflicts or subnet errors.
  • Incorrect PLC tag mapping.
  • Trigger or handshake timing errors.
  • Camera acquisition errors.
  • Processing delays.
  • Application or controller faults.
  • Excessive network load or packet loss.

13.1 Network maintenance checklist

  • Verify the camera and controller IP addresses against approved records.
  • Inspect Ethernet cables, connectors, and switch ports.
  • Check the device's actual communication diagnostics.
  • Confirm the correct protocol and configuration.
  • Check whether image-transfer bandwidth meets the application requirements.
  • Review PLC timeout and retry behavior.
  • Confirm that camera and PLC clocks are suitable for the required traceability.
  • Back up approved network and device configurations before making changes.

A successful ping confirms only limited network reachability. It does not prove that image acquisition, PLC communication, or inspection results are functioning correctly.

14. Common vision faults and troubleshooting

Symptom Possible causes Diagnostic approach
No image Power loss, trigger missing, cable fault, camera fault Check device status, acquisition diagnostics, and trigger
Image too dark Lighting failure, exposure too low, obstruction Inspect illumination and validated exposure settings
Image overexposed Excessive light, reflections, exposure too high Inspect surface reflections and acquisition settings
Blurry image Focus error, vibration, motion blur, contamination Check lens, mounting, working distance, and exposure
Inspection frequently fails Lighting variation, part-position variation, unstable threshold Compare images and measurements against validated samples
False pass Weak acceptance logic, incorrect ROI, insufficient validation Test known defective samples and review acceptance criteria
False reject Excessive sensitivity, contamination, normal variation Review representative good parts and inspection limits
Incorrect dimensions Calibration error, lens distortion, wrong reference Verify calibration and compare against a suitable reference artifact
Wrong robot target Incorrect coordinate transform, loose mount, frame mismatch Check calibration, frames, units, and mechanical position
Camera not ready Startup fault, application error, configuration mismatch Read the controller diagnostics and startup sequence
Trigger timeout Missed trigger, communication delay, busy camera Trace trigger timing, busy status, and completion handshake
Old result reused Stale status, missing acknowledgement, incorrect result sequencing Verify transaction identity and result-clearing behavior
Intermittent communication Damaged cable, connector issue, switch fault, network load Inspect physical links and communication error counters
Recipe mismatch Incorrect model or variant selection Compare the selected recipe with the actual part identity
Image quality changes over time Lighting degradation, lens contamination, camera movement Trend image quality and inspect the optical setup

Recommended troubleshooting order:

  1. Record the actual alarm and machine state.
  2. Confirm whether the image was acquired correctly.
  3. Check image quality before changing inspection logic.
  4. Compare the image with a known-good reference.
  5. Inspect the optical setup, part presentation, and calibration status.
  6. Verify the inspection result and PLC handshake.
  7. Make only authorized configuration changes.
  8. Validate the repaired system with representative good and defective samples.

16. Quality metrics and production performance

Machine vision performance should be evaluated using actual production and quality data, not only the number of images processed.

Metric Meaning Why it matters
Inspection cycle time Time required to acquire and evaluate an inspection Determines station throughput
First-pass yield Percentage of units accepted without rework at the defined stage Indicates process quality
False-reject rate Good parts incorrectly rejected Creates unnecessary rework and downtime
False-accept rate Defective parts incorrectly accepted Indicates escape risk
No-read or invalid-image rate Percentage of inspections that do not produce a valid result Reveals acquisition or process instability
Availability Proportion of required operating time the system is available Shows downtime impact
Repeatability Consistency of repeated measurements Supports confidence in measurements
Calibration drift Change in measured performance against a reference Helps identify mechanical or optical degradation
Mean time to repair Average time required to restore operation after a fault Supports maintenance improvement
Traceability completeness Proportion of required inspections correctly linked to the corresponding part Supports audits and defect containment

Metric definitions must be consistent. For example, an invalid image should not automatically be counted as a passed inspection, and a rejected part should not be confused with a failed camera.

17. Practical exercises

Exercise 1: Presence inspection

Configure a vision inspection to detect whether a defined BIW bracket is present.

  • Define the inspection region.
  • Collect images of correctly assembled and missing-bracket conditions.
  • Test permitted part-position and lighting variation.
  • Measure false rejects and false accepts.
  • Define the PLC response for pass, fail, and invalid image.

Exercise 2: Hole-position measurement

Use a suitable reference artifact to evaluate a camera's ability to measure hole centers.

  • Define the datum and coordinate system.
  • Calibrate the image-to-world relationship.
  • Measure the same reference repeatedly.
  • Compare measured values against independently established reference values.
  • Document repeatability, bias, uncertainty considerations, and acceptance criteria.

Exercise 3: PLC handshake fault

Simulate a vision controller that receives a trigger but does not return a completion signal.

  • Confirm the expected timeout behavior.
  • Verify that a previous pass result cannot be reused.
  • Check whether the PLC distinguishes timeout from inspection failure.
  • Verify the recovery sequence and event record.

Perform simulation or testing only in an authorized environment with the machine in the required safe condition.

Exercise 4: Robot-guidance offset

Investigate a robot that consistently approaches a target with a position offset.

  • Verify the image measurement.
  • Check camera mounting.
  • Verify hand-eye calibration and reference frames.
  • Confirm units, axis directions, tool data, and fixture datums.
  • Revalidate the resulting target position before production release.

19. Final competency checklist

  • Explain the difference between a vision sensor, smart camera, and PC-based vision system.
  • Select an appropriate camera type, field of view, and lighting arrangement for a defined BIW inspection.
  • Explain resolution, pixel scale, measurement accuracy, and calibration.
  • Understand ROI, thresholding, edge detection, pattern matching, OCR, and 3D inspection.
  • Distinguish calibration from verification and measurement validation.
  • Trace the PLC-to-vision trigger and result handshake.
  • Identify stale-result, timeout, and communication-fault risks.
  • Explain eye-in-hand and eye-to-hand robot guidance.
  • Diagnose image-quality problems before changing acceptance limits.
  • Maintain approved jobs, recipes, backups, and calibration records.
  • Track false rejects, false accepts, invalid images, and inspection availability.
  • Verify the repaired system against defined acceptance criteria before release.
