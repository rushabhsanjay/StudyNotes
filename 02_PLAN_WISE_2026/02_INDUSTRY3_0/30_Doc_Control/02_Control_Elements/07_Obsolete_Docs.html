1. Purpose and Scope

Obsolete document control is the process of identifying, marking, withdrawing, restricting, archiving, and retaining documents that are no longer approved for current operational use.

In industrial maintenance and automation, obsolete documents are a serious source of technical error. A technician may follow an outdated electrical schematic, use a superseded PLC backup, apply an old torque specification, or follow a maintenance procedure that no longer matches the installed equipment.

A document can be technically accurate for an earlier machine configuration and still be unsafe or incorrect for the current configuration.

Core principle: Obsolete documents must be prevented from being mistaken for current approved instructions, while their historical value is preserved when required.

This topic covers:

  • Identifying obsolete and superseded documents.
  • Marking and withdrawing old revisions.
  • Archiving and retention.
  • Controlling paper and digital copies.
  • Handling obsolete PLC, HMI, CNC, robot, and drive files.
  • Preventing obsolete information from returning to active use.
  • Audits, registers, practical checklists, and interview questions.

2. Essential Terminology

Term Meaning
Obsolete document A document no longer authorized for its previous intended use
Superseded document A previous revision replaced by a newer approved revision
Withdrawn document A document formally removed from active distribution or use
Archived document A document retained for historical, legal, technical, or evidential purposes
Current document The revision presently approved for the defined application
Uncontrolled copy A copy whose current status is not assured
Retention period Defined period for which a record must be preserved
Document disposition Decision to retain, archive, restrict, transfer, or destroy a document
Point of use Location where a document is used to perform an activity
Revision history Record of revisions and associated changes
Master register Index identifying controlled documents and their current status
Legacy document Information associated with an older system, technology, or configuration
Document withdrawal Removal of a document from active use or distribution
Historical baseline Preserved representation of a previous approved system state
Quarantine Controlled separation of material pending assessment or disposition

Terminology varies by organization. A superseded drawing may be obsolete for current maintenance but remain a valid historical record of the machine's earlier configuration.

3. Why Obsolete Document Control Matters

3.1 Electrical safety

An outdated electrical schematic may show a circuit arrangement that no longer exists.

A technician could isolate the wrong circuit, overlook an additional supply, or misunderstand the wiring of a replacement component.

Actual isolation must always be verified using the approved safety procedure and suitable test equipment. A drawing alone is not proof that a circuit is de-energized.

3.2 Machine operation

An outdated operating procedure may contain incorrect startup, recovery, reset, or changeover instructions.

Using it can lead to equipment damage, production interruptions, or unsafe machine behavior.

3.3 PLC and automation troubleshooting

An old PLC backup may differ from the running program because of later modifications or online edits.

Downloading the old file without assessing the difference can remove valid improvements, alter sequence behavior, or introduce unexpected faults.

3.4 Quality and process control

Obsolete inspection limits, test procedures, calibration instructions, and process specifications may cause incorrect acceptance decisions or inconsistent production.

3.5 Audits and investigations

The organization must distinguish the current approved document from historical revisions and establish which revision applied at a particular point in time.

Deleting every old version may destroy useful evidence. Leaving every old version available without clear status creates a different problem.

4. Why Documents Become Obsolete

Documents may become obsolete because of:

  • Engineering changes.
  • Equipment replacement or retrofit.
  • Software or firmware updates.
  • Revised operating procedures.
  • Changed safety requirements.
  • New quality or process specifications.
  • Incorrect or duplicate documentation.
  • Supplier or OEM updates.
  • Product or process discontinuation.
  • Machine decommissioning.
  • Changes in organizational responsibilities.
  • Replacement of a standard, specification, or work method.

A document may also be obsolete because it was never validly approved, was issued in error, or was associated with the wrong equipment.

The reason for obsolescence should be recorded where required so that users understand whether the document was replaced, withdrawn, rejected, or retained only for historical purposes.

5. Obsolete, Superseded, Withdrawn, and Archived Documents

These terms describe related but different conditions.

Status Meaning Typical action
Superseded Replaced by a newer approved revision Remove from current use and retain history as required
Obsolete No longer authorized for its previous operational purpose Restrict use and determine required retention
Withdrawn Formally removed from active distribution Confirm affected locations no longer use it
Archived Retained in a controlled historical repository Preserve identity, status, and retrieval capability
Rejected Not accepted for release or use Prevent release and retain evidence where required
Uncontrolled Current validity is not assured Verify status before relying on it
Cancelled No longer required or formally discontinued Record disposition and affected references

A document can be both superseded and archived. It can be withdrawn from operational use while remaining accessible to authorized personnel for investigation.

Important distinction: Archived does not mean approved for current use. Access to historical information must not create ambiguity about its operational status.

6. Obsolete Document Control Lifecycle

New revision approved or document withdrawn
    ↓
Obsolescence decision recorded
    ↓
Affected documents and locations identified
    ↓
Document status changed in the register
    ↓
Current-use copies withdrawn or invalidated
    ↓
Replacement revision issued where applicable
    ↓
Historical version transferred to controlled archive
    ↓
Access and retrieval restrictions applied
    ↓
Retention requirements monitored
    ↓
Authorized final disposition when permitted

For urgent safety-related changes, affected obsolete instructions may need to be withdrawn immediately, while replacement information is issued through an approved process.

The organization should never leave users without the necessary safe instructions merely because document administration is incomplete. Interim instructions must themselves be authorized and controlled.

7. Identifying Obsolete Documents

A document-control system should make obsolete information identifiable before someone relies on it.

7.1 Identification methods

  • Status field in the document-management system.
  • Obsolescence or supersession stamp where permitted.
  • Revision register and revision history.
  • Withdrawal notices.
  • Links to the replacement document.
  • Archive metadata.
  • Distribution and withdrawal records.
  • Access restrictions on archived files.
  • Physical segregation of historical paper documents.

7.2 Example status marking

DOCUMENT STATUS: SUPERSEDED
SUPERSEDED BY: H7-TB-EL-004 Rev-C
REPLACEMENT STATUS: APPROVED FOR USE
HISTORICAL USE: RETAINED FOR RECORD
CURRENT OPERATIONAL USE: NOT AUTHORIZED

This is an illustrative marking. Use the exact terminology and format required by the organization's procedure.

7.3 Avoid ambiguous filenames

Names such as OLD, BACKUP, FINAL_OLD, or NOT_LATEST are weak controls because they do not clearly identify the document, revision, status, or replacement.

Use controlled metadata and unique document identifiers rather than relying only on filenames.

8. Withdrawal of Paper Documents

Paper copies can remain in machine panels, maintenance folders, control rooms, workshops, binders, and contractor work areas long after the master has changed.

8.1 Withdrawal procedure

  1. Identify the document and superseded revision.
  2. Determine all controlled distribution locations.
  3. Identify the responsible person for each location.
  4. Issue or make available the replacement revision, where applicable.
  5. Remove the old copy from active use.
  6. Mark or transfer it to historical storage if retention is required.
  7. Record withdrawal completion where required.
  8. Verify that required locations now have access to the correct revision.

8.2 If a copy cannot be located

Record the missing copy and investigate the risk. Do not automatically mark the withdrawal complete simply because the document was issued years ago.

Where the copy could still be used for safety-critical or quality-critical work, apply appropriate interim controls and confirm that current information is available.

8.3 Physical disposal

Paper copies approved for destruction should be disposed of according to the organization's record-retention, confidentiality, and information-security requirements.

Confidential drawings, credentials, proprietary specifications, and sensitive system information may require secure disposal rather than ordinary waste handling.

9. Withdrawal of Digital Documents

Digital documents are harder to withdraw completely because they may exist in email attachments, personal folders, local drives, shared folders, removable media, or exported PDFs.

9.1 Digital controls

  • Change the document status in the authoritative repository.
  • Remove obsolete versions from active-use directories where appropriate.
  • Restrict historical copies to approved archive locations.
  • Update links and indexes to point to the current revision.
  • Notify affected users and teams.
  • Identify replicated or downloaded copies where required.
  • Prevent obsolete files from being republished as current.
  • Retain historical versions under the applicable retention policy.
  • Maintain logs or evidence of withdrawal where required.

9.2 Why deletion alone is insufficient

Deleting a file from one shared folder does not remove copies already downloaded, attached to emails, or saved to a technician's laptop.

Effective control therefore combines repository management, clear status identification, user communication, access controls, and point-of-use verification.

9.3 Search and retrieval controls

Archived documents should remain searchable by their original document IDs, revisions, asset associations, and relevant dates.

However, search results must clearly distinguish historical records from current approved documents. The first result returned by a search engine is not necessarily the right one for live maintenance.

10. Handling Obsolete Engineering Drawings

Engineering drawings are especially important because they describe physical systems and may be used for safety-critical maintenance.

Examples include:

  • Electrical schematics.
  • Panel layouts.
  • Cable schedules.
  • Pneumatic and hydraulic diagrams.
  • Mechanical assembly drawings.
  • Instrumentation diagrams.
  • Network topology diagrams.
  • PLC I/O lists.

10.1 Required controls

When a drawing becomes obsolete:

  • Identify the replacement revision, if one exists.
  • Update the master register.
  • Withdraw affected point-of-use copies.
  • Retain historical versions where required.
  • Link the obsolete drawing to the engineering change.
  • Verify that the current drawing reflects the authorized configuration.
  • Identify any associated documents that must also be revised.

10.2 Example

An electrical schematic shows an older PLC input module. A retrofit replaces the module and changes several I/O assignments.

If the old drawing remains beside the machine, a technician may connect a signal to the wrong terminal or diagnose a valid input as defective.

The revised schematic and I/O list must be approved and issued, while the previous revision is clearly restricted to historical use.

11. Handling Obsolete PLC, HMI, CNC, Robot, and Drive Files

Obsolete technical files require additional care because they can be downloaded to equipment and change its behavior.

11.1 PLC projects

  • Identify the asset and controller.
  • Record the project revision and associated firmware.
  • Determine whether the project matches the installed controller.
  • Restrict obsolete projects from production deployment.
  • Retain previous approved baselines where required.
  • Link the obsolete revision to its replacement or withdrawal reason.
  • Document restoration restrictions and compatibility issues.

11.2 HMI and SCADA projects

Control old screen projects, alarm definitions, tag mappings, communication settings, and runtime dependencies.

An obsolete HMI may display incorrect tag values or provide outdated operating instructions even if the PLC program remains current.

11.3 CNC and robot programs

Control program applicability, controller version, machine identity, tool assumptions, offsets, external axes, and required safety checks.

An archived program for a different machine or earlier configuration must not be treated as an immediately usable recovery file.

11.4 VFD and servo parameters

Older parameter sets may contain incorrect motor data, limits, control modes, feedback settings, or fault-response behavior for the current hardware.

Keep historical parameter sets clearly identified and prevent accidental restoration to the wrong device or asset.

11.5 Industrial network configurations

Obsolete IP registers, switch configurations, firewall rules, and gateway settings can create communication conflicts or security weaknesses.

Archive them with clear applicability dates and restrict reuse to authorized analysis or recovery purposes.

12. Obsolete Documents and Software/Hardware Obsolescence

These concepts are related but different.

Concept Example Main control
Document obsolescence An old electrical schematic is replaced Document withdrawal and archive
Software obsolescence A software version is no longer supported Migration and lifecycle planning
Hardware obsolescence A PLC module is discontinued Spare strategy and replacement planning
Firmware obsolescence A device firmware release is unsupported Compatibility and update assessment
Standard obsolescence A specification is superseded Applicability review and controlled adoption
Procedure obsolescence An old maintenance method is replaced Controlled reissue and user communication

A document may become obsolete because its underlying hardware has been replaced. Conversely, a hardware component may become obsolete while the historical drawings describing it remain valid records of an earlier installation.

Obsolete equipment does not automatically make every document associated with it obsolete for every purpose.

13. Retention and Archive Management

Historical documents may be required for:

  • Incident investigations.
  • Warranty or contractual disputes.
  • Regulatory or statutory obligations.
  • Equipment lifecycle history.
  • Product traceability.
  • Engineering comparison.
  • Root-cause analysis.
  • Verification of earlier approved configurations.
  • Legal or audit evidence.

13.1 Retention rules

Retention should follow the organization's approved records-retention schedule and applicable requirements.

Consider:

  • Record type.
  • Risk and criticality.
  • Regulatory obligations.
  • Contractual commitments.
  • Product and equipment lifecycle.
  • Incident or investigation status.
  • Legal holds.
  • Historical configuration requirements.
  • Storage capacity and retrieval needs.

Do not invent a universal retention period. The appropriate period depends on the document type, jurisdiction, industry, and organizational requirements.

13.2 Archive controls

An archive should preserve:

  • Document identity and revision.
  • Approval and release history.
  • Obsolete or superseded status.
  • Effective or applicability dates where relevant.
  • Related change records.
  • Asset or process association.
  • Retention category.
  • Access restrictions.
  • Integrity and readability.
  • Retrieval capability.

If the original file format becomes unreadable, the organization may need a controlled migration or archival representation while preserving the original record and the necessary evidence of authenticity.

13.3 Destruction and disposition

Destruction should occur only when authorized by the applicable retention rules and when no legal hold, investigation, or other preservation requirement prevents it.

Record the required approval and disposition evidence. Uncontrolled deletion of historical engineering information can undermine future troubleshooting and auditability.

14. Obsolete Document Register

A dedicated register or status field helps prevent obsolete information from returning to active use.

Recommended fields:

Field Purpose
Document ID Original document identifier
Document title Document name
Obsolete revision Revision being withdrawn
Replacement document New document ID or revision, if applicable
Reason for obsolescence Engineering change, withdrawal, error, or other reason
Date declared obsolete Recorded status-change date
Affected assets Equipment or processes associated with the document
Previous distribution locations Locations that may hold the old copy
Withdrawal status Pending, complete, or other defined status
Archive location Controlled historical storage
Retention category Applicable retention rule
Responsible owner Person or function accountable for the action
Change reference Linked engineering-change or document-control record

The register should support traceability without duplicating every field already reliably maintained in the document-management system.

15. Example: Obsolete PLC Backup at a Test-Bed

Assume a test-bed has undergone two authorized PLC modifications. An older backup remains in a shared maintenance folder and is mistakenly treated as the latest version.

Problem

The old project does not contain the latest diagnostic logic and may not match the installed controller.

Required action

  1. Identify the file, asset, controller, and revision.
  2. Compare the file with the approved current baseline and installed controller using supported tools.
  3. Determine the revision history and identify the authorized current release.
  4. Mark or relocate the old file so it cannot be mistaken for the current production master.
  5. Preserve it in the historical archive if required.
  6. Link it to its replacement revision and relevant engineering changes.
  7. Update the register and affected repository indexes.
  8. Notify maintenance personnel and other relevant users.
  9. Verify that the current approved project is accessible.
  10. Record the disposition and any required follow-up actions.

Do not immediately download the old project simply to see whether it works. Production equipment is a poor place to conduct casual experiments in version history.

16. Common Obsolete Document Control Failures

Failure Consequence Preventive control
Old copies remain beside machines Incorrect maintenance instructions may be used Point-of-use inspection and withdrawal
Obsolete files remain in active folders Users may select the wrong project Separate active releases from archives
Replacement documents are not linked Users cannot find the current version Maintain replacement references
Obsolete status is not recorded Historical files appear current Update the register and repository metadata
Every old file is deleted Historical evidence and recovery information may be lost Apply retention and disposition rules
Archives have no access controls Old files may be reused accidentally Restrict access and label historical status
Digital copies are ignored during withdrawal Email or local copies remain in circulation Identify distribution channels and communicate status
Obsolescence is not communicated Technicians continue using familiar old documents Notify affected functions and shifts
Wrong-asset files are archived together without clear identifiers Old files may be restored to the wrong machine Maintain asset-level applicability
Retention periods are assumed Records may be destroyed too early or kept improperly Follow the approved retention schedule
Archived formats become unreadable Records cannot be retrieved when needed Maintain format and retrieval controls
Withdrawal is marked complete without verification Old copies may still be active Record evidence and confirm completion where required

17. Practical Obsolete Document Checklist

Identification and assessment

  • Document ID and revision identified.
  • Reason for obsolescence recorded.
  • Replacement document identified where applicable.
  • Affected assets and processes determined.
  • Operational, safety, and quality implications assessed.
  • Relevant owner or authority notified.

Withdrawal

  • Master register updated.
  • Active repository locations reviewed.
  • Controlled paper copies withdrawn or invalidated.
  • Digital distribution locations considered.
  • Relevant users informed.
  • Current replacement information made available where required.
  • Withdrawal evidence recorded where required.

Archive and retention

  • Historical version preserved where required.
  • Obsolete status clearly identified.
  • Archive location recorded.
  • Access permissions applied.
  • Retention requirements determined.
  • File integrity and readability addressed.
  • Destruction or final disposition authorized where applicable.

Final verification

  • Obsolete information cannot reasonably be mistaken for current instructions.
  • Current documents are accessible at required points of use.
  • Replacement links and revision history are correct.
  • Related software and configuration files are handled appropriately.
  • Outstanding actions are recorded and assigned.

18. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is obsolete document control?

It is the process of identifying, withdrawing, restricting, archiving, and retaining documents that are no longer approved for their previous operational use.

Q2. What is the difference between an obsolete document and an archived document?

Obsolete describes the document's status for a defined use. Archived describes its controlled preservation for historical or other authorized purposes. An obsolete document may be archived.

Q3. Why should obsolete documents not simply be deleted?

They may be needed for historical configuration reconstruction, audits, investigations, legal obligations, or engineering comparison. Their retention must follow the applicable rules.

Q4. What is the risk of using an obsolete electrical drawing?

It may not represent the installed circuit and can lead to incorrect troubleshooting, wiring, isolation assumptions, or equipment modification.

Q5. How should an obsolete PLC backup be handled?

Identify the asset and revision, prevent accidental production use, link it to the approved replacement where applicable, archive it if required, and update the relevant records.

Q6. How do you control obsolete paper copies?

Identify distribution locations, provide the current approved revision where required, remove or invalidate old copies, record required withdrawal evidence, and retain historical records appropriately.

Q7. Why is deleting a digital file insufficient?

Copies may remain in emails, local drives, removable media, backups, and other repositories. Effective control requires status management, access controls, communication, and point-of-use verification.

Q8. Who should authorize document destruction?

The role or function designated by the organization's records-retention and document-control procedure, after applicable retention, legal-hold, and preservation requirements are checked.

Q9. What is the relationship between document obsolescence and engineering change management?

Engineering changes may create new approved revisions that supersede earlier documents. Document control ensures that obsolete versions are withdrawn from current use while required historical records are retained.

Q10. How can obsolete document control be audited?

Review the document register, revision history, replacement links, point-of-use copies, withdrawal evidence, archive status, access permissions, and retention records.

Q11. Can an obsolete document still be technically correct?

Yes. It may accurately describe a previous approved configuration but no longer represent the current equipment or requirements.

Q12. What is the most important control?

Preventing an obsolete document from being mistaken for current approved information while preserving the historical records required by the organization.

19. Key Principles to Remember

  • Obsolete information must be clearly distinguished from current approved information.
  • Superseded documents should be withdrawn from active use and archived when required.
  • Historical retention and operational authorization are different concepts.
  • Paper and digital copies both require appropriate controls.
  • PLC projects, drive parameters, network configurations, and other technical files need asset-specific status identification.
  • Replacement documents should be linked to the versions they supersede.
  • Retention and destruction must follow approved requirements.
  • Withdrawal should be verified where the consequences of continued use are significant.
  • Users must have access to current approved information at the point of use.
  • The system should preserve enough history to reconstruct what was approved and applicable at a particular time.

Effective obsolete document control prevents old information from quietly becoming an active engineering hazard. It also preserves the technical history needed to understand how the equipment reached its current state.
