Document revision is the controlled process of issuing a new version of an existing document while preserving its identity, approval status, and history of changes.

A revision may correct an error, incorporate an approved engineering modification, update a maintenance procedure, reflect a verified as-built condition, or improve the clarity of technical instructions.

The fundamental rule is that a new revision must identify what changed, why it changed, who authorized it, and which version is currently approved for use.

In industrial maintenance, revision control prevents technicians from troubleshooting a PLC using an obsolete I/O drawing, adjusting a VFD using an outdated parameter sheet, or performing preventive maintenance using superseded instructions.

1. Purpose of Document Revision

Document revision provides a reliable way to manage changes throughout a document's lifecycle.

Its objectives are to:

  • Identify the latest approved version of each controlled document.
  • Preserve previous revisions for traceability and historical investigation.
  • Record the reason and scope of every significant change.
  • Prevent obsolete documents from being mistaken for current instructions.
  • Link document updates to approved change requests, work orders, and engineering decisions.
  • Support auditability, safety, quality, and regulatory compliance.
  • Maintain consistency between drawings, software records, maintenance procedures, and equipment configuration.
  • Support comparison between historical and current technical information.

Revision control applies to drawings, SOPs, PM checklists, PLC documentation, CNC parameter records, network topology, risk assessments, commissioning reports, and many other controlled records.

2. Document Revision vs. Document Version

The terms revision and version are often used interchangeably, but an organization's document-control procedure may define them differently.

A revision generally identifies an officially controlled issue of a document. Version can be a broader term that includes working copies, drafts, saved edits, or software-managed versions.

Term Meaning Example
Draft Work in progress that has not been approved for controlled use Drawing Draft 0.2
Revision An identified issue under the formal revision scheme Revision B
Version A particular saved state; meaning depends on the system File version 14
Released revision A revision approved and issued for its intended use Revision C, Released
Superseded revision A previous issue replaced by a newer applicable issue Revision B after Revision C is released
Archived revision A retained historical record Revision A stored for traceability

A file's modified timestamp is not a reliable revision identifier. Opening a document, saving a formatting adjustment, or copying a file can change timestamps without creating an authorized revision.

3. Revision Numbering Systems

Organizations use different revision schemes. The important requirement is consistency, uniqueness, and traceability within the defined system.

3.1 Alphabetic revisions

Examples:

  • A: Initial released issue.
  • B: First revised issue.
  • C: Second revised issue.

This scheme is common in engineering drawings and technical documents.

However, the meaning of a revision letter is organization-specific. Some organizations use 0, 1, and 2 for initial and subsequent releases, while others use letters.

3.2 Numeric revisions

Examples:

  • Revision 0: Initial approved issue.
  • Revision 1: First approved change.
  • Revision 2: Second approved change.

Numeric revision schemes are easy to sort and compare, particularly in document repositories.

3.3 Major and minor versions

Some systems use a format such as 2.0, 2.1, and 3.0.

A typical convention might be:

  • 1.0: First formally released version.
  • 1.1: Minor approved update.
  • 2.0: Major approved update.

The exact meaning of a major or minor increment must be defined by the organization's procedure. Do not assume that a decimal point automatically determines the approval route.

3.4 Combined revision identifiers

A document may have a formal revision plus a separate release or software version.

For example:

Document revision: C

PLC project version: 4.2.1

These identifiers refer to different controlled items. The PLC project may be updated several times while its descriptive document remains unchanged, or a document may be revised without changing the running PLC program.

3.5 Example revision sequence

Document: EL-1059-PLC-IO

Revision A
    ↓
Initial approved I/O drawing

Revision B
    ↓
Approved sensor replacement incorporated

Revision C
    ↓
Verified wiring correction incorporated

Revision D
    ↓
Additional approved I/O modification incorporated

Each revision should have a corresponding history entry and required approval evidence.

4. Initial Issue vs. Revision

An initial issue creates the first formally released version of a document. A revision updates a document that already exists within the controlled system.

Aspect Initial issue Revision
Purpose Establish the first approved release Update an existing controlled issue
Previous released revision None Usually exists
Revision history Records initial issue Records new changes and earlier issues
Review Required according to the document procedure Required according to the change and document procedure
Approval Authorizes initial release Authorizes the updated issue
Superseded copy No previous released issue to supersede Previous applicable revision is superseded
Traceability Establishes the initial baseline Maintains continuity with the earlier baseline

A document that has already been released should not be silently overwritten and treated as a new initial issue merely to avoid maintaining its history.

5. What Triggers a Revision?

A new revision is required when the change affects information that must be maintained as a controlled issue under the applicable procedure.

Common triggers include:

  • Approved engineering changes.
  • As-built updates.
  • Incorrect technical information.
  • Changed equipment specifications.
  • New or revised maintenance instructions.
  • Changes to safety requirements or risk controls.
  • Updated software interfaces or configuration records.
  • Changes to component models, terminal numbers, or I/O assignments.
  • Updated process parameters or test acceptance criteria.
  • Revised manufacturer instructions.
  • Changes required by applicable standards or regulations.
  • Audit findings requiring a controlled correction.
  • Changes to responsibilities, approval rules, or document references.

Not every edit requires a formal revision. Some organizations permit controlled editorial corrections or internal working updates under a simplified process. The distinction must be explicitly defined in the document-control procedure.

6. Revision History

Revision history is the record of the changes made across successive document issues.

It allows a user to determine what changed between revisions and provides an audit trail for engineering decisions and technical investigations.

6.1 Common revision-history fields

Field Purpose
Revision Identifies the issue
Date Records the relevant issue or approval date
Change description Summarizes the modification
Change request Links to the authorized change record
Author or originator Identifies who prepared the change
Reviewer Identifies the technical reviewer where required
Approver Identifies the person or role authorizing release
Release status Shows whether the issue is draft, approved, released, or superseded
Effective date Identifies when the revision becomes applicable, if different from release date

Some systems store approval identities, dates, and workflow evidence electronically rather than printing every field on the document.

6.2 Example revision history

Revision Date Change description Change reference Status
A 2026-01-10 Initial release of PLC I/O drawing Initial issue Released
B 2026-03-12 Updated pressure-switch input mapping CR-014 Released
C 2026-05-08 Corrected terminal references after verified wiring change CR-022 Released
D 2026-08-20 Added approved sensor diagnostics CR-031 Released

The entries are illustrative. Actual history must use real approval records and dates.

6.3 Writing a useful change description

A change description should explain the technical change rather than merely stating that the document was updated.

Weak descriptions:

  • Updated drawing.
  • Modified as required.
  • Corrected document.
  • Latest changes incorporated.

Better descriptions:

  • Added pressure-switch input mapping for the revised test sequence.
  • Updated terminal references to match the verified panel wiring.
  • Revised PM inspection frequency following the approved maintenance review.
  • Updated network topology to reflect the approved industrial PC replacement.

A good history entry is concise, specific, and traceable to the relevant change record.

7. The Revision Control Workflow

Change or correction identified
        ↓
Change request or correction record created
        ↓
Existing document and revision identified
        ↓
Impact assessment completed
        ↓
Revision prepared
        ↓
Technical review completed
        ↓
Required approval obtained
        ↓
New revision released
        ↓
Previous revision superseded and retained
        ↓
Current copy distributed to points of use
        ↓
History and change records closed

The workflow may differ for low-risk editorial changes, emergency changes, and safety-critical engineering changes. The organization should define the required route for each category.

7.1 Identify the correct source

Before modifying a document, confirm:

  • Document number and title.
  • Current released revision.
  • Current status.
  • Equipment or process applicability.
  • Related change request.
  • Existing open comments or unresolved discrepancies.

This prevents changes being made to an obsolete copy that is later mistakenly released as current.

7.2 Prepare the revised document

Update the required content and preserve unaffected information.

Check document identity, revision designation, page numbering, references, units, component tags, drawing symbols, cross-references, and linked records.

For technical documents, use suitable comparison or review methods to identify unintended differences.

7.3 Review and approve

The reviewer checks correctness, completeness, consistency, and technical impact. The approver authorizes the release within their designated authority.

A change that affects a safety function may require specialist review and validation beyond ordinary document approval.

7.4 Release the new revision

After required approval, release the document through the controlled system.

The release should establish:

  • The revision identifier.
  • The approval status.
  • The release or effective date as applicable.
  • The authorized repository location.
  • The status of previous revisions.
  • Any required communication, training, or implementation action.

7.5 Preserve previous revisions

Retain previous revisions according to the organization's retention and archival rules.

Historical revisions can be essential when investigating an earlier breakdown, reconstructing the configuration during an incident, or determining which instruction was valid at a specific time.

Retention does not mean that old revisions should remain available as if they were current. Historical access and operational use must be clearly distinguished.

8. Revision vs. Modification vs. As-Built

These concepts work together but serve different purposes.

Concept Main question answered Example
Modification What change is proposed or made? Add a new pressure permissive to PLC logic
Revision Which controlled document issue contains the change? Release the updated PLC sequence description as Revision C
As-built What is the verified final installed condition? Record the actual verified sensor wiring and installed configuration
Change request Why is the change needed and how is it authorized? CR records the problem, impact, review, and approval
Revision history What changed between document issues? Revision C records the approved sensor mapping change

A modification can trigger multiple document revisions. An as-built update may result in a new revision. A revision can also correct an existing document error without changing the physical equipment.

9. Revision Control for Engineering Drawings

Engineering drawings need clear revision identification because technicians use them to make decisions about real equipment.

Important controls include:

  • Drawing number and title.
  • Equipment or assembly identification.
  • Revision identifier.
  • Revision history block.
  • Drawing approval information.
  • Sheet number and total sheets.
  • Relevant references and cross-references.
  • Change clouds or other approved change-marking conventions where used.
  • Clear status and issue purpose.

Example: Electrical drawing revision

Suppose Revision B shows a sensor connected to terminal X12:4. An approved retrofit changes the connection to X12:6.

The revision process should verify the physical wiring and relevant interfaces, revise the drawing, review the associated PLC I/O mapping, update the history, and release the new issue.

If the physical installation is found to differ without an approved change record, the discrepancy must be investigated before the drawing is accepted as the authoritative record.

10. Revision Control for PLC, HMI, CNC, and Drive Records

Software and configuration records require careful distinction between document revision and installed program identity.

10.1 PLC records

Maintain the applicable information:

  • Controller and hardware identity.
  • Project or program identifier.
  • Approved project revision.
  • Installed project identity where verifiable.
  • Relevant firmware and module configuration.
  • Change reference.
  • Backup and restore details.
  • Verification results.
  • Related I/O, alarm, and functional-description revisions.

A PLC program revision and a PLC documentation revision should be linked where required, but they do not need to use the same numbering scheme.

10.2 HMI records

Revision-controlled HMI documentation may include screen layouts, alarm lists, tag mappings, user permissions, operating instructions, and configuration records.

An HMI screen revision must be linked to the correct project version when necessary so the documented alarms and controls match the deployed system.

10.3 CNC and robot records

Depending on the system, relevant records include machine parameters, approved programs, offsets or tool data procedures, robot frames, tool data, controller configuration, and safety-related settings.

Changes must preserve the previous approved baseline where required, establish the new approved state, and retain evidence of testing and acceptance.

10.4 VFD and servo records

For critical settings, record the applicable controller identity, approved parameter baseline, changed values, change reference, and verification results.

A parameter sheet may receive a new revision even when the manufacturer's parameter number remains unchanged. The revision identifies the controlled record, not the parameter's internal identifier.

11. Minor, Major, and Emergency Revisions

Revision categories can help determine review depth, but the classification system must be defined locally.

Category Typical example Typical control approach
Editorial Correct a spelling error without changing technical meaning Simplified review if explicitly permitted
Minor technical Correct a non-safety-critical technical reference Technical review and required approval
Major engineering Change machine sequence or equipment design Formal impact assessment, engineering review, testing, and approval
Safety-critical Change a safety function or protection requirement Required specialist review, risk assessment, verification, and validation
Emergency Urgent change to address an immediate operational or safety condition Defined emergency authorization and controls, followed by required review and documentation

The revision letter or number does not itself establish the risk level. A change from Revision B to Revision C may be editorial or safety-critical depending on what changed.

12. Effective Date and Transition to a New Revision

The release date and effective date may differ.

  • Release date: The date the controlled document is formally issued.
  • Effective date: The date or condition from which the revision applies.
  • Implementation date: The date the technical change is actually carried out, where applicable.

For example, a revised PM procedure might be released on 1 October and become effective on 15 October after personnel training and planning are completed.

A revised machine drawing may be approved before a scheduled retrofit but should not be represented as an as-built record of the modified installation until the relevant physical changes have been verified.

If revisions apply to different machines, serial-number ranges, software versions, or production phases, the applicability must be explicit.

13. Preventing Revision Conflicts

Revision conflicts occur when multiple documents or copies claim to represent the current state but contain different information.

Common causes include:

  • Email attachments circulated outside the controlled repository.
  • Multiple editable copies stored on separate computers.
  • A technician updating a local drawing without formal release.
  • A new revision issued while old printed copies remain at the machine.
  • Software changed without updating the corresponding configuration register.
  • Two departments using different revision conventions.
  • A change request closed without updating all affected records.

Controls include:

  • One authoritative repository or clearly defined source of truth.
  • Role-based permissions.
  • Unique document identifiers.
  • Defined revision-numbering rules.
  • Formal review and release workflows.
  • Superseded-copy management.
  • Change and approval traceability.
  • Periodic point-of-use verification.
  • Clear rules for emergency and temporary documents.

For critical machine records, it should be possible to establish both the current approved revision and the revision applicable at the time of a historical event.

14. Digital Revision Control and Audit Trail

A digital document-control system can automate much of the revision process.

Useful capabilities include:

  • Version and revision metadata.
  • Workflow-based review and approval.
  • Automatic revision-history entries.
  • Access control and edit permissions.
  • Electronic signatures where appropriate.
  • Comparison between document revisions.
  • Links to change requests and work orders.
  • Controlled publication and distribution.
  • Searchable historical records.
  • Retention and archival controls.
  • Notifications to affected users.
  • Audit logs recording relevant actions.

The system should preserve the relationship between a document's content, revision identifier, approval record, and release status.

An audit log that records file edits is not necessarily the same as a formal revision history. A file may be edited several times during preparation before one approved revision is released.

15. Common Revision-Control Mistakes

Mistake Consequence Prevention
Overwriting a released document Historical traceability is lost Preserve previous approved issues
Incrementing revision without meaningful history Users cannot identify what changed Record a specific change description
Releasing a document before approval Unauthorized instructions may be used Enforce release permissions
Updating only the drawing Related procedures or registers remain inconsistent Complete a document-impact review
Treating a draft as a released revision Unverified information reaches operations Display and control document status
Confusing software version with document revision Wrong configuration may be assumed to be installed Track each identifier separately and link them
Using the revision date as proof of installation Planned and actual configurations become confused Record implementation and verification evidence
Failing to withdraw old copies Personnel may use superseded information Control distribution and point-of-use copies
Reusing revision labels incorrectly Different documents or issues become ambiguous Define numbering and uniqueness rules
Failing to preserve earlier issues Historical incidents become difficult to investigate Apply retention and archival rules

16. Revision Control in a Maintenance Knowledge Base

A machine knowledge base should identify the current controlled revision while preserving the links to historical issues and related technical records.

Recommended fields include:

Field Purpose
Asset ID Identifies the equipment
Document ID Uniquely identifies the document
Document title Describes the record
Current revision Identifies the current approved issue
Document status Draft, under review, released, superseded, or archived
Release date Records formal issue
Effective date Records applicability where required
Change reference Links to the associated authorization
Previous revision Supports historical navigation
Repository location Points to the controlled record
Approved software baseline Links the document to relevant software or configuration
Responsible owner Identifies the accountable role
Last verification date Records when the relevant condition was last checked

For maintenance systems using SAP, Power BI, or other reporting tools, the register can support dashboards showing overdue revisions, open change requests, and documents awaiting release.

The dashboard should report the controlled status from an authoritative data source rather than relying on filenames or manually maintained color indicators.

17. Revision-Control Metrics

Useful measures include:

Metric Purpose
Overdue revision actions Tracks documents awaiting required revision
Revision approval lead time Measures the time needed for review and approval
Document rework rate Tracks revisions returned because of errors or incomplete information
Superseded-copy incidents Measures use or discovery of obsolete copies
Change-to-document closure time Measures delay between approved implementation and completion of required document updates
Revision traceability rate Measures whether released revisions have complete required change and approval records
Audit findings Identifies recurring weaknesses in revision control

A useful measure is the revision traceability rate:

\[ \text{Revision Traceability Rate} = \frac{\text{Released revisions with complete required history and approval records}} {\text{Total released revisions in scope}} \times 100\\% \]

Define the scope, reporting period, and meaning of "complete" before comparing departments or sites.

18. Practical Workplace Example

A test-bed PLC program is modified to add a new permissive, and the HMI alarm text is also changed.

The previous documentation refers to the old logic and alarm behavior.

A controlled revision process should:

  1. Identify the current released PLC documentation, HMI documentation, and related operating procedure.
  2. Link the changes to the approved engineering change request.
  3. Record the actual PLC and HMI project identities.
  4. Verify the revised permissive and alarm behavior against approved acceptance criteria.
  5. Update the relevant functional description, alarm list, and operating instructions.
  6. Review the revised documents for consistency with the verified configuration.
  7. Assign new document revisions under the established numbering scheme.
  8. Record each change in the revision history.
  9. Obtain required approvals and release the updated documents.
  10. Preserve the previous revisions and control obsolete copies.
  11. Update the machine knowledge base and notify affected personnel.
  12. Close the change only after required evidence and document-control actions are complete.

This makes it possible for the next technician to establish which program and instructions applied before the change, what was changed, and which approved records apply afterward.

19. Revision-Control Checklist

Before preparing a revision

  • Current released document and revision identified.
  • Change request or correction record established where required.
  • Reason and scope of change documented.
  • Affected documents and systems identified.
  • Risk and technical impacts assessed where applicable.
  • Correct revision convention confirmed.

Before release

  • Technical content checked.
  • Required review completed.
  • Required approval recorded.
  • Revision identifier updated correctly.
  • Change description is clear and specific.
  • References and cross-references checked.
  • Applicability and effective date clarified where necessary.
  • Associated software or configuration identities checked where relevant.

After release

  • New revision is available in the authoritative repository.
  • Previous revision is retained and marked appropriately.
  • Superseded point-of-use copies are withdrawn or controlled.
  • Affected users are informed or trained where necessary.
  • Related documents and registers are updated.
  • Change and approval records are linked.
  • Historical revisions remain retrievable according to retention rules.
  • Required implementation and verification actions are closed.

20. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is document revision control?

Document revision control is the formal process of identifying, reviewing, approving, releasing, tracking, and retaining successive issues of a controlled document.

Q2. Why is revision history important?

It establishes what changed, why it changed, when the change was issued, and who authorized it. This supports audits, troubleshooting, incident investigation, and engineering traceability.

Q3. What is the difference between revision and revision history?

The revision identifies a specific controlled issue. Revision history records the changes made across successive issues.

Q4. Does a higher revision number automatically mean the document is correct?

No. The revision identifier does not prove technical accuracy, approval, implementation, or applicability. Those must be established through the required records and controls.

Q5. Can a document be revised without changing the physical machine?

Yes. A revision may correct an existing documentation error, clarify instructions, or update a requirement without modifying the installed equipment.

Q6. Can the physical machine change without the associated document revision changing?

It can happen, but if the change affects required controlled information, the documentation is incomplete until the required update is completed. Unauthorized changes must be investigated rather than silently accepted.

Q7. What is the difference between release date and effective date?

The release date identifies when the document was formally issued. The effective date identifies when the revision applies, if the organization defines a separate effective date.

Q8. How should obsolete revisions be managed?

They should be retained according to the applicable retention procedure, marked as superseded or archived, and prevented from being mistaken for current operational instructions.

Q9. What is the difference between a document revision and a PLC program revision?

A document revision identifies an issue of a document. A PLC program revision identifies a controlled state of the software project. The two should be traceably linked where required, but they are separate identifiers.

Q10. What is the most important revision-control principle in maintenance?

A technician must be able to identify the correct approved document for the equipment and determine what changed between that issue and previous revisions.

21. Key Principles to Retain

  • A revision is a controlled issue, not merely a saved file.
  • Revision history must describe meaningful changes and preserve traceability.
  • The revision numbering scheme must be defined and used consistently.
  • A new revision requires the applicable review, approval, and release controls.
  • Revision identifiers, software versions, release dates, and implementation dates represent different information.
  • Previous revisions must be retained when required, but not mistaken for current operational instructions.
  • One engineering change can trigger multiple document revisions.
  • As-built status depends on verified actual conditions, not simply the existence of a newer revision.
  • Digital revision history and formal approval records serve related but different purposes.
  • Effective revision control allows maintenance personnel to establish which approved information applies to a machine, why it changed, and what evidence supports the current issue.
