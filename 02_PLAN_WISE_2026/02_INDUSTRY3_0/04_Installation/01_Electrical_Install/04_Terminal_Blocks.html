1. Purpose and scope

Terminal blocks are electrical connection devices used to connect, distribute, isolate, test, and organize conductors inside electrical panels, machine control cabinets, junction boxes, and field installation enclosures.

They provide structured connection points between incoming supplies, PLCs, sensors, actuators, relays, drives, and external machine wiring.

A terminal block is more than a convenient place to join wires. Correct selection and installation affect electrical safety, signal reliability, troubleshooting time, maintenance quality, and future modification work.

Typical applications include:

  • AC power distribution.
  • 24 V DC control power distribution.
  • PLC digital and analog I/O connections.
  • Sensor and actuator wiring.
  • Motor control circuits.
  • VFD and servo-drive control interfaces.
  • Shielded instrumentation and communication wiring.
  • Protective earth distribution.
  • Test, measurement, and fault-isolation points.

The focus is on understanding terminal-block types, electrical ratings, selection, mounting, wiring, labeling, testing, troubleshooting, and industrial maintenance practices.

3. Basic terminal-block construction

A typical terminal block consists of several functional parts.

The current path and clamping mechanism depend on the terminal design. Different manufacturers may use different internal geometries, even when the terminal blocks appear similar externally.

Do not assume that two terminal blocks are interchangeable simply because both fit the same DIN rail.

4. Major types of terminal blocks

4.1 Screw terminal blocks

Screw terminals use a screw-operated clamping mechanism to secure the conductor.

Advantages:

  • Widely available.
  • Suitable for many power and control applications.
  • Familiar to maintenance technicians.
  • Available in a broad range of conductor sizes and ratings.

Limitations:

  • Incorrect torque can produce loose or damaged connections.
  • Repeated maintenance may lead to inconsistent tightening.
  • Stranded conductors can be damaged by unsuitable clamping.
  • Vibration performance depends on the terminal design and correct installation.

Practical considerations:

  • Tighten to the manufacturer's specified torque.
  • Use the approved conductor type and cross-section.
  • Avoid applying excessive force to the screw.
  • Verify whether ferrules are required, permitted, or unsuitable for the specific terminal.

4.2 Spring-clamp terminal blocks

Spring-clamp terminals use a spring mechanism to maintain contact pressure on the conductor.

Advantages:

  • Consistent clamping behavior within the design limits.
  • Often suitable for vibration-prone industrial environments.
  • Can reduce routine retightening requirements.
  • Some designs support quick insertion and removal.

Limitations:

  • Correct conductor preparation is essential.
  • The release tool and insertion method depend on the design.
  • Not every spring terminal accepts every conductor construction or ferrule type.

Spring-clamp technology includes multiple mechanisms, so the specific manufacturer's installation instructions remain important.

4.3 Push-in terminal blocks

Push-in terminals allow compatible conductors or ferrules to be inserted into a spring contact with minimal actuation.

Advantages:

  • Fast wiring.
  • Compact panel construction.
  • Consistent connection quality when correctly installed.
  • Useful for high-density control panels.

Limitations:

  • Conductor compatibility must be verified.
  • Some terminal designs require a tool for solid conductors, stranded conductors, or removal.
  • Improper conductor preparation can prevent correct insertion.

Push-in does not mean that every conductor can be pushed directly into every terminal. Follow the specified conductor preparation method.

4.4 Barrier terminal blocks

Barrier terminal blocks use insulating barriers between connection points.

They are used in some power, control, and equipment wiring arrangements where the design benefits from clearly separated terminals.

Consider:

  • Rated voltage and current.
  • Required clearances.
  • Conductor size.
  • Environmental conditions.
  • Protection against accidental contact.
  • Applicable enclosure and insulation requirements.

Barrier terminals should not be treated as automatically suitable for every high-voltage application.

4.5 Stud terminal blocks

Stud terminals use threaded studs, nuts, and appropriate termination hardware to secure conductors or lugs.

They are commonly associated with higher-current connections and certain power-distribution arrangements.

Important requirements include:

  • Correct stud and lug ratings.
  • Compatible conductor and lug sizes.
  • Correct hardware and assembly sequence.
  • Specified tightening torque.
  • Protection against accidental contact.
  • Suitable insulation and spacing.
  • Mechanical support for connected cables.

A stud terminal must not be overloaded simply because its metal hardware appears physically large.

4.6 Multi-level terminal blocks

Multi-level terminal blocks provide two or more electrically distinct connection levels within a compact width.

Typical applications include:

  • PLC digital I/O.
  • Sensor supply and signal wiring.
  • Compact machine control panels.
  • High-density interface wiring.

The levels are not necessarily electrically connected to one another. Verify their internal circuit arrangement and markings before wiring.

4.7 Double-level and triple-level terminals

These are multi-level terminal configurations designed to increase connection density.

Advantages include reduced DIN-rail length and organized wiring.

Potential disadvantages include:

  • More difficult access to lower levels.
  • Increased risk of wiring errors if labeling is poor.
  • Reduced convenience during testing.
  • More complex cross-connection arrangements.

The compactness of a panel is useful only if the connections remain accessible and traceable.

4.8 Disconnect terminal blocks

Disconnect terminals allow a circuit to be opened at a defined point without removing the conductor from the terminal.

Depending on the design, disconnection may use:

  • A movable knife or blade.
  • A removable bridge.
  • A plug-in disconnect element.
  • A specified test or isolation accessory.

Common applications include instrument loops, control circuits, and selected PLC signal circuits.

Important distinction: a disconnect terminal is not automatically a load-break switch or a safety-rated isolation device. Confirm its switching capability, current rating, and permitted operating conditions before opening it under load.

4.9 Fuse terminal blocks

Fuse terminals incorporate a fuse holder into the terminal assembly.

They provide a compact way to protect individual control or signal branches.

Applications include:

  • Sensor power.
  • Relay coil circuits.
  • Small DC control branches.
  • Selected instrument circuits.

Verify:

  • Fuse type and size.
  • Rated voltage and current.
  • Breaking capacity.
  • Fuse time-current characteristics.
  • Conductor protection.
  • Accessibility for replacement.
  • Indication or monitoring features, if fitted.

A fuse terminal does not automatically protect every conductor connected to a distribution network. The circuit design must establish which conductors and loads are protected.

4.10 PE terminal blocks

Protective earth terminals connect protective conductors to the intended protective bonding system.

Many DIN-rail PE terminals use a dedicated conductive mounting interface to establish a connection to the approved rail and panel bonding arrangement.

Requirements include:

  • Correct protective-earth terminal type.
  • Suitable conductor cross-section.
  • Compatible DIN rail and mounting arrangement.
  • Correct contact with the conductive mounting surface.
  • Compliance with the enclosure's bonding design.
  • Verified continuity of the complete protective path.

A standard feed-through terminal with a green/yellow marker is not automatically a certified PE terminal.

4.11 Shield terminal blocks and shield clamps

Shield terminals or shield clamps provide a controlled means of terminating cable shields for EMC purposes.

They may be used for:

  • Analog instrumentation cables.
  • Encoder feedback cables.
  • Industrial communication cables.
  • VFD and servo-related shielded cables, where the arrangement is suitable.

The correct shield connection depends on the cable type, frequency range, equipment instructions, and EMC design.

A shield terminal does not replace the protective earth conductor, and the shield should not be assumed to carry protective fault current.

4.12 Test and measurement terminal blocks

Test terminals provide access for measurement, loop checking, or diagnostic equipment.

They are useful for:

  • Analog current loops.
  • Instrumentation.
  • Selected control circuits.
  • Commissioning and maintenance.

Some designs provide shorting functions or dedicated test plugs. These features must be used according to their intended circuit arrangement.

For current transformers, shorting provisions are particularly important where the design requires the secondary to remain shorted when disconnected from its burden. Never open a current-transformer secondary circuit while energized unless an approved procedure and suitable equipment specifically permit the operation.

6. Electrical ratings and selection parameters

Terminal selection must be based on the actual application and manufacturer data.

6.1 Rated voltage

Rated voltage indicates the voltage conditions for which the terminal is designed under specified conditions.

Consider:

  • Voltage between adjacent terminals.
  • Voltage between conductive parts and earth.
  • AC versus DC application.
  • Applicable overvoltage category and pollution degree.
  • Required insulation coordination.
  • The terminal block's approval conditions.

The voltage rating printed on a product must be interpreted within the relevant manufacturer's data and installation standard.

6.2 Rated current

Rated current depends on the terminal's design and specified operating conditions.

It can be affected by:

  • Conductor cross-section.
  • Number of loaded poles.
  • Ambient temperature.
  • Adjacent terminal loading.
  • Enclosure temperature.
  • Cross-connection accessories.
  • Installation and derating requirements.

A terminal marked for a particular current is not necessarily suitable for carrying that current under every possible enclosure and loading condition.

6.3 Conductor cross-section

Terminal blocks are specified for particular conductor cross-sections, often expressed in square millimetres.

Selection must consider:

  • Minimum and maximum conductor sizes.
  • Solid or stranded conductor construction.
  • Flexible conductor class.
  • Ferrule compatibility.
  • Number of conductors per connection point.
  • Insulation diameter and insertion geometry.

Do not force an oversized conductor into a terminal or use an improvised method to secure an undersized conductor.

6.4 Short-circuit withstand and protection

Terminal blocks must be suitable for the fault conditions and protective arrangement of the circuit.

Verify the applicable short-circuit rating or withstand data, protective-device coordination, and any restrictions on conductor configuration.

A terminal's continuous-current rating alone does not prove that it can withstand the prospective short-circuit current.

6.5 Temperature rating

Temperature considerations include:

  • Ambient air temperature.
  • Terminal contact heating.
  • Current loading.
  • Adjacent components.
  • Enclosure ventilation.
  • Conductor insulation rating.
  • Temperature limits specified by the terminal manufacturer.

Excessive heat can degrade insulation, loosen or damage connections, and shorten equipment life.

6.6 Environmental suitability

Consider the actual installation environment:

  • Dust and conductive contamination.
  • Moisture and condensation.
  • Corrosive atmospheres.
  • Vibration and mechanical shock.
  • Temperature cycling.
  • Oil, coolant, or chemical exposure.
  • Enclosure ingress protection.

The enclosure may provide environmental protection, but cable entry, terminal access, and maintenance modifications must preserve the required protection.

7. DIN-rail mounting

DIN rails provide standardized mechanical support for many industrial terminal blocks and control components.

Common rail profiles include TS 35 variants and other profiles defined by the applicable equipment system.

7.1 Installation principles

  • Verify the specified rail profile and compatibility.
  • Mount the rail securely to the panel structure.
  • Use appropriate end stops where required.
  • Fit terminal blocks in the correct orientation.
  • Maintain the manufacturer's specified spacing.
  • Use end plates, partition plates, and separators where required.
  • Verify that bridges and accessories are compatible with the terminal series.
  • Ensure that wiring and cable weight do not impose excessive mechanical loads.

7.2 PE terminal mounting

Some PE terminals establish their earth connection through the approved metal rail interface. The rail itself must then be properly bonded as part of the panel design.

Do not assume that every painted or coated mounting rail provides reliable protective continuity. Follow the approved assembly method and verify the completed bonding path.

7.3 End plates and partition plates

End plates provide insulation and a finished boundary at the end of a terminal group.

Partition plates separate groups where required for insulation, voltage separation, or wiring organization.

These accessories must be compatible with the terminal series and the required insulation arrangement.

8. Wiring terminal blocks correctly

8.1 Conductor preparation

Correct preparation prevents poor contact, broken strands, exposed copper, and mechanical damage.

A suitable process is:

  1. Identify the wire and confirm the required terminal.
  2. Verify conductor type and cross-section.
  3. Cut the conductor cleanly.
  4. Strip insulation to the specified length.
  5. Inspect the conductor for damaged or missing strands.
  6. Fit a compatible ferrule or lug if required by the terminal design.
  7. Use the specified crimping tool and die.
  8. Insert the conductor fully into the designated connection point.
  9. Operate or tighten the clamp according to the terminal design.
  10. Perform the prescribed visual and mechanical checks.

Do not twist stranded conductors into a narrow bundle and assume that this is equivalent to a correctly crimped ferrule.

8.2 Ferrules

Wire-end ferrules are metal sleeves crimped onto stranded conductors to provide a defined termination geometry for compatible terminals.

Advantages can include:

  • Better strand containment.
  • Easier insertion.
  • Reduced risk of stray strands.
  • More consistent conductor preparation.

Important limitations:

  • The ferrule must match the conductor cross-section and terminal design.
  • The ferrule length must suit the connection.
  • Twin ferrules must be explicitly compatible with the terminal and both conductors.
  • Crimping must use the appropriate tool and profile.
  • Some terminal types have specific rules about ferrule use.

Ferrules do not increase the allowable current rating of the terminal.

8.3 Tightening torque

Screw terminal connections must be tightened to the specified torque where the manufacturer provides a torque value.

Too little torque can produce high contact resistance and heating. Too much torque can damage threads, clamping parts, or conductors.

Do not apply one remembered torque value to every terminal block. Different products, conductor sizes, and terminal designs require different values.

Use a suitable calibrated tool where specified, and follow the manufacturer's instructions.

8.4 Multiple conductors in one terminal

Multiple conductors may only be connected in a terminal if the manufacturer permits the exact combination.

Verify:

  • Number of conductors allowed.
  • Conductor type and cross-section.
  • Ferrule or lug compatibility.
  • Electrical rating for the configuration.
  • Mechanical clamping requirements.

If a circuit requires distribution to several loads, use a suitable distribution terminal, approved bridge system, or separate terminals rather than forcing several conductors into an unsuitable connection point.

9. Cross-connection bridges and distribution

Cross-connection bridges connect compatible terminal points electrically.

They are often used to distribute a common supply or reference across a row of terminals.

9.1 Common applications

  • Distributing 24 V DC to multiple sensors.
  • Distributing 0 V to designated return terminals.
  • Sharing a common potential among selected digital I/O terminals.
  • Connecting related control circuits.

9.2 Bridge selection

Verify:

  • Compatibility with the terminal series.
  • Number of poles.
  • Rated current and voltage.
  • Correct cut length, if permitted.
  • Required insulation covers or end accessories.
  • Electrical separation between different potential groups.

Do not assume that a bridge fits electrically just because it fits physically. Some product families have visually similar but incompatible accessories.

For a three-wire sensor, the typical conductors are supply, return, and output. The exact connections depend on PNP/NPN type and PLC input compatibility.

Practical checks

  • Confirm sensor voltage and output type.
  • Confirm the PLC input module's common arrangement.
  • Verify the terminal numbering.
  • Confirm the return path.
  • Verify that the field wire reaches the intended input.
  • Check the PLC input LED and software status.
  • Use the drawing and terminal plan to trace the circuit.

A sensor can be powered correctly while its signal wire is landed on the wrong terminal. Always verify both the electrical connection and the PLC address mapping.

The exact circuit depends on whether the output is a relay contact, sourcing transistor, sinking transistor, or another output type.

Before wiring, verify:

  • Output module type.
  • Output voltage and current limits.
  • Load inrush current.
  • Output common configuration.
  • Required external supply.
  • Inductive-load suppression.
  • Fuse or branch protection.
  • Required electrical isolation.

A terminal block is only a connection point. It does not provide electrical isolation or overload protection unless the specific assembly includes a corresponding rated feature.

12. Terminal blocks in analog signal circuits

Analog signals require careful handling because small electrical disturbances can cause measurable errors.

12.1 4–20 mA current loops

A typical two-wire transmitter circuit includes a DC supply, transmitter, and analog input in a series loop.

Terminal blocks can provide organized connections for the positive supply path, transmitter terminals, signal path, and return.

Important checks include:

  • Correct polarity.
  • Correct loop supply voltage.
  • Compatible input resistance and transmitter requirements.
  • Total loop resistance.
  • Correct isolation and grounding.
  • Shield termination.
  • Test access and disconnect arrangement.

A current loop can fail because of an open circuit, reversed polarity, excessive loop resistance, a faulty transmitter, or an incorrect analog input configuration.

12.2 0–10 V signals

Voltage signals depend on the signal voltage relative to the specified reference.

Potential problems include:

  • Ground-reference differences.
  • Voltage drop in shared return conductors.
  • Electromagnetic interference.
  • Incorrect input impedance.
  • Incorrect common-terminal wiring.
  • Poor shield or routing arrangements.

Use suitable terminals, conductors, and routing for the signal class. Avoid mixing low-level analog signals with noisy drive output wiring without following the required separation and EMC design.

12.3 Disconnect and test terminals

Disconnect or test terminals can make loop checking easier by providing a defined test point.

Before disconnecting an analog loop, determine whether opening the circuit will affect process control, alarms, or machine safety. A test terminal does not automatically make a live circuit safe to disconnect.

For safety-critical or process-critical measurements, follow the approved bypass, test, and restoration procedure.

13. Protective earth terminal blocks

PE terminal blocks are fundamental to the protective bonding arrangement of an industrial panel.

13.1 Typical applications

  • Panel incoming protective conductor.
  • Machine frame bonding.
  • Motor protective conductor.
  • Door and removable panel bonding where required.
  • Cable gland plate bonding where applicable.
  • Protective conductors for external equipment.

13.2 Installation principles

  • Use the designated PE terminal type.
  • Match the conductor to the required cross-section and fault conditions.
  • Ensure the terminal and mounting arrangement are compatible.
  • Avoid relying on painted surfaces or incidental contact.
  • Keep protective conductors identifiable.
  • Verify continuity using the required test method.
  • Maintain protective bonding after panel modifications.

13.3 PE terminal versus ordinary terminal

A PE terminal may have a specialized internal conductive connection or mounting interface designed for protective earthing.

An ordinary feed-through terminal is designed primarily to carry circuit current between conductors. It should not be substituted for a PE terminal unless the specific product and assembly arrangement are approved for that purpose.

PE conductors must not be fused or switched in ways that violate the applicable protective design.

14. Shield termination terminals

Shielded cables are commonly used for analog signals, encoders, communication links, and drive-related circuits.

14.1 Purpose of shield termination

The shield helps control electromagnetic coupling by providing a conductive screen around the signal conductors.

Its effectiveness depends on:

  • Cable construction.
  • Shield coverage and continuity.
  • Termination geometry.
  • Bonding impedance.
  • Frequency range.
  • Cable routing.
  • The electrical environment.

14.2 Practical installation

  • Use the specified shield clamp or termination accessory.
  • Prepare the shield without damaging internal insulation.
  • Maintain the required shield contact area.
  • Avoid long, unnecessary shield pigtails where low-impedance high-frequency bonding is required.
  • Follow the equipment manufacturer's grounding arrangement.
  • Keep the shield separate from signal conductors unless the design explicitly specifies otherwise.

Some applications require shield bonding at both ends; others use a different arrangement. Follow the system's EMC design rather than applying a universal rule.

15. Terminal labeling and identification

Correct identification is essential for maintenance, commissioning, and fault tracing.

15.1 Terminal numbering

Each terminal should have a unique identifier within the relevant terminal strip or numbering scheme.

Example:

Terminal Strip: X1
X1:01  +24V_SENSOR
X1:02  0V_SENSOR
X1:03  SENSOR_204_OUT
X1:04  SENSOR_205_OUT
X1:05  PLC_DI_COMMON

These identifiers are examples only. Actual naming conventions depend on the project's documentation standard.

15.2 Wire and terminal identifiers

A useful identification system connects the physical terminal to:

  • Device tag.
  • Wire number.
  • PLC tag or I/O address.
  • Drawing sheet and connection reference.
  • Cable number where applicable.

For example:

Identifier Example Purpose
Terminal strip X1 Identifies the terminal group
Terminal number X1:03 Identifies an individual terminal
Device tag B204 Identifies a field device according to the project convention
PLC tag Part_Present_DI Identifies the logical signal
Wire number DI-204 Identifies the conductor
Drawing reference Sheet 12, Grid C4 Helps locate the circuit

A consistent system reduces the time needed to trace a field signal through a panel.

15.3 Label quality

Labels should remain readable under the expected environment and service life.

Consider:

  • Temperature resistance.
  • Oil and coolant exposure.
  • Abrasion.
  • Moisture.
  • UV exposure where relevant.
  • Label retention.
  • Compatibility with the terminal marking system.

Handwritten labels may be acceptable under an approved temporary arrangement, but permanent machine installations should follow the required identification standard.

16. Terminal block layout and segregation

Terminal blocks should be grouped logically to reduce wiring errors and simplify maintenance.

A practical arrangement may separate:

  • Incoming and outgoing power.
  • AC control circuits.
  • 24 V DC positive and return.
  • Digital inputs and outputs.
  • Analog signals.
  • Communication wiring.
  • Protective earth.
  • Shield termination.
  • External circuits with separate supplies.

16.1 Separation requirements

Separation depends on the voltages involved, insulation coordination, signal type, applicable standards, and equipment instructions.

Do not assume that placing different voltage circuits in separate rows is sufficient if required creepage, clearance, or barrier provisions are not met.

16.2 Maintainability

A good terminal layout allows a technician to:

  • Identify the correct terminal quickly.
  • Access the conductor without disturbing unrelated wiring.
  • Measure a signal safely.
  • Disconnect an appropriate circuit when permitted.
  • Replace a damaged conductor.
  • Trace a field cable.
  • Restore the original arrangement correctly.

The most compact terminal arrangement is not necessarily the best arrangement for maintenance.

17. Common terminal-block wiring mistakes

  1. Using a terminal outside its permitted conductor cross-section.
  2. Failing to strip the conductor to the specified length.
  3. Leaving exposed copper outside the intended clamping region.
  4. Cutting or damaging conductor strands during stripping.
  5. Using an incompatible ferrule.
  6. Using a twin ferrule when the terminal does not permit the conductor combination.
  7. Tightening screw terminals without observing the specified torque.
  8. Connecting multiple conductors where only one is permitted.
  9. Installing an incompatible cross-connection bridge.
  10. Bridging different electrical potentials accidentally.
  11. Using an ordinary terminal in place of an approved PE terminal.
  12. Leaving terminal numbers or wire identifiers unreadable.
  13. Mixing analog and noisy power wiring without suitable segregation.
  14. Disconnecting an energized circuit without confirming the terminal's switching rating and the circuit's safe operating procedure.
  15. Assuming that a terminal's current rating alone proves short-circuit suitability.
  16. Omitting end plates or partition plates where required.
  17. Failing to verify the correct level on a multi-level terminal.
  18. Using a disconnected terminal as if it were a safety-rated isolator.
  19. Allowing excessive cable weight or vibration to stress the terminal.
  20. Modifying terminal wiring without updating the as-built drawings.

18.1 Diagnostic method

  1. Record the symptom and affected device or signal.
  2. Locate the relevant terminal strip on the drawing.
  3. Identify the terminal number, wire number, and connected device.
  4. Determine whether the circuit must be isolated before inspection.
  5. Verify supply, signal, and return paths using the approved procedure.
  6. Inspect conductor preparation, clamp condition, and labeling.
  7. Compare measurements with the equipment specifications.
  8. Correct the identified fault.
  9. Re-test the affected function.
  10. Update the relevant records if the wiring or terminal arrangement has changed.

Avoid randomly retightening every terminal. Unnecessary intervention can damage terminals or introduce new faults.

19. Inspection and maintenance checklist

19.1 Visual inspection

  • Terminal type matches the design.
  • Terminal ratings suit the circuit.
  • DIN rail and mounting arrangement are secure.
  • Required end plates and separators are installed.
  • Terminal numbers are readable.
  • Wire identifiers match the drawings.
  • Conductors are correctly prepared and inserted.
  • No loose strands or unintended exposed copper are visible.
  • Bridges are compatible and correctly installed.
  • PE terminals and bonding connections are correct.
  • Shields are terminated according to the design.
  • Wiring is routed and supported correctly.
  • No discoloration, corrosion, cracking, or signs of overheating are present.

19.2 Electrical and mechanical checks

  • Verify continuity where required.
  • Check protective bonding using the prescribed method.
  • Check voltage, polarity, or signal values as appropriate.
  • Inspect screw connections according to the approved maintenance procedure.
  • Use specified torque only where a torque check is required.
  • Confirm disconnect and test-terminal operation where applicable.
  • Verify fuse ratings and branch protection.
  • Confirm that any temporary test connections have been removed.
  • Confirm that the circuit has been restored to its intended state.

Do not perform resistance, continuity, or torque checks on energized circuits unless the specific procedure and equipment are designed to permit the operation. Use the required isolation and test-before-touch procedure.

20. Terminal block selection workflow

A repeatable selection method reduces compatibility errors.

  1. Identify the circuit function. Determine whether it is power, control, analog, communication, protective earth, or shield termination.
  2. Establish the electrical requirements. Determine voltage, current, fault conditions, and required isolation.
  3. Identify conductor requirements. Confirm cross-section, conductor construction, ferrule use, and number of conductors.
  4. Select the terminal technology. Choose screw, spring-clamp, push-in, disconnect, fuse, PE, or another appropriate type.
  5. Verify the ratings. Check the manufacturer's technical data and applicable approvals.
  6. Check mechanical compatibility. Confirm rail profile, width, end accessories, and bridge system.
  7. Plan the terminal layout. Organize by function, potential, accessibility, and segregation requirements.
  8. Define identification. Establish terminal numbering, wire numbers, and drawing references.
  9. Install and terminate. Follow the specified preparation and connection procedure.
  10. Inspect and test. Verify the wiring and required electrical characteristics.
  11. Update documentation. Record the final terminal arrangement and any approved changes.

21. Practical industrial scenarios

Scenario 1: Proximity sensor works intermittently

A sensor is correctly powered, but the PLC input occasionally turns OFF.

Investigation:

  • Check the sensor output and the PLC input status.
  • Trace the signal conductor through the relevant terminal strip.
  • Inspect conductor insertion, ferrule quality, and terminal condition.
  • Check the 0 V return path and supply stability.
  • Inspect cable routing near VFD and solenoid wiring.
  • Verify that the signal is connected to the correct PLC input.

A loose terminal can cause a symptom that looks like a failed sensor. Verify the complete circuit before replacing the device.

Scenario 2: 24 V DC distribution terminal overheats

Several sensors and actuators share a bridged terminal group. A terminal shows discoloration.

Potential causes include:

  • Total current exceeds the applicable rating.
  • The bridge is unsuitable for the load.
  • A terminal contact is damaged.
  • A conductor is incorrectly prepared.
  • A screw connection has incorrect torque.
  • Ambient temperature or grouping conditions require derating.

Corrective action:

  • Isolate the affected circuit using the approved procedure.
  • Inspect the terminal, conductor, and bridge.
  • Establish the actual load current and fault history.
  • Verify ratings and the required protection.
  • Replace damaged parts with compatible components.
  • Reorganize the distribution if necessary.
  • Verify operation and document the correction.

Do not simply tighten a visibly damaged or overheated terminal and return it to service.

Scenario 3: Analog pressure reading fluctuates

A 4–20 mA pressure transmitter shows unstable readings.

Investigation:

  • Verify the transmitter's supply and polarity.
  • Trace the current loop through all terminals.
  • Inspect disconnect or test terminals for the intended position.
  • Check the total loop resistance.
  • Verify shield termination and signal-cable routing.
  • Confirm that the PLC analog input range and scaling are correct.

Terminal faults, an open test link, poor reference connections, and incorrect scaling can produce similar symptoms. Separate electrical diagnosis from software scaling verification.

Scenario 4: Terminal strip modification after PLC replacement

A replacement PLC has a different I/O arrangement, and several field wires need to be moved.

Correct procedure:

  1. Confirm the replacement module specifications and approved design.
  2. Identify each field wire from the existing terminal plan.
  3. Verify the old and new I/O mapping.
  4. Determine whether the terminal strip can remain unchanged or requires modification.
  5. Make the approved changes under the required isolation procedure.
  6. Update wire and terminal identifiers as necessary.
  7. Update the I/O list and electrical drawings.
  8. Perform loop checks and functional verification.
  9. Record the final configuration.

Physical compatibility does not guarantee electrical or logical compatibility.

22. Documentation for industrial maintenance

Terminal documentation should allow a technician to trace a signal without dismantling the panel.

Maintain:

  • Terminal-strip designation and terminal numbers.
  • Terminal manufacturer and part number where required.
  • Terminal type and ratings.
  • Cross-connection and accessory details.
  • Wire numbers and conductor sizes.
  • Field cable identifiers.
  • Device tags and PLC I/O mapping.
  • Drawing sheet and connection references.
  • Fuse ratings and disconnect-terminal arrangements.
  • PE and shield termination details.
  • Approved modifications and revision history.

A terminal schedule is especially useful for panels containing multiple terminal strips, large numbers of sensors, remote I/O, or field junction boxes.

23. Interview questions and answers

1. What is the main purpose of a terminal block?

It provides an organized and maintainable electrical connection point between conductors, equipment, and field wiring.

2. What is the difference between a feed-through terminal and a disconnect terminal?

A feed-through terminal provides a continuous electrical connection between compatible conductors. A disconnect terminal provides a designed means to open a circuit at a defined point. Its load-breaking and isolation capabilities must be verified separately.

3. Why are ferrules used?

Ferrules provide a defined termination for compatible stranded conductors, helping contain strands and improve insertion consistency. Their use must follow the terminal manufacturer's requirements.

4. Can two wires be connected to one terminal?

Only when the terminal manufacturer permits the specific number, conductor type, cross-section, and termination arrangement.

5. What is the difference between a PE terminal and an ordinary terminal?

A PE terminal is designed and approved for the intended protective bonding function. An ordinary terminal should not be used as a protective terminal unless the product and assembly arrangement are explicitly approved for that purpose.

6. Why are terminal blocks bridged?

Bridges distribute a common electrical potential to multiple compatible terminals. Their current rating, compatibility, insulation, and branch-protection requirements must be checked.

7. Why might a terminal block overheat?

Possible causes include excessive current, poor contact, incorrect conductor preparation, damaged clamping parts, improper torque, or unsuitable derating conditions.

8. Can a terminal block be disconnected while energized?

Only if its design, ratings, circuit function, and approved procedure explicitly permit the operation. A disconnect feature does not automatically make a terminal safe to operate under load.

9. Why are terminal numbers important?

They link physical wiring to schematics, terminal schedules, wire numbers, PLC I/O lists, and maintenance records.

10. Why must shield terminals be selected carefully?

Shield termination affects EMC performance. The required bonding arrangement depends on the cable, equipment, signal type, and system design.

11. Does a terminal's current rating establish its short-circuit suitability?

No. Fault withstand and protective-device coordination must also be verified under the applicable requirements.

12. What should be done after changing terminal wiring?

Verify the circuit, perform the required functional tests, and update the as-built schematic, terminal schedule, wire identifiers, I/O records, and revision history.

24. Final mental model

A terminal block is the physical interface between electrical design and field wiring.

To work with terminal blocks effectively, understand five things:

  1. Electrical suitability: Voltage, current, conductor size, fault conditions, and environmental ratings.
  2. Mechanical connection: Correct preparation, ferrules where suitable, clamping method, torque, and conductor support.
  3. Circuit function: Power distribution, control signals, analog loops, protective earth, shielding, or isolation.
  4. Traceability: Terminal numbers, wire identifiers, device tags, and drawings.
  5. Maintainability: Safe access, test points, disconnect provisions, fault isolation, and accurate documentation.

For industrial maintenance, the most useful habit is to trace a signal from the field device through the identified terminal, into the relevant PLC or interface, and back through its supply and return path. A well-designed terminal system makes that process straightforward instead of turning a simple wiring fault into an afternoon of panel archaeology.
