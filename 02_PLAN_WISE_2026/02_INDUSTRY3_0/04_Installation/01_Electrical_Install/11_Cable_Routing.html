1. Purpose and scope

Cable routing is the planned physical path followed by electrical cables between their source, destination, junctions, control panels, machines, and field devices.

In industrial installations, cable routing affects electrical safety, electromagnetic compatibility (EMC), signal reliability, mechanical protection, maintenance access, cooling, fire safety, and future modifications.

A technically correct circuit can still fail in service if its cable route exposes it to excessive heat, vibration, mechanical damage, electrical noise, moisture, or poor termination access.

Cable routing applies to:

  • Power distribution cables.
  • PLC input/output wiring.
  • Analog instrumentation cables.
  • VFD and servo motor cables.
  • Encoder and resolver feedback cables.
  • Industrial Ethernet and fieldbus cables.
  • Pneumatic and hydraulic machine interfaces where electrical cables share routes with other services.
  • Safety circuits, emergency-stop circuits, and protective-device wiring.
  • CNC, robot, test-bench, conveyor, and assembly-line installations.

The goal is not simply to find a path between two points. The goal is to create a route that is safe, reliable, maintainable, documented, and suitable for the machine's operating environment.

A typical industrial cable installation may use several routing systems.

3. Common cable-routing methods

3.1 Cable trays

Cable trays support cables along a defined route, typically above machines, along structural steelwork, or through service corridors.

Common forms include:

  • Ladder tray.
  • Perforated tray.
  • Solid-bottom tray.
  • Wire-mesh tray.
  • Heavy-duty industrial tray.
Tray type Typical application Important consideration
Ladder tray Larger power cables and long industrial runs Check cable support spacing, sag, and protection
Perforated tray General industrial cable distribution Check loading, ventilation, and edge protection
Solid-bottom tray Applications needing additional support or containment Heat dissipation and drainage need consideration
Wire-mesh tray Lighter cables and flexible routing arrangements Check mechanical strength and suitability for the environment

Advantages include accessibility, flexibility, and relatively straightforward inspection.

Limitations include exposure to dust, oil, falling objects, moisture, and mechanical impact unless the design provides suitable protection.

3.2 Cable trunking and wire ducts

Trunking provides an enclosed or partially enclosed route for cables.

Common applications include:

  • Control-panel wiring.
  • Machine electrical cabinets.
  • Short equipment interconnections.
  • Organized routing of control and signal wiring.

Panel wire ducts make internal wiring easier to trace and modify. However, the duct must not be overfilled, and conductors must not be crushed when covers are installed.

Use trunking suitable for the environment and cable type. A panel wiring duct is not automatically suitable for outdoor use, washdown areas, or heavy mechanical exposure.

3.3 Conduit

Conduit encloses cables or individual insulated conductors and provides mechanical protection.

Common types include:

  • Metallic conduit.
  • Non-metallic conduit.
  • Flexible conduit.
  • Liquid-tight flexible conduit.
  • Rigid conduit systems.

Conduit is useful where cables require protection from impact, abrasion, contamination, or other environmental hazards.

Check conduit fill, bend radius, pulling tension, fittings, bonding requirements for metallic systems, and the manufacturer's installation instructions.

3.4 Cable trenches and underground ducts

Cable trenches and underground duct systems are used where cables must travel below floor level or underground.

Important considerations include:

  • Water accumulation and drainage.
  • Cable support and separation.
  • Mechanical protection.
  • Covers and safe access.
  • Fire barriers where required.
  • Cable identification.
  • Future cable-pulling provisions.
  • Utility crossings and approved clearances.

A trench that fills with water, oil, or debris can turn a routine cable route into a maintenance and safety problem.

3.5 Flexible cable carriers and drag chains

Drag chains guide cables that move repeatedly with machine axes, gantries, robots, and other moving assemblies.

They help control the cable's path and reduce uncontrolled bending or snagging.

Typical applications include:

  • CNC machine axes.
  • Gantry systems.
  • Automated transfer systems.
  • Moving fixtures.
  • Industrial inspection equipment.
  • Selected robot dress-pack arrangements.

Cable carriers require special attention to dynamic bending, acceleration, cable separation, support, and service life.

3.6 Flexible conduit and machine harnesses

Flexible conduit and machine harnesses connect stationary electrical infrastructure to moving or vibration-prone equipment.

Applications include motors, sensors, actuators, robot tooling, and machine-mounted junction boxes.

The selected assembly must suit the movement, oil exposure, coolant, temperature, abrasion, and chemical conditions involved.

Flexible conduit protects a cable mechanically but does not automatically make the cable suitable for continuous flexing or repeated torsion.

4. Cable-routing design inputs

Before selecting a route, establish the main engineering inputs.

Input Why it matters
Cable function Determines signal, power, safety, and EMC requirements
Cable type Defines insulation, shielding, flexibility, temperature, and environmental limits
Cable length Affects voltage drop, communication performance, and permitted drive or encoder length
Current and load Affects conductor sizing, thermal performance, and protection
Operating environment Determines enclosure, material, and ingress-protection requirements
Mechanical movement Determines flex-life, bend radius, and carrier requirements
Noise sources Determines routing separation and shielding strategy
Fire requirements May determine cable construction, penetrations, and fire stopping
Maintenance access Influences tray position, route accessibility, and spare capacity
Future expansion Determines whether the route can accept planned additional cables
Applicable standards Establishes mandatory installation and safety requirements

The route should be designed with the cable specification and equipment manufacturer's limits in mind. Cable routing cannot compensate for an incorrectly selected cable.

5. Cable-route planning procedure

A practical cable-routing design can follow this sequence.

  1. Identify the source and destination of each cable.
  2. Confirm cable function, type, size, shielding, and environmental rating.
  3. Review machine-layout and electrical drawings.
  4. Identify available trays, ducts, conduits, and machine entry points.
  5. Identify heat sources, moving components, sharp edges, and mechanical hazards.
  6. Establish required segregation from other cable categories.
  7. Check route length, voltage drop, cable-pulling conditions, and manufacturer limits.
  8. Check bend radius, support spacing, loading, and dynamic movement requirements.
  9. Provide suitable access for installation, inspection, and future replacement.
  10. Assign cable tags and update the cable schedule and route drawings.

Do not route cables first and attempt to resolve all safety, EMC, and access problems afterward. That approach generally produces a very expensive exercise in regret.

6. Cable length, voltage drop, and signal limitations

Cable length is not only a physical-layout concern. It can affect electrical performance.

6.1 Voltage drop

For a simple DC circuit using a two-wire cable, the approximate voltage drop is:

\[ \Delta V = I R\_{\text{loop}} \]

Where:

  • \(\Delta V\) is the voltage drop.
  • \(I\) is the load current.
  • \(R\_{\text{loop}}\) is the total resistance of the outgoing and return conductors.

For a conductor of uniform cross-sectional area:

\[ R = \rho \frac{L}{A} \]

Where:

  • \(R\) is conductor resistance.
  • \(\rho\) is the conductor material's resistivity.
  • \(L\) is conductor length.
  • \(A\) is conductor cross-sectional area.

For a two-wire DC circuit with equal conductors and one-way route length \(L\), the approximate loop resistance is:

\[ R\_{\text{loop}} = 2\rho \frac{L}{A} \]

Actual design must account for conductor temperature, connection resistance, current conditions, cable construction, and applicable voltage-drop limits.

Long 24 VDC routes can be particularly important because a modest voltage drop may affect sensors, valves, remote I/O, or other equipment with a narrow operating-voltage range.

6.2 Communication limits

Industrial communication links have specific limits for cable type, length, topology, and termination.

Examples include:

  • Industrial Ethernet channel and link-length limits.
  • RS-485 cable and network-topology constraints.
  • Fieldbus-specific cable and termination requirements.
  • Encoder cable-length restrictions.
  • Manufacturer-defined drive-to-motor cable limits.

Longer is not automatically acceptable merely because the cable is shielded.

6.3 VFD and servo cable length

Excessive motor-cable length can affect reflected-wave voltage, common-mode current, electromagnetic emissions, and drive performance.

Follow the drive manufacturer's limits and requirements for output reactors, dV/dt filters, sine-wave filters, shield construction, and cable termination.

Do not add a filter or extend a cable based solely on a general rule. Confirm compatibility with the specific drive and motor combination.

7. Bend radius and cable handling

Bend radius is the radius of curvature through which a cable is bent.

Cable manufacturers specify minimum bend radii because excessive bending can damage conductors, insulation, shields, and internal cable geometry.

Typical requirements differ for:

  • Fixed installation.
  • Occasional movement.
  • Continuous flexing.
  • Torsional movement.
  • Drag-chain applications.
  • High-flex encoder and servo cables.

The required bend radius may depend on cable diameter, construction, movement, and installation conditions.

7.1 Fixed cable installation

For fixed installation:

  • Observe the specified minimum bend radius.
  • Avoid sharp bends at tray corners and enclosure entries.
  • Use suitable guides and supports.
  • Avoid crushing cables under covers or clamps.
  • Protect cable insulation from sharp edges.

7.2 Dynamic cable installation

For moving cables:

  • Use cables rated for the actual motion.
  • Follow the carrier manufacturer's cable arrangement guidance.
  • Maintain the required bend radius throughout the motion.
  • Avoid tensile loading on conductors and connectors.
  • Allow the cable the intended movement space.
  • Check the required minimum spacing between cables.
  • Avoid twisting cables unless they are designed for torsional movement.

A cable suitable for fixed installation may fail quickly when repeatedly flexed in a machine axis.

8. Cable pulling and installation tension

Cable pulling can damage cables even when the final route looks correct.

Potential damage includes:

  • Stretched conductors.
  • Deformed insulation.
  • Damaged shield braid.
  • Displaced internal conductors.
  • Crushed cable geometry.
  • Damaged outer jackets.

Use suitable pulling methods, rollers, guides, and approved pulling accessories.

Check the manufacturer's maximum pulling tension and sidewall-pressure limits where applicable. Do not pull directly on connector pins, signal conductors, or an improvised shield connection.

For long or complex routes, assess pulling direction, intermediate access points, bend count, conduit fill, and pulling tension before installation.

9. Cable supports and mechanical protection

Cables must be supported so that their weight, movement, and environmental exposure do not damage the cable or its termination.

Check:

  • Tray loading and support spacing.
  • Vertical cable support.
  • Cable cleats where required.
  • Protection against vibration.
  • Abrasion at penetrations.
  • Strain relief at equipment entries.
  • Separation from hot surfaces.
  • Protection against moving machinery.
  • Resistance to oil, coolant, chemicals, and moisture.

Cable cleats and supports must suit the cable diameter, weight, installation arrangement, and any fault-current forces that the design requires them to withstand.

Do not use ordinary cable ties as a substitute for engineered short-circuit cable restraint where the design requires suitable cleats.

10. Routing near motors, VFDs, and high-current conductors

Power conductors and switching equipment can produce electromagnetic interference.

Common sources include:

  • VFD output cables.
  • Servo motor cables.
  • Motor feeders.
  • Welding power cables.
  • Transformer feeders.
  • High-current DC conductors.
  • Switching power supplies and contactor circuits.

Good routing practices include:

  • Follow specified separation distances.
  • Use separate trays or compartments where required.
  • Avoid long parallel runs of noisy power cables beside sensitive signal cables.
  • Cross different cable categories at approximately right angles when practical and permitted.
  • Use suitable shielded cables and termination arrangements.
  • Maintain the specified bonding and protective-earth arrangements.
  • Keep cable routes away from excessive heat and mechanical hazards.

The required separation depends on cable categories, shielding, tray construction, voltage, current, installation standards, and equipment instructions. There is no single universal distance suitable for every installation.

11. Routing of PLC I/O and instrumentation cables

PLC and instrumentation routes should preserve signal integrity and make faults easier to diagnose.

11.1 Digital I/O

Digital input and output cables may carry 24 VDC, 110 VAC, or other specified control voltages.

Check:

  • Rated voltage and conductor size.
  • Isolation between voltage classes.
  • Inductive loads and transient suppression.
  • Segregation from sensitive analog wiring.
  • Terminal and channel identification.
  • Cable protection at machine entries.

11.2 Analog I/O

Analog signals may include 4–20 mA, 0–10 V, RTD, thermocouple, load-cell, and other measurement circuits.

Check:

  • Correct cable type.
  • Shielding and termination requirements.
  • Signal return and reference arrangements.
  • Separation from noisy power circuits.
  • Cable length and input specifications.
  • Sensor-specific wiring requirements.

11.3 Remote I/O

Remote I/O installations may include power, communication, and field-device wiring in the same physical area.

Maintain the required segregation and follow the remote I/O manufacturer's guidance for supply wiring, communication wiring, grounding, shielding, and connector installation.

12. Routing industrial Ethernet and fieldbus cables

Communication cables require correct routing and installation to preserve network performance.

12.1 Industrial Ethernet

Check:

  • Suitable industrial cable category and rating.
  • Link-length limitations.
  • Connector compatibility.
  • Bend radius and pulling tension.
  • Separation from interference sources.
  • Shielding and bonding where specified.
  • Protection from oil, coolant, vibration, and mechanical damage.

Avoid unnecessary joints and adapters in demanding industrial routes. Where a joint is unavoidable, use an approved connection system suitable for the network and environment.

12.2 RS-485 and fieldbus

Follow the relevant network topology, cable specification, and termination rules.

Important factors include:

  • Cable impedance.
  • Maximum segment length.
  • Permitted branch or stub lengths.
  • Termination resistors.
  • Shield connections.
  • Reference-conductor requirements.
  • Number of devices.
  • Network baud rate and device-specific limits.

Poor physical routing can create intermittent communication faults that resemble software or PLC configuration problems.

13. Routing cables through machine moving sections

Moving machine sections require a route designed for repeated movement rather than merely static support.

Typical locations include:

  • CNC X, Y, and Z axes.
  • Gantry transfer systems.
  • Moving robot tooling.
  • Linear transfer units.
  • Automated inspection heads.
  • Cable carriers between stationary and moving structures.

13.1 Drag-chain design

When using a drag chain, consider:

  • Dynamic bend radius.
  • Cable outer diameter.
  • Cable weight.
  • Internal chain width and height.
  • Cable separation and dividers.
  • Unsupported chain length.
  • Acceleration and speed.
  • Travel distance.
  • Torsion and flex-cycle requirements.
  • Replacement access.

Avoid overfilling the chain or bundling cables so tightly that they cannot move as designed.

13.2 Robot dress packs

Robot dress packs must accommodate the robot's motion envelope.

Consider:

  • Wrist rotation and torsion.
  • Cable and hose flexibility.
  • Clamping points.
  • Connector orientation.
  • Collision risk.
  • Abrasion and snagging.
  • Service life under the actual motion program.

A route that works at one robot position may be damaged at another. Verify the full movement envelope during commissioning.

14. Environmental considerations

Cable-routing design must account for the actual operating environment.

Environment Routing consideration
High temperature Cable temperature rating, heat separation, ventilation
Oil and coolant Chemical compatibility and suitable jacket materials
Washdown Appropriate ingress protection and connection systems
Outdoor installation UV resistance, weather protection, drainage, and temperature range
High vibration Suitable supports, strain relief, and connector retention
Dusty areas Enclosure, ingress protection, and contamination control
Welding areas Mechanical protection and assessment of electromagnetic interference
Corrosive environment Suitable materials, glands, supports, and bonding interfaces
Moving machinery Flex-rated cable, controlled movement, and snag prevention
Fire-rated penetrations Approved fire-stopping arrangements and cable-system compatibility

The cable jacket, tray, conduit, glands, supports, and enclosure must all be suitable for the environment.

15. Fire barriers and wall penetrations

Cable routes frequently pass through walls, floors, fire-rated partitions, and equipment enclosures.

Penetrations must preserve the required protection level of the building or machine installation.

Important practices include:

  • Use approved penetration systems.
  • Apply suitable fire stopping where required.
  • Maintain enclosure ingress protection.
  • Prevent sharp-edge damage.
  • Preserve cable support on both sides.
  • Keep cable identification visible where practical.
  • Avoid blocking drainage or other required services.

Fire stopping must be compatible with the cable types, penetration geometry, and applicable fire-resistance requirements.

Do not seal a penetration using an improvised material simply because it fills the gap.

16. Cable route identification and documentation

Cable routes should be traceable from drawings to the physical installation.

Useful documents include:

  • Cable schedule.
  • Cable-route layout.
  • Electrical schematics.
  • Tray and conduit layout.
  • Panel layout.
  • Machine harness drawings.
  • Network topology.
  • Cable termination schedule.
  • Cable-pulling plan.
  • As-built drawings.
  • Cable test and commissioning records.

A cable tag should allow a technician to identify the cable's source, destination, and corresponding drawing.

16.1 Example cable schedule

Cable tag Source Destination Function Route

C-MTR-001 VFD-01 Motor M-01 VFD motor output Shielded motor route

17. Spare capacity and future expansion

Cable routes should provide suitable capacity for expected future changes.

Consider:

  • Spare tray capacity.
  • Conduit fill and pulling feasibility.
  • Additional communication cables.
  • New sensors and actuators.
  • Replacement cable requirements.
  • Separation between cable categories.
  • Access for future installation.
  • Structural loading.
  • Fire stopping and enclosure limits.

Spare capacity should be designed, not assumed from the fact that a tray currently appears half empty.

Do not exceed the permitted loading, fill, or thermal limits in the name of future expansion.

18. Cable-routing inspection and commissioning

18.1 Pre-installation checks

  • Cable schedule and route drawings are approved.
  • Cable type and rating match the design.
  • Cable lengths and equipment limits are verified.
  • Tray, conduit, and support capacity is adequate.
  • Required segregation is identified.
  • Mechanical and environmental hazards are assessed.
  • Dynamic movement requirements are confirmed.
  • Required fire barriers and penetrations are planned.

18.2 Installation checks

  • Cable pulling limits are observed.
  • Bend radii are maintained.
  • Cables are supported and protected.
  • Sharp edges and pinch points are eliminated.
  • Required cable separation is maintained.
  • Shielding and bonding are installed according to the design.
  • Cable tags are fitted.
  • Connectors and glands are secured.
  • Moving cable routes are checked throughout their travel.

18.3 Commissioning checks

  • Cable destinations match the drawings.
  • Required continuity tests are completed.
  • Insulation-resistance tests are performed only where suitable for the connected equipment and approved test procedure.
  • Protective-earth continuity is verified where required.
  • Network links and communication performance are checked.
  • Analog signals are verified under operating conditions.
  • Motor rotation and drive operation are checked using the approved procedure.
  • Cable routes are checked during machine movement.
  • As-built documentation is updated.

Never apply insulation-resistance test voltage to connected PLCs, drives, sensors, encoders, communication ports, or other electronics unless the equipment manufacturer explicitly permits the test arrangement.

20. Practical industrial examples

Example 1: PLC analog input fluctuates when a motor starts

Possible route-related causes include:

  • Analog cable runs parallel to the motor feeder.
  • Cable shielding is terminated incorrectly.
  • Signal return wiring has an unintended reference path.
  • Motor switching creates interference in a nearby route.

Investigation should compare the physical route against the electrical design, inspect shielding and bonding, and verify the signal under controlled operating conditions.

Example 2: CNC encoder faults occur during axis movement

Possible causes include:

  • Incorrect cable type for dynamic movement.
  • Cable bend radius violated at an axis limit.
  • Cable carrier overfilled.
  • Cable snagging or excessive torsion.
  • Damaged connector or shield termination.

Inspect the complete travel range rather than checking only the stationary axis position.

Example 3: Industrial Ethernet drops intermittently

Possible causes include:

  • Cable damage or excessive bending.
  • Connector termination problems.
  • Excessive link length.
  • Poor routing near interference sources.
  • Inappropriate cable or connector for the environment.

Check the physical layer, link diagnostics, cable route, and equipment specifications before assuming the PLC program is responsible.

Example 4: A new sensor installation causes repeated maintenance problems

Possible causes include:

  • Cable routed through a hot or wet area.
  • No strain relief at the sensor.
  • Insufficient mechanical protection.
  • Wrong cable jacket material.
  • Cable route obstructs routine machine maintenance.

The corrective action should address the route and installation design, not just replace the damaged cable repeatedly.

21. Cable-routing standards and engineering references

Applicable requirements depend on the machine, site, installation type, country, and adopted standards.

Commonly relevant standards and guidance include:

  • IEC 60204-1: Electrical equipment of machines.
  • IEC 60364 series: Low-voltage electrical installations.
  • IEC 61439 series: Low-voltage switchgear and controlgear assemblies, where applicable.
  • IEC 61914: Cable cleats for electrical installations.
  • IEC 61000 series: Electromagnetic compatibility.
  • ISO 12100: Machinery safety risk assessment and risk reduction.
  • Manufacturer installation instructions: Cable carriers, drives, motors, connectors, communication systems, and cable glands.

The applicable edition and local adoption must be verified for the project. A standards list is not a substitute for checking the actual requirements governing the installation.

22. Interview questions and answers

1. What is cable routing?

Cable routing is the planned physical path of a cable between its source and destination, designed to meet electrical, mechanical, environmental, EMC, safety, and maintenance requirements.

2. What is the difference between cable tray and conduit?

A cable tray supports and organizes cables along an accessible route. Conduit encloses cables or conductors and provides a defined level of mechanical protection according to its design.

3. Why is bend radius important?

Excessive bending can damage conductors, insulation, shielding, and internal cable geometry. The permitted radius depends on the cable construction and whether the cable is fixed or moving.

4. Why should cable-pulling tension be controlled?

Excessive pulling force can stretch conductors, deform insulation, damage shields, and shorten cable service life.

5. Why should analog cables be routed carefully near VFD cables?

VFD switching and motor-cable currents can introduce electromagnetic interference into sensitive measurement circuits.

6. Why do drag-chain cables require special selection?

They must withstand repeated bending and possibly torsion, acceleration, vibration, and continuous movement that ordinary fixed-installation cables may not tolerate.

7. Why is cable identification important?

It allows technicians to trace circuits, locate faults, identify destinations, and perform maintenance without relying on guesswork.

8. What is the importance of cable-route documentation?

It supports safe installation, troubleshooting, replacement, future expansion, and verification that the physical installation matches the approved design.

9. Can a cable be extended beyond a drive manufacturer's recommended length?

Not without engineering review. Cable length can affect electrical stress, common-mode currents, emissions, and drive operation. Any extension must be checked against the manufacturer's requirements.

10. Why should cable routing be checked during machine movement?

A route that appears correct while stationary may bend excessively, snag, twist, stretch, or collide with moving components during operation.

23. Final mental model

A reliable cable route must satisfy five connected requirements:

  1. Electrical performance: Appropriate conductor sizing, voltage drop, and equipment limits.
  2. EMC: Suitable separation, shielding, bonding, and cable construction.
  3. Mechanical integrity: Correct support, bend radius, pulling tension, and dynamic movement capability.
  4. Environmental protection: Resistance to temperature, coolant, oil, moisture, chemicals, and mechanical hazards.
  5. Maintainability: Clear identification, accessible routes, spare capacity, and accurate documentation.

For industrial maintenance, cable routing is part of the machine's engineered system. A route that prevents damage, reduces interference, allows inspection, and supports future modifications is far more valuable than one that merely connects the two endpoints.
