1. Definition and purpose

Network termination is the process of ending a network cable at a connector, jack, patch panel, terminal, or network device interface so that the cable can form a reliable electrical or optical connection.

In industrial automation, network termination includes preparing, connecting, securing, and testing copper Ethernet cables and fibre-optic links at their intended endpoints.

Correct termination is essential because an incorrectly terminated cable can cause intermittent communication, reduced link speed, packet errors, link drops, or complete network failure.

The main objectives are:

  • Establish reliable electrical or optical continuity.
  • Maintain the transmission performance required by the network.
  • Preserve correct conductor pairing and pin assignments.
  • Maintain shielding and grounding arrangements where specified.
  • Prevent mechanical strain, conductor damage, and connector loosening.
  • Support reliable PLC, HMI, robot, remote I/O, drive, and SCADA communication.
  • Ensure the installation can be tested, labelled, and maintained.

2. Types of network termination

The termination method depends on the cable type, connector, device interface, and installation standard.

Termination type Typical application Key consideration
RJ45 modular plug Copper Ethernet patch cables Correct pinout, cable compatibility, and connector type
RJ45 keystone jack Fixed structured cabling Correct pair termination and punch-down technique
Industrial M12 connector Industrial Ethernet devices Correct coding, pin assignment, sealing, and environmental rating
Field-attachable Ethernet connector On-site cable assembly Follow the manufacturer's conductor preparation and assembly procedure
Patch panel Structured cabling systems Correct port assignment, pair termination, and labelling
Fibre-optic connector Fibre Ethernet links Connector type, polarity, cleanliness, and optical performance
Fibre splice Fibre joint or repair Correct fusion or mechanical splice method and loss verification
SFP or SFP+ transceiver interface Fibre or supported copper uplinks Compatibility, media type, wavelength, and optical budget
Industrial terminal or proprietary interface Vendor-specific communication systems Use the exact pinout and termination requirements for the device

Not every network cable should be terminated using a field-fitted RJ45 plug. Fixed horizontal cabling is often terminated at jacks or patch panels, while suitable patch cords connect those termination points to equipment.

3. Copper Ethernet cable construction

A typical balanced copper Ethernet cable contains four twisted pairs of insulated conductors.

Each pair is twisted to help reduce electromagnetic interference and crosstalk. The cable's transmission performance depends on the conductor geometry, twist rate, pair balance, cable category, connectors, and installation quality.

Common cable categories include:

  • Cat 5e: Commonly used for 1000BASE-T Ethernet when the complete channel meets the applicable requirements.
  • Cat 6: Supports higher performance and may support 10GBASE-T over shorter distances under the applicable conditions.
  • Cat 6A: Designed to support 10GBASE-T over a compliant channel up to 100 metres.
  • Industrial Ethernet cable: Designed for particular industrial conditions, such as oil exposure, vibration, flexing, or harsh environments, depending on the specified cable construction.

Category alone does not guarantee suitability for a particular machine. Check the cable's electrical performance, environmental rating, flexibility requirements, connector compatibility, and the device manufacturer's instructions.

4. Ethernet conductor colour codes

Two common Ethernet wiring schemes are T568A and T568B. They use the same eight conductor positions but arrange the green and orange pairs differently.

Pin T568A conductor T568B conductor
1 White/green White/orange
2 Green Orange
3 White/orange White/green
4 Blue Blue
5 White/blue White/blue
6 Orange Green
7 White/brown White/brown
8 Brown Brown

The pin numbers refer to the standard eight-position modular connector arrangement. Confirm the viewing orientation from the connector or wiring standard before terminating; looking at the plug from the opposite side can reverse the apparent order.

4.1 Straight-through and crossover cables

A conventional straight-through cable uses the same wiring scheme at both ends, such as T568A at both ends or T568B at both ends.

A conventional crossover cable uses T568A at one end and T568B at the other, crossing the relevant transmit and receive pairs for legacy Ethernet interfaces.

Modern equipment often supports Auto MDI-X, which can automatically accommodate straight-through or crossover wiring. However, the installation must still follow the required cabling standard and device specifications.

Important: Never use a different wiring scheme merely to compensate for an unexplained communication fault. Verify the complete cable and interface requirements.

5. Cable preparation before termination

Cable preparation has a direct effect on the final connection quality.

5.1 Inspect the cable

Before cutting or terminating, verify:

  • Correct cable type and category.
  • Correct conductor count and construction.
  • Suitable solid or stranded conductor type for the connector.
  • Appropriate shield construction, if required.
  • Adequate cable length.
  • No crushing, cuts, kinks, or jacket damage.
  • Suitability for the temperature, oil, coolant, vibration, and flexing conditions.

5.2 Prepare the jacket

Use a suitable stripping tool and follow the connector manufacturer's instructions.

Avoid:

  • Cutting into conductor insulation.
  • Nicking copper conductors.
  • Removing more jacket than necessary.
  • Unnecessarily untwisting the pairs.
  • Damaging the foil or braid shield.
  • Cutting the drain wire where the termination design requires it.

Maintain the pair twists as close to the termination as the applicable cabling requirements permit. Excessive untwisting can degrade transmission performance, particularly at higher frequencies.

5.3 Separate the pairs

Arrange the conductors according to the selected pinout.

Verify the order visually before inserting the conductors into the connector. A single pair reversal or misplaced conductor can cause failure or degraded performance.

6. RJ45 plug termination procedure

This procedure applies to compatible field-terminated or modular Ethernet plugs. Connector-specific instructions take precedence.

  1. Confirm that the plug is designed for the cable category, conductor type, conductor gauge, and cable diameter.
  2. Cut the cable end squarely.
  3. Strip the outer jacket to the length specified by the connector manufacturer.
  4. Preserve the pair twists as much as practical.
  5. Arrange the conductors according to T568A or T568B, as specified by the installation.
  6. Verify all eight conductor positions.
  7. Trim the conductors evenly if required by the connector design.
  8. Insert the conductors fully into the plug's contact channels.
  9. Ensure the outer jacket reaches the intended strain-relief or clamp area.
  10. Crimp using the specified tool if the connector requires crimping.
  11. Inspect the completed connector.
  12. Test the cable using an appropriate wire-map and performance test method.

Inspection points

  • Correct conductor sequence.
  • Conductors fully inserted.
  • No damaged or exposed conductor beyond the intended contact area.
  • Jacket retained by the connector's strain relief where designed.
  • Connector latch intact.
  • Shield continuity maintained where the design requires it.
  • No loose components or uneven termination.

Some industrial connectors use screw terminals, insulation-displacement contacts, or other assembly mechanisms instead of conventional crimping. Follow the exact procedure for that connector.

7. Keystone jack and patch-panel termination

Fixed structured cabling is commonly terminated at jacks or patch panels rather than at field-fitted plugs.

7.1 Basic procedure

  1. Verify the cable ID and destination.
  2. Confirm the required T568A or T568B scheme.
  3. Prepare the jacket without damaging the pairs.
  4. Route each pair to its designated colour-coded terminal.
  5. Maintain pair twists as close to the termination as the system requirements permit.
  6. Use the specified punch-down tool or termination mechanism.
  7. Trim excess conductors if the connector design requires it.
  8. Secure the cable and maintain strain relief.
  9. Fit the jack or panel component correctly.
  10. Test wire map and the required channel or link performance.
  11. Apply the port label and update the cable schedule.

7.2 Why pair integrity matters

Ethernet transmission uses balanced differential signalling. Correct pair assignments and pair geometry are essential to preserve balance and control crosstalk.

A cable can show continuity on all eight pins and still fail a higher-performance certification test due to split pairs, excessive untwist, poor connector quality, or other transmission faults.

8. Industrial M12 Ethernet connectors

Industrial Ethernet equipment may use circular M12 connectors, particularly where compactness, sealing, vibration resistance, or environmental protection is required.

Common coding types include:

  • D-coded: Commonly used for certain industrial Ethernet connections, traditionally including 10/100 Mbps applications.
  • X-coded: Used for higher-speed industrial Ethernet connections, including applications supporting 10 Gbps when the complete system is compatible.

The coding type is not interchangeable simply because both connectors are circular M12 connectors.

Termination precautions

  • Verify the coding type and pin assignment.
  • Check the cable and connector compatibility.
  • Use the specified stripping and assembly tools.
  • Maintain shield termination as required.
  • Tighten the coupling nut to the manufacturer's specified torque.
  • Verify the required ingress-protection rating after assembly.
  • Keep seals and mating surfaces clean.
  • Do not twist or pull the cable to compensate for a misaligned connector.
  • Test the completed cable and connection.

Never force an incompatible M12 connector into a device. Mechanical similarity does not guarantee electrical or protocol compatibility.

9. Shielded Ethernet cable termination

Shielded cables may use foil, braid, or combined shielding arrangements. The connector and device must support the intended shielding design.

Correct shield termination helps maintain electromagnetic compatibility (EMC), particularly in industrial environments containing VFDs, servo drives, motors, contactors, and high-current switching equipment.

Good practices

  • Use a connector compatible with the cable's shield construction.
  • Maintain the intended shield contact through the connector.
  • Avoid cutting or folding the shield in a way that defeats the connector design.
  • Preserve the cable's specified bend radius.
  • Follow the machine's equipotential bonding and grounding design.
  • Check shield continuity using an appropriate method.
  • Keep shield connections consistent with the cable and equipment requirements.

Shielding is not a substitute for correct routing, separation from noise sources, proper bonding, or correct cable construction.

Do not arbitrarily connect or disconnect a shield at one end as a general troubleshooting trick. The correct arrangement depends on the system design and EMC requirements.

10. Fibre-optic termination

Fibre termination creates an optical connection between a fibre and a connector or other optical interface.

Fibre systems may use factory-terminated patch cords, field-installable connectors, fusion splices, or mechanical splices. The appropriate method depends on the fibre type, installation, required loss, available equipment, and project specification.

10.1 Fibre connector types

Common connector families include:

  • LC.
  • SC.
  • ST.

The connector must match the installed equipment and optical distribution hardware. The connector family alone does not identify the fibre type, polishing geometry, or optical compatibility.

10.2 Fibre termination procedure

  1. Verify the fibre type and intended route.
  2. Identify the required connector or splice method.
  3. Inspect the cable and confirm the minimum bend radius.
  4. Prepare the fibre using the specified tools.
  5. Clean and inspect the fibre end face.
  6. Complete the connector installation or splice.
  7. Protect the finished connection from dust and mechanical stress.
  8. Clean and inspect mating connector end faces before connection.
  9. Verify polarity and the intended transmit/receive path.
  10. Test insertion loss or other specified optical parameters.
  11. Record the result and label the completed link.

10.3 Fibre safety

  • Never look directly into a fibre or optical connector.
  • Use suitable inspection equipment.
  • Follow the site's laser and optical safety procedures.
  • Handle glass fragments using the approved procedure.
  • Collect fibre scraps in a designated container.
  • Keep protective caps fitted to disconnected connectors.

A fibre link may carry invisible optical energy. The absence of visible light is not proof that the link is inactive.

11. Fibre splicing and optical loss

Fibre splicing joins two optical fibres, commonly using fusion splicing or an approved mechanical splice.

The quality of the splice depends on the fibre type, alignment, end-face preparation, equipment condition, and splice method.

Important concepts include:

  • Insertion loss: Optical power lost through a connection or link.
  • Return loss: A measure of reflected optical power.
  • Fusion splice: A joint made by aligning and fusing fibre ends.
  • Mechanical splice: A joint that aligns fibre ends using a mechanical arrangement.
  • OTDR: Optical Time-Domain Reflectometer, used to analyse fibre events and link characteristics.
  • OLTS: Optical Loss Test Set, used to measure end-to-end optical loss.

An OTDR trace is useful for locating events such as splices, connectors, and faults, but it is not automatically a substitute for the required end-to-end insertion-loss test.

Use the test method and acceptance limits specified for the installed fibre system.

12. Connector compatibility and pinout verification

Correct termination requires more than matching the connector shape.

Verify the following before assembly:

  • Connector type and coding.
  • Pin or contact assignment.
  • Cable category and conductor construction.
  • Solid versus stranded conductor compatibility.
  • Shield construction and termination method.
  • Required transmission speed.
  • Environmental rating.
  • Temperature and chemical resistance.
  • Manufacturer's assembly tools and torque requirements.
  • Compatibility with the actual device interface.

A connector that physically fits may still have an incorrect pin assignment, incompatible media type, or unsuitable performance rating.

13. Cable testing after termination

Testing must verify the characteristics relevant to the cable and its intended application.

13.1 Copper Ethernet testing

Test What it checks
Continuity Whether the expected conductors are electrically connected
Wire map Pin-to-pin mapping, opens, shorts, reversals, and certain pair faults
Length Estimated cable length or conductor-pair length, depending on the tester
Split-pair detection Whether conductors are assigned to the wrong twisted pairs
Insertion loss Signal attenuation through the channel or link
Return loss Reflections caused by impedance discontinuities
NEXT Near-end crosstalk
Cable certification Compliance with the selected category or link-performance specification

A basic continuity tester is useful but cannot certify that a cable meets Cat 5e, Cat 6, Cat 6A, or another performance specification.

Use a cable certifier when the project requires standards-based cabling certification.

13.2 Fibre-optic testing

Depending on the installation specification, fibre tests may include:

  • End-face inspection.
  • Polarity verification.
  • Continuity or light-source verification.
  • Insertion-loss measurement.
  • OTDR testing.
  • Inspection of transceiver and link compatibility.

Use suitable test equipment and follow the required reference method and acceptance limits.

15. Troubleshooting a newly terminated Ethernet cable

Use a controlled sequence rather than replacing components at random.

Verify cable ID and intended endpoints
              ↓
Inspect connector and cable preparation
              ↓
Check physical link status
              ↓
Run wire-map and continuity tests
              ↓
Check cable performance if required
              ↓
Verify switch port and device interface
              ↓
Check VLAN and IP configuration
              ↓
Test the required industrial protocol
              ↓
Record the results and corrective action

Example: PLC link remains down

A PLC has no Ethernet link after a new cable is installed.

  1. Confirm that the PLC and switch are powered and their interfaces are enabled.
  2. Verify the cable is connected to the intended PLC and switch ports.
  3. Inspect both connectors for incomplete insertion or physical damage.
  4. Test the cable using a suitable wire-map tester.
  5. Verify connector pinout and cable compatibility.
  6. Check the switch's link state and relevant interface diagnostics.
  7. Replace or reterminate the cable only when evidence supports a physical fault.
  8. Verify communication after the link is restored.

If the physical link is up but the PLC remains unreachable, continue with VLAN, IP addressing, routing, and application-level checks rather than assuming the termination is defective.

16. Installation quality and environmental considerations

Industrial network terminations must withstand the environment for which they are designed.

Consider:

  • Vibration and mechanical movement.
  • Cable flexing and repeated motion.
  • Oil, coolant, dust, and cleaning chemicals.
  • Temperature extremes.
  • Moisture and ingress protection.
  • Electromagnetic interference.
  • Mechanical strain at connectors.
  • Cable routing and bend radius.
  • Separation from power and high-noise wiring as required by the installation design.

For moving machinery, use cable and connector assemblies rated for the actual flexing conditions. A standard fixed-installation cable should not automatically be used in a drag chain or continuously moving application.

For outdoor or washdown applications, verify the complete mated connector system's environmental rating. A connector's rating may depend on correct mating, seals, and assembly.

17. Termination records and handover

Every completed termination should be traceable to the installed network design.

Maintain the following information as required:

  • Cable ID.
  • Source device and interface.
  • Destination device and interface.
  • Cable and connector type.
  • Pinout or wiring scheme.
  • Shield termination details where applicable.
  • Fibre type, strand, connector, and polarity information where relevant.
  • Test method and results.
  • Technician or contractor identification where required.
  • Installation date.
  • Deviations and corrective actions.
  • Relevant drawing and change references.

A cable that works during initial commissioning but has no identification or test record is harder to maintain and may need to be tested again during a future breakdown.

18. Standards and technical references awareness

Relevant standards depend on the cable system, media, connector, environment, and project specification.

  • ANSI/TIA-568 series: Structured telecommunications cabling requirements, including relevant copper and fibre cabling specifications.
  • ISO/IEC 11801 series: Generic cabling requirements.
  • IEC 61918: Industrial communication networks and installation of communication networks in industrial premises.
  • IEC 61784 series: Industrial communication network profiles and related specifications.
  • IEC 61000 series: Electromagnetic compatibility requirements and test methods.

Confirm the applicable edition and scope before using a standard as an installation acceptance criterion. Connector-specific assembly instructions and project requirements remain essential.

19. Practical commissioning checklist

Before termination

  • Confirm the cable type and destination.
  • Verify the connector is compatible with the cable.
  • Confirm the pinout or fibre polarity requirements.
  • Check the cable for visible damage.
  • Select the correct preparation and termination tools.
  • Confirm any required shield termination arrangement.

During termination

  • Strip the cable without damaging conductors or shielding.
  • Maintain pair twists as required.
  • Follow the correct pin assignment.
  • Ensure conductors are fully seated.
  • Apply strain relief correctly.
  • Maintain connector seals and mechanical retention.
  • Follow fibre cleaning and handling procedures where applicable.

After termination

  • Inspect the completed termination.
  • Verify wire map or fibre polarity.
  • Perform the required cable-performance or optical tests.
  • Check link status at both ends where possible.
  • Verify application communication.
  • Label both endpoints.
  • Record test results and any deviations.
  • Update the cable schedule and network documentation.

20. Interview questions and answers

1. What is network termination?

It is the process of connecting the end of a network cable to a compatible connector, jack, patch panel, or device interface while preserving the required electrical or optical performance.

2. What is the difference between T568A and T568B?

They are two Ethernet conductor arrangements. The main difference is the placement of the green and orange pairs. Both ends of a conventional straight-through cable normally use the same scheme.

3. Why should twisted pairs remain twisted close to the termination?

Maintaining pair geometry helps preserve balance and reduce crosstalk, which affects high-frequency transmission performance.

4. Why can a cable pass a continuity test but still fail Ethernet communication?

Continuity does not prove correct pair construction, acceptable crosstalk, insertion loss, return loss, connector performance, or compliance with the required cabling category.

5. What is the difference between a cable tester and a cable certifier?

A basic tester checks wiring and certain faults. A certifier measures specified transmission parameters against a defined cabling performance standard.

6. What should be checked if a new cable shows no link?

Check device power, port status, connector seating, cable pinout, continuity, cable compatibility, and the physical connection at both ends.

7. Why is shield termination important in industrial Ethernet?

Correct shield termination contributes to EMC performance and helps manage interference in electrically noisy environments.

8. What is the difference between a fibre connector and a fibre splice?

A connector provides a detachable optical interface. A splice joins fibre ends into a continuous optical path, generally without a routinely detachable connection.

9. What is a split pair?

A split pair occurs when conductors are connected with apparent pin-to-pin continuity but do not preserve the intended twisted-pair grouping, degrading transmission performance.

10. Why must connector compatibility be verified?

Connectors may differ in pinout, conductor compatibility, coding, environmental rating, and transmission performance even when they appear mechanically similar.

21. Final engineering principles

  • Use connectors designed for the actual cable and installation environment.
  • Follow the correct pinout and preserve twisted-pair geometry.
  • Maintain shielding and bonding according to the approved EMC design.
  • Protect connectors from mechanical stress and environmental damage.
  • Clean, inspect, and test fibre connections using appropriate procedures.
  • Do not treat a continuity test as proof of full Ethernet performance.
  • Verify the physical link before moving to IP and application-level diagnostics.
  • Label both ends and document the completed connection.
  • Use the required test method and acceptance limits for the project.
  • Record discrepancies and corrective actions so that the installation remains maintainable.

Core principle: A reliable network termination must provide the correct connection, preserve the cable's transmission performance, withstand the operating environment, and be verified through appropriate testing.
