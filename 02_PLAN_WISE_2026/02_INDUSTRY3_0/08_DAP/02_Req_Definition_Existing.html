1. Introduction

Requirement definition and existing-machine study are the foundation of a successful automation project, machine retrofit, reliability improvement, or production-line modification.

Requirement definition establishes what must change, why the change is needed, what outcome is expected, and how success will be measured.

Existing-machine study establishes what is currently installed, how the machine operates, how its control systems interact, what limitations exist, and what constraints must be considered before proposing a solution.

These two activities must be connected. Requirements describe the desired future state, while the existing-machine study establishes the actual starting state.

Business Need / Production Problem
                ↓
Requirement Collection
                ↓
Requirement Analysis and Definition
                ↓
Existing-Machine Study
                ↓
Gap and Constraint Analysis
                ↓
Feasibility and Risk Assessment
                ↓
Proposed Automation Architecture

The order of detailed activities can overlap. Site inspection may reveal missing requirements, while requirement analysis may identify additional information that must be collected from the machine.

2. Requirement definition

A requirement is a documented statement describing a capability, condition, constraint, interface, or outcome that a system must satisfy.

Requirements should be clear enough to guide design, procurement, programming, testing, commissioning, and acceptance.

For example:

  • Weak requirement: "Improve PLC communication."
  • Better requirement: "The PLC shall detect loss of communication with the remote I/O station, generate a diagnostic alarm, and transition the affected machine sequence to its defined safe response."
  • Weak requirement: "Make the machine faster."
  • Better requirement: "Reduce the measured automatic cycle time from the approved baseline to the agreed target without violating quality, process, or safety requirements."

A requirement must describe the required outcome rather than prematurely prescribe an unverified solution.

3. Why requirement definition matters

Poorly defined requirements cause scope changes, incompatible hardware selection, incomplete software, commissioning delays, and disagreements over whether the project is complete.

Requirement definition helps to:

  • Establish a shared understanding between production, maintenance, engineering, quality, safety, and suppliers.
  • Identify mandatory functions and constraints.
  • Separate essential requirements from optional improvements.
  • Define measurable acceptance criteria.
  • Prevent unapproved scope expansion.
  • Support cost and schedule estimation.
  • Create traceability from the original problem to the final test.
  • Clarify ownership of interfaces and responsibilities.
  • Reduce assumptions during design and implementation.

The key principle is that a requirement must be understood before a solution is selected.

4. Sources of requirements

Requirements can originate from several sources.

Source Example Evidence to collect
Production Increase output or reduce cycle time Production records and cycle-time studies
Maintenance Reduce recurring breakdowns Failure history and maintenance logs
Quality Prevent incorrect assembly Defect data and inspection requirements
Safety Improve guarding or interlocking Risk assessment and safety requirements
Engineering Replace obsolete hardware Equipment lifecycle and compatibility information
Management Reduce operating cost Business case and cost baseline
IT/OT Integrate machine data into SCADA Network and cybersecurity requirements
Compliance Meet applicable regulatory requirements Applicable standards and compliance records
Operators Improve usability and fault recovery Operator interviews and observation
Suppliers Define supported interfaces and capabilities Datasheets, manuals, and technical clarification

A statement from one stakeholder is an input, not automatically an approved requirement. It must be evaluated against operational needs, safety obligations, technical feasibility, and project authority.

5. Stakeholder identification

A stakeholder is any person or organization that influences the project, supplies information, approves the design, operates the equipment, maintains it, or is affected by its outcome.

Typical stakeholders include:

  • Production and operations.
  • Maintenance and electrical engineering.
  • Automation and controls engineering.
  • Mechanical engineering.
  • Quality.
  • Safety and EHS.
  • IT/OT and cybersecurity.
  • Procurement.
  • Project management.
  • Equipment manufacturer or system integrator.
  • Plant management.

Different stakeholders may have conflicting priorities. Production may want minimum downtime, maintenance may want standard components, procurement may prioritize cost, and safety may require additional engineering or validation.

The requirement process must resolve these conflicts through documented decisions rather than leaving them to be discovered during commissioning.

6. Requirement collection techniques

6.1 Interviews

Interview operators, maintenance technicians, engineers, supervisors, and equipment owners.

Useful questions include:

  • What problem occurs?
  • When and under what conditions does it occur?
  • How often does it happen?
  • What is the current recovery procedure?
  • What happens when communication or power is lost?
  • Which functions are essential for production?
  • What changes have previously been attempted?
  • What should happen when a fault occurs?

Record actual observations separately from opinions and assumptions.

6.2 Direct observation

Observe the machine during relevant operating modes:

  • Manual operation.
  • Automatic cycle.
  • Setup and changeover.
  • Startup and shutdown.
  • Fault recovery.
  • Maintenance access.
  • Product or recipe changes.

Where safe and authorized, observe the actual sequence and record timings, operator interactions, and system responses.

6.3 Historical data analysis

Review:

  • Breakdown reports.
  • PLC fault logs.
  • CNC alarms.
  • HMI alarm history.
  • Downtime records.
  • Quality defects.
  • Cycle-time data.
  • Preventive maintenance records.
  • Spare-parts consumption.
  • Energy and utility data.

Historical records help distinguish isolated incidents from recurring patterns.

6.4 Document review

Review available electrical drawings, PLC backups, I/O lists, sequence descriptions, manuals, network drawings, software versions, and previous modification records.

Do not assume the documentation is correct simply because it exists. Verify important information against the installed system.

6.5 Measurement and testing

Measurements may include cycle time, sensor response, voltage, current, communication performance, temperature, vibration, or other process variables.

Testing must follow approved procedures and safety controls. Do not introduce faults or interrupt equipment operation without authorization.

7. Types of requirements

7.1 Functional requirements

Describe what the system must do.

Examples:

  • Start the automatic cycle only when the required permissives are satisfied.
  • Select the correct robot program based on the validated part identifier.
  • Generate an alarm when a required sensor signal is not received within the defined timeout.
  • Record production counts and fault events.

7.2 Performance requirements

Describe measurable performance.

Examples:

  • Cycle time.
  • Throughput.
  • Response time.
  • Data update rate.
  • Recovery time.
  • Availability target.

Performance targets should have defined measurement methods, operating conditions, and acceptance limits.

7.3 Interface requirements

Describe how the system exchanges signals, data, material, or commands with other systems.

Examples:

  • PLC-to-robot handshake.
  • PLC-to-CNC communication.
  • HMI-to-PLC tag mapping.
  • SCADA data exchange.
  • Remote I/O communication.
  • Barcode scanner or printer integration.

7.4 Safety requirements

Describe safety-related conditions that must be met.

Examples include guard interlocking, emergency-stop behavior, prevention of unexpected restart, safe access for maintenance, and required safety-function performance.

These requirements must be derived from the applicable risk assessment, machine design, and relevant standards. Ordinary control logic is not automatically a substitute for a safety-rated function.

7.5 Reliability and maintainability requirements

Examples:

  • Fault diagnostics must identify the affected station or device.
  • Critical replacement components must be identified.
  • Required PLC and HMI backups must be available.
  • Recovery procedures must be documented.
  • Maintenance access must be adequate.
  • Obsolete components must be addressed in the proposed design.

7.6 Data and cybersecurity requirements

Examples:

  • Defined PLC tags must be available to an approved data-collection system.
  • Network access must be authorized.
  • Required user roles and access controls must be implemented.
  • Software versions and configuration changes must be controlled.
  • Data retention and time synchronization must meet project requirements.

7.7 Constraints

Constraints limit the available solutions.

Examples include:

  • Existing machine footprint.
  • Existing mechanical interfaces.
  • Approved manufacturers.
  • Limited shutdown time.
  • Required production continuity.
  • Existing network architecture.
  • Budget limits.
  • Software compatibility.
  • Applicable plant standards.

8. Requirement quality and measurability

A good requirement should be:

  • Clear: It has one understandable interpretation.
  • Specific: It identifies the required behavior or condition.
  • Verifiable: Compliance can be demonstrated through inspection, analysis, or testing.
  • Traceable: It has a unique identifier and links to its source.
  • Feasible: It can be achieved within the approved constraints.
  • Consistent: It does not conflict with another approved requirement.
  • Necessary: It addresses a genuine need or obligation.

Avoid words such as "fast," "user-friendly," "robust," and "easy to maintain" unless they are accompanied by defined criteria.

For example, "The alarm screen must be user-friendly" is difficult to verify. A stronger requirement could specify that each fault must display an alarm identifier, equipment tag, description, timestamp, and approved recovery guidance.

9. Requirement specification structure

A requirement register is useful for controlling the requirement set.

Field Purpose
Requirement ID Unique identifier
Requirement statement Defines the required behavior or condition
Category Functional, performance, safety, interface, or other
Source Identifies the stakeholder or document
Priority Mandatory, important, or optional according to the project scheme
Rationale Explains why the requirement exists
Verification method Test, inspection, analysis, or demonstration
Acceptance criteria Defines the condition for passing
Owner Identifies the responsible person
Status Draft, reviewed, approved, changed, or closed
Related requirement Records dependencies or relationships

Example:

ID Requirement Verification Acceptance criterion
FR-001 Machine shall prevent automatic start when a required permissive is false Functional test Automatic start is inhibited under each specified test condition
IF-001 PLC shall exchange the agreed handshake signals with the robot controller Interface test All defined signals and timeout responses behave as specified
AL-001 Machine shall generate a diagnostic alarm on the specified communication failure Fault-injection test under an approved procedure Correct alarm appears within the specified time
BK-001 Final PLC project backup shall be supplied Document inspection and restore verification Approved project version is delivered and the restore procedure is demonstrated as required

The IDs and examples are illustrative. Actual acceptance criteria must be defined and approved for the project.

10. Requirement traceability

Requirement traceability connects each requirement to its origin, design implementation, verification activity, and final result.

Requirement
     ↓
Design Element
     ↓
Hardware / Software Implementation
     ↓
Test Case
     ↓
Test Result
     ↓
Acceptance and Closure

For example:

  • Requirement: Detect loss of communication to remote I/O.
  • Design element: PLC diagnostic logic and communication status monitoring.
  • Implementation: Diagnostic tag, alarm handling, and defined response.
  • Test case: Simulate or safely introduce the specified communication-loss condition.
  • Result: Record detection time, alarm, and system response.
  • Closure: Confirm that the result meets the approved acceptance criteria.

Traceability prevents requirements from being forgotten and prevents unverified functions from being treated as complete.

11. Existing-machine study

An existing-machine study is a structured assessment of the installed machine or production system before modification.

It establishes the current configuration, actual behavior, limitations, interfaces, risks, and available evidence.

Its purpose is not merely to copy the existing drawings. It must establish whether the documented design matches the physical machine and identify details that could affect the proposed modification.

The study normally includes electrical, mechanical, control, software, network, safety, operational, maintenance, and documentation aspects.

12. Prepare for the site study

Before visiting the machine:

  1. Identify the equipment and the scope of the investigation.
  2. Review available drawings, manuals, and previous modifications.
  3. Agree on access with production and maintenance.
  4. Define required operating conditions and observations.
  5. Prepare a checklist and data-recording format.
  6. Identify required instruments and authorized personnel.
  7. Establish isolation, permit, and safety requirements.
  8. Determine which data can be collected without interrupting production.

Never open energized panels, bypass guards, force outputs, disconnect network cables, or create machine faults without the appropriate authorization and safe work procedure.

13. Machine identification and physical condition

Record the machine identity and its installed configuration.

Typical information includes:

  • Machine name and asset number.
  • Manufacturer and model.
  • Serial number.
  • Installation year, if known.
  • Production line and station.
  • Control-panel identifiers.
  • PLC and HMI models.
  • CNC, robot, and drive models.
  • Safety controller or relay models.
  • Remote I/O stations.
  • Network switches and communication modules.
  • Major sensors and actuators.
  • Relevant firmware and software versions.

Also inspect the physical condition where authorized:

  • Signs of overheating.
  • Damaged cables or connectors.
  • Loose or corroded terminals.
  • Poor labeling.
  • Obsolete or discontinued components.
  • Unused or undocumented wiring.
  • Panel ventilation problems.
  • Inadequate maintenance access.
  • Missing drawings or component labels.

Record findings with photographs only when permitted by site rules. Each photograph should be linked to the equipment tag, location, date, and relevant observation.

14. Existing electrical-system study

Review the electrical distribution, control power, field wiring, protective devices, and grounding arrangements.

14.1 Power and protection

Identify:

  • Incoming supply characteristics.
  • Control-power supplies.
  • Circuit breakers and fuses.
  • Motor starters and drives.
  • Overload protection.
  • Isolation devices.
  • Earthing and bonding.
  • Control-transformer or power-supply ratings where applicable.

Check whether the installed protection and wiring match the available drawings and the applicable requirements.

14.2 Control wiring

Review:

  • Terminal blocks.
  • Wire numbers.
  • Cable tags.
  • Digital and analog signals.
  • Shielding and grounding arrangements.
  • Separation of power and signal circuits.
  • Field-device power supplies.
  • Safety-related wiring.
  • Spare terminals and unused cores.

Do not infer signal voltage, polarity, or safety function from wire color alone. Verify the circuit drawings, component datasheets, and approved measurement procedures.

15. Existing PLC and I/O study

The PLC study establishes the installed controller, hardware configuration, program organization, signal mapping, and communication interfaces.

15.1 PLC hardware

Record:

  • Manufacturer and CPU model.
  • Firmware revision.
  • Installed rack or station configuration.
  • Power supplies.
  • Digital and analog I/O modules.
  • Communication modules.
  • Remote I/O adapters.
  • Memory and capacity constraints.
  • Available spare channels.
  • Engineering software and project version.

15.2 Software configuration

Where access is authorized, review:

  • Program organization.
  • Task or scan configuration.
  • Sequence logic.
  • Interlocks and permissives.
  • Timers and counters.
  • Alarm handling.
  • Data blocks, tags, and symbols.
  • Communication configuration.
  • Fault recovery and reset logic.
  • Retentive data.
  • Passwords, licenses, and access arrangements according to site policy.
  • Backup availability and version history.

Do not modify the live program merely to understand it. Collect a controlled backup where permitted, record its source and timestamp, and distinguish the uploaded project from any offline file whose correspondence to the installed controller has not been verified.

15.3 I/O inventory

An I/O list should identify each signal's tag, description, signal type, source or destination, channel address, electrical characteristics, and functional use.

Example:

Tag Description Type Source or destination Study focus
DI_001 Part present Digital input Proximity sensor Voltage, polarity, logic, and location
DI_002 Clamp extended Digital input Limit switch Sequence use and fault behavior
DO_001 Clamp extend Digital output Valve solenoid Output rating and interlocks
AI_001 Pressure feedback Analog input Pressure transmitter Range, scaling, and signal quality
AO_001 Speed reference Analog output Drive Range, scaling, and command behavior

The tags are illustrative. The actual naming convention must follow the plant standard.

16. HMI, CNC, robot, and drive study

Existing-machine analysis must include the connected systems that influence machine operation.

16.1 HMI

Inspect:

  • Screen hierarchy.
  • Manual and automatic controls.
  • Alarm screens.
  • Setpoints and recipe handling.
  • User access levels.
  • Maintenance screens.
  • Diagnostic messages.
  • Operator instructions.
  • PLC communication status.

Identify missing diagnostics, unclear messages, unauthorized access risks, and inconsistent operating behavior.

16.2 CNC

Record:

  • CNC manufacturer and model.
  • Control software version where available.
  • PLC/PMC interface signals.
  • M-code functions.
  • Cycle-start and completion signals.
  • Alarm interfaces.
  • Machine-builder documentation.
  • Existing communication interfaces.

16.3 Robot

Record:

  • Robot manufacturer and controller.
  • Program-selection method.
  • PLC-to-robot handshake.
  • Start, ready, busy, complete, and fault signals.
  • Part and recipe data exchange.
  • Safety-zone interfaces.
  • Communication-loss response.
  • Recovery and reset sequence.

16.4 VFD and servo systems

Record:

  • Drive or servo model.
  • Motor rating.
  • Control mode.
  • Speed or position reference.
  • Command and status words.
  • Actual speed or position feedback.
  • Fault codes.
  • Communication interface.
  • Relevant safety functions.
  • Parameter backup availability.

The objective is to understand how each subsystem contributes to the overall machine sequence and where the proposed modification may affect it.

17. Existing network and communication study

Prepare a current network topology showing how controllers, HMIs, remote I/O, drives, robots, CNCs, switches, and supervisory systems communicate.

HMI / Engineering Station
           |
      Industrial Switch
           |
          PLC
       /   |    \
 Remote  Drive  Robot
 I/O             Controller
           |
      CNC / Other
      Interface

This is a conceptual example, not a recommended universal topology. The actual drawing must reflect the installed architecture.

Collect:

  • Device names and addresses.
  • Communication protocols.
  • Switch models and port assignments.
  • Network segmentation.
  • Redundant paths, if present.
  • Communication update requirements.
  • Existing diagnostics.
  • Known intermittent faults.
  • Approved remote-access arrangements.
  • Dependencies on plant IT/OT infrastructure.

Avoid exposing passwords, private keys, or other secrets in the study report. Store sensitive configuration information only through approved plant processes.

18. Existing safety-system study

Safety assessment must determine which safety functions exist and how the proposed change could affect them.

Review:

  • Emergency-stop devices.
  • Guard switches and interlocks.
  • Safety relays or safety PLCs.
  • Robot safety zones.
  • Light curtains and scanners.
  • Drive safety functions.
  • Safety reset arrangements.
  • Restart prevention.
  • Safety-related communication.
  • Existing risk assessment and validation records.

Identify undocumented modifications, inconsistent drawings, missing validation evidence, and differences between the actual installation and the approved safety design.

Do not assume that a function is safety-rated because it stops a machine under normal conditions. Its architecture, components, performance requirements, and validation evidence must be established.

Any proposed change affecting a safety function requires the appropriate impact assessment, authorization, and verification or validation before the modified equipment is released.

19. Sequence and operating-mode study

Document the actual machine sequence in a form that engineering and production can review.

For each step, identify:

  • Entry conditions.
  • Required commands.
  • Expected feedback.
  • Timeout limits.
  • Completion criteria.
  • Fault conditions.
  • Operator indications.
  • Reset behavior.
  • Recovery behavior.
  • Interlocks preventing invalid transitions.

Also study:

  • Manual mode.
  • Automatic mode.
  • Setup mode.
  • Maintenance mode.
  • Changeover.
  • Startup and shutdown.
  • Power-loss recovery.
  • Communication-loss recovery.
  • Interrupted-cycle recovery.

20. Performance and reliability baseline

Before improving a machine, measure its existing performance.

Useful baseline measures include:

  • Cycle time.
  • Production output.
  • Availability.
  • Unplanned downtime.
  • Mean time between failures (MTBF).
  • Mean time to repair (MTTR).
  • Quality defect rate.
  • Scrap and rework.
  • Changeover time.
  • Energy consumption.
  • Repeated alarm frequency.

20.1 Availability

A simple availability calculation is:

\[ A = \frac{T\_{\text{operating}}}{T\_{\text{operating}} + T\_{\text{downtime}}} \times 100\\% \]

For example, if a machine operates for 450 minutes and experiences 30 minutes of downtime during the defined observation window:

\[ A = \frac{450}{450+30} \times 100\\% = 93.75\\% \]

The observation period and downtime definition must be consistent. Planned downtime, breaks, changeovers, and minor stops may be classified differently by the plant's reporting method.

20.2 MTTR

A basic repair-time calculation is:

\[ MTTR = \frac{\text{Total repair time}}{\text{Number of repairs}} \]

If four recorded repairs require a combined 240 minutes, the average repair time is 60 minutes per repair.

Ensure the records distinguish active repair time from waiting for spares, vendor support, or production access if those delays are important to the analysis.

20.3 Cycle-time study

Measure the same defined cycle under comparable operating conditions.

Record:

  • Cycle start and end events.
  • Product or recipe.
  • Operating mode.
  • Machine configuration.
  • Number of observations.
  • Outliers and interruptions.
  • Relevant environmental or process conditions.

A single cycle-time measurement is rarely sufficient to establish a reliable baseline.

21. Gap analysis

Gap analysis compares the current state with the required future state.

Area Existing condition Required condition Identified gap
PLC Unsupported controller Supported controller Hardware migration required
Diagnostics Generic fault message Device-specific diagnostics Software and HMI changes required
Data collection No approved production-data interface Defined SCADA data interface Network and data mapping required
Documentation Outdated wiring drawings Verified as-built drawings Drawing survey and update required
Recovery Manual troubleshooting without clear guidance Defined diagnostic and recovery procedure Software and maintenance documentation required

A gap is not automatically a reason to replace equipment. Some gaps can be resolved through configuration, documentation, repair, training, or limited modification.

22. Distinguish symptoms from root causes

An existing-machine study should avoid jumping from a symptom directly to a proposed component replacement.

For example:

  • Symptom: Remote I/O communication drops intermittently.
  • Possible causes: Loose connector, poor power quality, incorrect network configuration, damaged cable, failing switch, electromagnetic interference, or a defective adapter.
  • Evidence required: Diagnostic logs, physical inspection, configuration review, power measurements, and approved testing.
  • Proposed action: Selected only after the evidence supports the cause.

A useful analysis method is the 5 Whys, in which the investigator repeatedly asks why the observed problem occurred until a sufficiently supported underlying cause is identified.

However, the number of questions is not a substitute for evidence. Some faults have several contributing causes and require more structured analysis.

Clearly distinguish verified facts, stakeholder statements, assumptions, and recommendations. This prevents uncertain information from silently becoming a design requirement.

24. Practical example: planning a PLC retrofit

Assume a machine has an obsolete PLC, intermittent communication faults, incomplete drawings, and a requirement to connect selected machine data to a local SCADA system.

Step 1: Define the requirements

Document the required machine functions, performance expectations, interfaces, diagnostic behavior, safety constraints, data points, and acceptance criteria.

Step 2: Study the existing system

Inspect the PLC hardware, program, I/O mapping, electrical drawings, HMI, network, drives, CNC or robot interfaces, and existing backups.

Step 3: Establish the baseline

Collect communication fault history, downtime, current cycle-time data, production constraints, and the installed hardware configuration.

Step 4: Identify gaps

Compare the current system with the required future state. Identify obsolete hardware, missing documentation, unsupported interfaces, network constraints, and unresolved safety impacts.

Step 5: Evaluate alternatives

Possible alternatives may include:

  • Repairing the existing system.
  • Replacing only the failed or obsolete components.
  • Migrating to a supported PLC.
  • Retrofitting the PLC and HMI together.
  • Adding a separate, approved data-collection interface.

Each alternative should be evaluated for technical feasibility, cost, lifecycle support, production risk, maintainability, and acceptance requirements.

Step 6: Create traceable outputs

The completed requirement definition and machine study should provide the inputs for the proposed architecture, equipment specification, DAP, risk assessment, and test plan.

25. Common mistakes

  • Defining the solution before understanding the problem.
  • Collecting requirements only from management and ignoring operators or maintenance.
  • Treating assumptions as confirmed facts.
  • Copying old drawings without checking the installed machine.
  • Ignoring undocumented field modifications.
  • Studying the PLC but overlooking robots, CNCs, drives, and network dependencies.
  • Failing to capture fault history and performance baseline.
  • Writing requirements that cannot be tested.
  • Omitting safety and cybersecurity constraints.
  • Assuming the original program backup matches the live controller.
  • Recommending replacement without comparing feasible alternatives.
  • Failing to document open questions and missing evidence.
  • Ignoring recovery and maintenance requirements.
  • Starting implementation before the necessary requirements and technical constraints are sufficiently understood.

26. Interview questions and answers

Q1. What is requirement definition?

It is the process of converting stakeholder needs, operational problems, and applicable obligations into clear, approved, traceable, and verifiable requirements.

Q2. Why is existing-machine study necessary before a retrofit?

It establishes the actual installed configuration, operating behavior, interfaces, constraints, faults, and documentation gaps that determine whether a proposed modification will work.

Q3. What is the difference between a functional requirement and a performance requirement?

A functional requirement defines what the system must do. A performance requirement defines how well, how quickly, or under what measurable operating conditions it must do it.

Q4. What is requirement traceability?

It links a requirement to its source, design implementation, verification activity, test evidence, and final acceptance.

Q5. Why should the live PLC program not be changed during an initial study?

Uncontrolled changes can alter machine behavior, introduce safety or production risks, and compromise the evidence needed to understand the existing system. Changes must follow authorized engineering and change-control procedures.

Q6. What should be recorded during a PLC inventory?

The controller model, firmware, modules, I/O configuration, communication interfaces, software version, relevant diagnostics, and backup status.

Q7. What is the purpose of a performance baseline?

It provides a documented reference for determining whether the proposed improvement achieved its intended operational result.

Q8. What is gap analysis?

It compares the current system with the required future state and identifies missing capabilities, technical deficiencies, constraints, and necessary actions.

Q9. How can an engineer avoid selecting the wrong corrective action?

Collect evidence, distinguish symptoms from root causes, evaluate alternatives, and verify assumptions before committing to a design or component replacement.

Q10. What are the key outputs of requirement definition and existing-machine study?

An approved or reviewed requirement register, existing-system inventory, drawings and interface findings, performance baseline, risk and constraint register, gap analysis, and documented inputs for the proposed architecture.

27. Final checklist

Requirement definition

  • The business or technical problem is documented.
  • Stakeholders and requirement sources are identified.
  • Functional and non-functional requirements are separated.
  • Constraints, exclusions, and assumptions are recorded.
  • Requirements are measurable and verifiable.
  • Safety and cybersecurity obligations are identified.
  • Acceptance criteria and verification methods are defined.
  • Requirements are traceable and controlled.

Existing-machine study

  • Machine identity and installed hardware are recorded.
  • Electrical drawings and wiring have been checked.
  • PLC, HMI, CNC, robot, and drive interfaces are understood.
  • I/O mapping and communication architecture are documented.
  • Safety functions and relevant validation records are reviewed.
  • Operating sequences and recovery behavior are understood.
  • Fault history and performance baselines are collected.
  • Existing backups and software versions are assessed.
  • Documentation gaps and undocumented changes are recorded.
  • Assumptions and open questions are clearly identified.

Readiness for the next stage

  • Current-state findings are reviewed.
  • Requirement gaps are identified.
  • Technical and operational constraints are known.
  • Feasible alternatives can be evaluated.
  • The next-stage architecture and equipment specification can be developed against a defined requirement set.

28. Key takeaway

Requirement definition explains what the system must achieve. Existing-machine study establishes what is installed, how it behaves, and what constraints exist. Together, they provide the evidence needed to select a suitable automation architecture and specify equipment without relying on guesswork.
