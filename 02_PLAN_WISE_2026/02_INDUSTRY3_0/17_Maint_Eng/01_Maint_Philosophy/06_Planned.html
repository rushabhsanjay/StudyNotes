1. Definition

Planned maintenance is maintenance work that has been prepared in advance by defining the required scope, method, resources, materials, responsibilities, and intended execution window before the work begins.

The purpose is to reduce uncertainty during execution, improve work quality, use resources efficiently, and minimize avoidable equipment downtime.

Examples include:

  • Scheduling a motor bearing replacement during a planned production shutdown.
  • Preparing tools, spare parts, permits, and manpower for a VFD replacement.
  • Planning an overhaul of a hydraulic power pack.
  • Arranging a PLC or CNC backup before authorized hardware replacement.
  • Scheduling electrical panel cleaning and inspection.
  • Preparing a maintenance order for a recurring conveyor fault.
  • Planning a sensor replacement after a condition-monitoring alert.
  • Organizing a machine relocation or control-system retrofit.

Planned maintenance is primarily about preparing and organizing maintenance work before execution. It does not, by itself, specify why the work is required.

The maintenance trigger may be a preventive schedule, condition-based finding, predictive warning, corrective defect, statutory requirement, or another approved need.

2. Objectives of Planned Maintenance

The main objectives are:

  • Reduce delays caused by missing parts, tools, information, or manpower.
  • Improve the quality and consistency of maintenance work.
  • Reduce the time equipment remains unavailable.
  • Coordinate maintenance with production schedules.
  • Improve safety through advance hazard assessment and permit preparation.
  • Ensure correct technical documents and procedures are available.
  • Arrange competent personnel and specialist support.
  • Estimate labor, material, and service costs.
  • Improve spare-parts availability.
  • Reduce repeat work caused by incomplete preparation.
  • Improve work-order history and maintenance performance analysis.
  • Provide a clear basis for scheduling, execution, and verification.

Planned maintenance does not guarantee that a repair will be successful or that no unexpected problem will occur. It creates the conditions for work to be performed efficiently and correctly.

3. Planned Maintenance Versus Maintenance Triggers

Maintenance strategy and work planning are related but different concepts.

Term Meaning Example
Preventive maintenance Work triggered by a predetermined time or usage interval Replace a filter every specified number of operating hours
Condition-based maintenance Work triggered by an observed condition reaching an approved criterion Replace a filter when differential pressure reaches its intervention limit
Predictive maintenance Work informed by analysis of condition trends and likely future deterioration Schedule bearing replacement based on a validated degradation assessment
Corrective maintenance Work intended to correct an identified defect Repair a damaged sensor cable
Emergency maintenance Urgent work required to address an immediate failure or unacceptable risk Respond to a failed critical cooling system
Planned maintenance Work prepared in advance before execution Arrange a repair package with parts, labor, permits, and a shutdown window
Scheduled maintenance Work assigned to a specific date, time, shift, or execution window Perform the prepared repair on Saturday's shutdown

A maintenance task can be both corrective and planned. For example, a defective proximity sensor may be identified today, and its replacement may be prepared and scheduled for the next approved production break.

Similarly, preventive maintenance can be planned and scheduled. These terms describe different aspects of the maintenance process rather than competing strategies.

4. Planned Maintenance Workflow

A typical planned maintenance workflow is:

Maintenance Need Identified
        ↓
Notification or Work Request Created
        ↓
Scope and Technical Requirements Defined
        ↓
Risk and Safety Requirements Assessed
        ↓
Labor, Tools, Parts, and Documents Identified
        ↓
Work Package Prepared
        ↓
Resources and Equipment Availability Confirmed
        ↓
Work Scheduled and Authorized
        ↓
Maintenance Executed
        ↓
Testing and Verification Completed
        ↓
Records Updated and Work Closed
        ↓
Results Reviewed for Improvement

The stages may overlap depending on the site's maintenance system, but the responsibilities should remain clear.

4.1 Identify the maintenance need

The work may originate from:

  • A preventive maintenance schedule.
  • A condition-monitoring alert.
  • A breakdown or recurring fault.
  • An operator inspection.
  • An audit finding.
  • A safety observation.
  • A statutory inspection.
  • An engineering modification.
  • An obsolescence or reliability improvement.
  • A manufacturer recommendation.

The initial request should identify the asset, location, observed problem or required task, operational impact, and urgency.

4.2 Define the work scope

The scope states exactly what the maintenance team must accomplish.

A vague scope such as "repair conveyor" is inadequate for effective planning.

A better scope may identify the conveyor section, the observed defect, the required inspection, the affected components, the expected repair, and the verification required before return to service.

The scope should also identify exclusions or boundaries where they affect responsibility or safety.

4.3 Assess risk and safety requirements

Before work is authorized, identify relevant hazards and controls.

These may include:

  • Electrical isolation.
  • Lockout/tagout or the applicable energy-control procedure.
  • Stored electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic, mechanical, or gravitational energy.
  • Hot surfaces.
  • Rotating machinery.
  • Work at height.
  • Lifting operations.
  • Chemical exposure.
  • Confined spaces where applicable.
  • Robot or CNC movement hazards.
  • Process and production hazards.
  • Required permits and competency.

Risk assessment and isolation planning must be appropriate to the task. A planned work order does not replace the site's formal safety procedures.

4.4 Identify resources

Determine the required:

  • Personnel and competencies.
  • Spare parts and consumables.
  • Tools and measuring instruments.
  • Test equipment.
  • Drawings and manuals.
  • Software, licenses, and configuration files.
  • External vendors or specialist support.
  • Production shutdown or access window.
  • Permits and approvals.

A task should not be considered ready merely because it appears on the schedule.

5. Work Orders and Work Packages

A work order authorizes and tracks maintenance work within the site's maintenance system.

A work package is the collection of information and resources needed to execute that work. It may be attached to the work order or stored in an approved document system.

A well-prepared work package typically contains:

  • Equipment identification.
  • Work-order or notification number.
  • Problem description or maintenance objective.
  • Defined scope.
  • Technical procedure or work instructions.
  • Risk assessment and safety controls.
  • Required isolation points.
  • Labor and competency requirements.
  • Material and spare-parts list.
  • Tools and test equipment.
  • Drawings and configuration references.
  • Estimated duration.
  • Production coordination requirements.
  • Acceptance criteria.
  • Testing and commissioning steps.
  • Documentation and closeout requirements.

The level of detail should match the complexity and risk of the task. A simple filter replacement does not require the same documentation package as a PLC migration or critical machine retrofit.

6. Work Planning and Work Scheduling

Planning and scheduling are related, but they are not identical.

Work planning determines what must be done and how the work will be executed.

Work scheduling determines when the work will be performed and coordinates it with available resources and operational constraints.

Planning activity Scheduling activity
Define the technical scope Assign the execution date or shift
Estimate labor hours Check personnel availability
Identify materials and tools Confirm parts are available by the required date
Define safety and testing requirements Coordinate permits and production access
Prepare procedures and drawings Sequence work with other maintenance activities
Estimate work duration Allocate a shutdown window
Identify vendor support Confirm vendor attendance
Define acceptance criteria Confirm the work can be completed within the allocated window

A technically complete work package may still be impossible to execute on the desired date because the production line is unavailable, a specialist is absent, or a critical spare has not arrived.

Good planning identifies these constraints early rather than allowing them to become surprises during the shutdown.

7. Work Readiness and the Ready-to-Schedule Concept

A work order should be assessed for readiness before it is placed into a committed execution schedule.

A task may be considered ready when the essential requirements are confirmed:

  • Scope is clear and technically understood.
  • Required procedures and drawings are available.
  • Risk assessment and safety requirements are defined.
  • Required materials are available or confirmed for the planned date.
  • Tools and test instruments are available and suitable.
  • Competent personnel are identified.
  • Vendor support is confirmed where required.
  • Equipment access and production availability are agreed.
  • Expected duration and dependencies are understood.
  • Testing and acceptance criteria are defined.

Some organizations distinguish between planned, ready to schedule, scheduled, and in progress. Exact status names depend on the maintenance system.

The important principle is that incomplete work should not be presented as fully ready. Otherwise, the schedule becomes a list of intentions rather than an executable plan.

8. Maintenance Backlog

The maintenance backlog is the set of identified maintenance work that has not yet been completed, usually tracked according to defined work-order statuses.

Backlog can include:

  • Work awaiting technical planning.
  • Work awaiting materials.
  • Work awaiting scheduling.
  • Ready work awaiting an execution window.
  • Work delayed by production constraints.
  • Work requiring vendor support.

Backlog is not automatically bad. A controlled backlog helps maintenance teams organize work and allocate resources. An unmanaged backlog can hide urgent defects, increase risk, and make planning unreliable.

8.1 Backlog prioritization

Prioritization should consider:

  • Safety and environmental consequences.
  • Production impact.
  • Equipment criticality.
  • Product-quality impact.
  • Risk of further damage.
  • Statutory deadlines.
  • Condition severity.
  • Availability of a suitable maintenance window.
  • Required lead time for parts and services.

A low-risk improvement should not displace an urgent safety-related defect merely because the improvement is easier to complete.

8.2 Backlog aging

Backlog aging measures how long work has remained open or incomplete.

Aging analysis can reveal:

  • Long material lead times.
  • Repeatedly deferred maintenance.
  • Inadequate planning capacity.
  • Poorly defined work scopes.
  • Unavailable production windows.
  • Work that is no longer required but remains open.
  • Resource shortages.

Old work should be reviewed for continued validity, changed risk, and possible changes in scope.

9. Work Priority Versus Work Status

Priority and status answer different questions.

  • Priority: How urgently should the work be addressed?
  • Status: What stage has the work reached?

For example, a high-priority work order may still be awaiting a critical spare part. Its status does not make its underlying risk disappear.

Priority concept Example Planning implication
Emergency Immediate failure or unacceptable risk Follow emergency response and authorization procedures
Urgent Significant risk or imminent operational consequence Expedite technical assessment, resources, and execution
Normal Routine repair or maintenance need Include in the normal planning and scheduling process
Low Improvement with limited immediate consequence Schedule when resources and access are available

These labels are illustrative. Each site should define its own priority categories, escalation rules, and response expectations.

Priority should be based on risk and operational consequence, not simply on who submitted the request most recently or most loudly.

10. Labor Planning and Work Duration

Labor planning estimates the personnel and time required to complete the task safely and correctly.

10.1 Work-duration estimate

The estimate may include:

  • Preparation and pre-job briefing.
  • Isolation and verification.
  • Disassembly.
  • Inspection and diagnosis.
  • Repair or replacement.
  • Reassembly.
  • Testing and commissioning.
  • Documentation.
  • Restoration of the work area.
  • Handover to operations.

Elapsed duration and labor hours are different.

For example, a task lasting four hours with two technicians may require approximately eight labor-hours, excluding other supporting activities.

\[ \text{Labor-hours} = \text{Number of workers} \times \text{Hours worked per person} \]

The equation is a basic estimate. Actual labor accounting should follow the site's rules for breaks, travel, waiting time, and indirect work.

10.2 Skill requirements

Identify the required competence before assigning the work.

A PLC program modification may require an authorized controls engineer, while a mechanical alignment task may require a technician trained in the relevant procedure.

Sending an unqualified person to a task does not make the task cheaper. It often converts a planned repair into a more expensive diagnostic exercise.

10.3 Estimate uncertainty

Work duration may be uncertain when the defect is not fully understood.

In such cases:

  • Separate confirmed work from diagnostic work.
  • Identify likely contingencies.
  • Prepare for plausible additional defects.
  • Establish escalation and authorization rules.
  • Avoid presenting uncertain estimates as guarantees.

The plan should make uncertainty visible instead of hiding it inside an arbitrary duration.

11. Spare Parts and Material Readiness

Material availability is one of the most common constraints on maintenance execution.

11.1 Material planning

Identify:

  • Manufacturer and part number.
  • Equipment compatibility.
  • Required quantity.
  • Available stock.
  • Lead time.
  • Obsolescence status.
  • Approved successor part.
  • Required configuration or firmware compatibility.
  • Special storage requirements.
  • Vendor or repair-service requirements.

A visually similar component is not necessarily a valid replacement. Electrical ratings, interface compatibility, mechanical dimensions, firmware, safety approvals, and environmental specifications may differ.

11.2 Critical spares

Critical spare planning should consider:

  • Consequence of failure.
  • Equipment population and installed base.
  • Failure history.
  • Procurement lead time.
  • Repairability.
  • Obsolescence.
  • Storage and shelf-life requirements.
  • Alternative equipment or approved substitutions.

A spare part is useful only if it is compatible, serviceable, accessible, and available when needed.

11.3 Repairable components

For repairable components such as PLC modules, drives, servo amplifiers, and industrial PCs, the plan may include:

  • Vendor repair turnaround.
  • Spare replacement unit.
  • Configuration backup.
  • Firmware compatibility.
  • Repair quotation and approval.
  • Testing and acceptance requirements.
  • Warranty or repair-report requirements.
  • Return and installation tracking.

Where a replacement unit is installed, the removed unit and the installed unit should be traceable through the site's material and equipment records.

12. Production Coordination and Shutdown Planning

Maintenance work often competes with production for access to equipment.

Shutdown planning must account for:

  • Production demand.
  • Equipment availability.
  • Upstream and downstream dependencies.
  • Buffer capacity.
  • Product changeovers.
  • Required isolation.
  • Cooling or depressurization time.
  • Mechanical access.
  • Lifting and handling arrangements.
  • Testing duration.
  • Restart and stabilization time.

12.1 Shutdown window

A shutdown window is the approved period during which the equipment can be made unavailable for maintenance.

The work must fit within the practical window, including isolation, execution, testing, restoration, and handover.

The planned repair duration alone is not the full shutdown duration.

12.2 Sequencing work

Several tasks may be grouped into one shutdown if they share:

  • The same equipment isolation.
  • The same access requirement.
  • Compatible specialist resources.
  • A common production window.
  • Similar restoration and testing requirements.

However, combining tasks can increase risk if the scope becomes too large, dependencies are unclear, or critical testing is rushed.

A shutdown plan should identify task sequence, dependencies, responsible teams, and the conditions required before production restarts.

13. Safety Planning and Isolation

Planned maintenance should include safety controls before the work begins.

13.1 Energy sources

Depending on the equipment, energy sources may include:

  • Electrical supply.
  • Stored energy in DC buses or capacitors.
  • Pneumatic pressure.
  • Hydraulic pressure.
  • Rotational inertia.
  • Gravity and suspended loads.
  • Springs and mechanical tension.
  • Heat.
  • Chemical energy.
  • Unexpected automatic or remote movement.

The exact isolation and verification process must follow the approved site procedure and applicable regulations.

13.2 Lockout/tagout and verification

Where required, the plan should identify:

  • Energy sources and isolation points.
  • Authorized isolation personnel.
  • Lockout and tagout requirements.
  • Stored-energy discharge or restraint.
  • Verification of the safe state.
  • Group isolation requirements.
  • Personal protection and access controls.
  • Conditions for removing isolation and restoring equipment.

An HMI stop command, PLC output, or software interlock is not automatically an adequate energy isolation method.

13.3 Return to service

Before releasing equipment to operations, confirm:

  • Tools and temporary materials are removed.
  • Guards and protective devices are restored.
  • Connections and covers are secured.
  • Required testing is complete.
  • Safety functions are restored and verified as required.
  • Bypasses or temporary modifications are removed or formally controlled.
  • Personnel are clear of hazardous areas.
  • Operations receives the required handover.

Safety-critical testing and authorization must not be skipped to meet a production restart deadline.

14. Technical Documentation Required for Planned Work

The documentation depends on the task but may include:

  • As-built electrical drawings.
  • Mechanical drawings.
  • Pneumatic and hydraulic diagrams.
  • PLC and drive backups.
  • I/O lists and network architecture.
  • Parameter lists.
  • Manufacturer manuals.
  • Maintenance procedures.
  • Risk assessments.
  • Calibration records.
  • Test procedures.
  • Acceptance criteria.
  • Previous fault history.
  • Previous maintenance reports.
  • Vendor instructions.
  • Relevant permits and inspection records.

For automation maintenance, configuration documentation is particularly important.

Before authorized changes to a PLC, CNC, HMI, VFD, or servo system, confirm the required backup, version identification, recovery procedure, and change authorization.

A backup that cannot be restored or whose version is unknown should not be treated as a verified recovery asset.

15. Planned Maintenance for PLC, CNC, HMI, and Industrial Automation

Automation maintenance requires planning beyond the physical replacement of a module. Configuration, software compatibility, process dependencies, and commissioning must also be considered.

15.1 PLC replacement or modification

The work package may require:

  • PLC manufacturer, model, and hardware revision.
  • Current program backup and version.
  • Firmware and software compatibility.
  • I/O configuration.
  • Network and device configuration.
  • Communication settings.
  • Safety-related program and authorization requirements, where applicable.
  • Current fault history.
  • Required test cases.
  • Rollback or recovery plan.
  • Approval for the change.

Before modifying a running production system, establish how the work will be authorized, tested, documented, and restored if the modification fails.

15.2 HMI and SCADA changes

Plan for:

  • Application and project backup.
  • Runtime and engineering software versions.
  • Tag and communication configuration.
  • Alarm and event behavior.
  • Recipe and setpoint handling.
  • User access and security.
  • Historical data and database dependencies.
  • Operator training where necessary.
  • Functional acceptance testing.

A screen that opens successfully is not proof that its tags, alarms, recipes, or control commands work correctly.

15.3 VFD and servo replacement

Plan for:

  • Exact drive and motor identification.
  • Parameter backup and comparison.
  • Motor data and feedback-device compatibility.
  • Control mode and reference source.
  • Communication configuration.
  • Protection and safety-related interfaces.
  • Mechanical load and coupling considerations.
  • Required tuning or commissioning steps.
  • Functional and interlock testing.

Do not assume that copying parameters from a different drive model will produce a safe or correct configuration.

15.4 CNC maintenance

Plan for:

  • Machine and control-system identification.
  • Relevant alarm history.
  • Machine-builder documentation.
  • Tool and work-offset preservation where applicable.
  • Servo and spindle parameters.
  • PLC or ladder backups where authorized.
  • Axis reference and alignment requirements.
  • Machine-tool geometry checks when relevant.
  • Dry-run and controlled production verification.

CNC maintenance may involve several interacting subsystems. The work package should define the exact scope and acceptance criteria rather than relying on a generic "machine tested" statement.

16. Planned Maintenance for Electrical and Mechanical Repairs

16.1 Electrical component replacement

A planned electrical repair may require:

  • Circuit and equipment identification.
  • Current electrical drawings.
  • Correct replacement part.
  • Rated tools and test instruments.
  • Isolation and safe-state verification.
  • Wiring identification and labeling.
  • Manufacturer torque requirements.
  • Applicable electrical tests.
  • Functional testing and documentation.

Replacement of a protective device must preserve the required ratings, coordination, and intended protection. A device should not be substituted solely because it fits the mounting arrangement.

16.2 Mechanical repairs

Mechanical work may require:

  • Assembly drawings.
  • Correct tools and lifting equipment.
  • Alignment requirements.
  • Bearing and seal specifications.
  • Lubrication requirements.
  • Fastener torque specifications.
  • Tolerances and clearances.
  • Guarding requirements.
  • Trial-run and acceptance checks.

16.3 Combined mechanical and electrical work

For a motor or pump replacement, coordinate:

  • Mechanical disconnection and installation.
  • Electrical disconnection and reconnection.
  • Alignment and coupling checks.
  • Earthing and protection checks.
  • Rotation-direction verification where applicable.
  • Instrumentation and control checks.
  • Interlock verification.
  • Functional testing under controlled conditions.

A motor that runs successfully without its intended load may still fail to perform correctly when connected to the machine. Acceptance testing must reflect the equipment's actual function and approved procedure.

17. Planning Tools and Maintenance Management Systems

Maintenance planning may use a CMMS, ERP system, work-order software, spreadsheets, or an integrated combination.

A computerized maintenance management system (CMMS) typically supports equipment records, work requests, work orders, scheduling, materials, and maintenance history.

An enterprise resource planning (ERP) system may integrate maintenance with purchasing, inventory, finance, and production.

17.1 Common planning records

  • Equipment master.
  • Functional location.
  • Maintenance notification.
  • Work order.
  • Task list or job plan.
  • Material reservation.
  • Labor allocation.
  • Maintenance schedule.
  • Permit and risk records.
  • Completion confirmation.
  • Maintenance history.

17.2 SAP PM concepts

Exact transaction codes and workflows vary by SAP version and site configuration, but common concepts include:

  • Functional location: Represents an equipment position or system location.
  • Equipment master: Holds information about an individual maintainable asset.
  • Maintenance notification: Records a problem, request, or maintenance need.
  • Maintenance order: Organizes and tracks authorized maintenance work.
  • Task list: Provides reusable operations and instructions.
  • Maintenance plan: Defines recurring maintenance scheduling rules where configured.
  • Material reservation: Supports preparation of required components.
  • Confirmation: Records work performed, time, and relevant execution details.
  • Technical completion: Marks a defined stage of work-order completion according to the configured process.

A notification is not the same thing as a completed work order. A finding may be recorded first, then assessed and converted into planned work according to the site's workflow.

17.3 Master-data quality

Planning accuracy depends on reliable master data.

Check that:

  • Asset identifiers are correct.
  • Equipment location is accurate.
  • Spare-part records match the installed equipment.
  • Task lists are current.
  • Labor estimates are realistic.
  • Maintenance intervals are approved.
  • Obsolete equipment is identified.
  • Technical documents point to the correct revision.

Poor master data causes downstream planning errors, even when the software itself works correctly.

18. Work Order Planning and Scheduling Metrics

Maintenance performance should be assessed using consistent definitions and reliable records.

KPI Meaning
Planned work percentage Proportion of maintenance work classified as planned under the site's definition
Schedule compliance Proportion of scheduled work completed within the defined scheduling period
Ready-work percentage Proportion of the relevant backlog that meets the site's readiness criteria
Work-order aging Time elapsed since work was created or reached a defined status
Planning accuracy Difference between estimated and actual labor or duration
Material availability Proportion of required materials available when needed
Rework rate Proportion of work requiring repeat intervention because the original work was incomplete or ineffective
Maintenance backlog Volume of open maintenance work, measured using a defined basis
Emergency work percentage Proportion of maintenance effort or work orders classified as emergency
Unplanned downtime Time lost to equipment stoppages that were not planned
Maintenance cost Recorded labor, materials, services, and other relevant maintenance costs

18.1 Planned work percentage

One possible formula is:

\[ \text{Planned Work \\%} = \frac{\text{Planned Maintenance Hours}}{\text{Total Maintenance Hours}} \times 100 \]

The organization must define which hours count as planned and how emergency, corrective, project, and indirect work are treated.

A high planned-work percentage does not automatically mean maintenance is effective. Work may be carefully scheduled yet fail because of poor diagnosis or inadequate execution.

18.2 Schedule compliance

A common calculation is:

\[ \text{Schedule Compliance \\%} = \frac{\text{Scheduled Work Completed as Defined}}{\text{Total Work Scheduled}} \times 100 \]

The denominator, time window, and meaning of "completed" must be consistent. Canceled work, deferred work, and work partly completed should be treated according to a documented rule.

18.3 Backlog in labor-weeks

Some maintenance organizations express backlog in labor-weeks:

\[ \text{Backlog Weeks} = \frac{\text{Estimated Labor-Hours of Open Work}}{\text{Effective Weekly Labor Capacity}} \]

Effective capacity should reflect actual available maintenance labor after accounting for relevant leave, routine duties, meetings, training, and other constraints.

Backlog weeks are a planning indicator, not a universal target. Appropriate levels depend on the site's risk, asset population, resource model, and work mix.

19. Planning Quality and the Maintenance Feedback Loop

Planning improves when actual execution results are compared with the original assumptions.

Record:

  • Estimated and actual labor.
  • Estimated and actual duration.
  • Parts planned and parts consumed.
  • Additional defects discovered.
  • Delays and their causes.
  • Safety or access issues.
  • Test results.
  • Equipment condition after maintenance.
  • Repeat failures.
  • Recommendations for future work.

19.1 Why actual-versus-estimated data matters

Suppose a motor replacement is repeatedly estimated at three hours but consistently takes six hours because alignment, wiring checks, and commissioning are omitted from the estimate.

The solution is not to pressure technicians to work faster. The job plan should be corrected to include the actual required activities and resources.

19.2 Continuous improvement

A useful review asks:

  • Was the work scope correct?
  • Was the diagnosis supported by the evidence?
  • Were the correct parts available?
  • Were labor and duration estimates realistic?
  • Were safety controls adequate?
  • Were the required tests completed?
  • Did the repair eliminate the problem?
  • Should the task list or maintenance strategy be changed?

The results should update future job plans, task lists, spare-part requirements, and risk assessments where appropriate.

20. Common Planning Failures

Failure Consequence Improvement
Vague work scope Unclear responsibilities and incomplete repairs Define the defect, boundaries, and acceptance criteria
Missing spare parts Equipment remains unavailable while materials are sourced Confirm material readiness before scheduling
Unrealistic duration Work overruns the shutdown window Include isolation, testing, restoration, and handover
Incorrect asset identification Wrong parts or procedures are used Verify equipment and location records
Missing drawings or backups Increased diagnostic and recovery time Confirm document availability and revision
Inadequate risk assessment Hazards are discovered during execution Assess hazards and isolation requirements before work
Wrong skill assignment Delays, poor-quality work, or unsafe execution Match competency to the task
No commissioning plan Equipment is returned without adequate verification Define functional tests and acceptance criteria
Schedule overload Tasks compete for the same people or equipment window Resolve resource and dependency conflicts
Repeated deferral Defects remain open and risk may increase Review priority, constraints, and escalation
Poor completion records History and planning assumptions remain inaccurate Record actual work, findings, and verification results
No post-work review Recurring planning problems persist Update job plans using actual execution evidence

21. Practical Example: Planned Replacement of a PLC Power Supply

A PLC system experiences intermittent power-supply diagnostic faults. The controller is still operating, but the fault history indicates that the issue requires investigation and possible replacement.

21.1 Define the job

The work scope identifies:

  • PLC cabinet and affected power supply.
  • Fault history and symptoms.
  • Correct replacement part and compatibility.
  • Required isolation procedure.
  • Configuration and recovery requirements.
  • Required checks after replacement.
  • Acceptance criteria for returning the system to service.

The fault should be investigated sufficiently to distinguish a defective power supply from an upstream supply problem, loose connection, excessive load, or another cause.

21.2 Prepare resources

The planner arranges:

  • Compatible spare power supply.
  • Qualified maintenance personnel.
  • Electrical drawings and manufacturer instructions.
  • Appropriate test equipment.
  • Approved isolation and verification steps.
  • Production access window.
  • Required permits and authorization.
  • Functional test procedure.
  • Contingency plan if the replacement does not resolve the fault.

21.3 Schedule the work

The maintenance team coordinates with production to select a suitable window. Dependencies such as controller shutdown, process state, backup requirements, and restart checks are agreed in advance.

21.4 Execute and verify

After isolation and replacement under the approved procedure:

  1. Verify installation and connections.
  2. Restore the system according to the approved procedure.
  3. Check power-supply and PLC diagnostics.
  4. Verify I/O communication and relevant machine functions.
  5. Confirm interlocks and protective functions as required.
  6. Monitor the system for recurrence of the original symptom.
  7. Record test results and any additional findings.

21.5 Close the work order

Record the component installed, removed component identification where applicable, actual labor, findings, test results, and final equipment status.

If the original fault persists, reopen or continue the technical investigation rather than treating component replacement as proof of successful repair.

This example demonstrates planned corrective maintenance: a defect initiates the work, while advance preparation organizes its safe and effective execution.

22. Planned Maintenance Checklist

Before scheduling

  • The maintenance need and asset are clearly identified.
  • The work scope and boundaries are documented.
  • Technical diagnosis is sufficient for the proposed work.
  • Safety hazards, isolation, and permit requirements are identified.
  • Required procedures, drawings, and backups are available.
  • Required spare parts and consumables are confirmed.
  • Labor, competence, tools, and specialist support are identified.
  • Work duration and major dependencies are estimated.
  • Testing and acceptance criteria are defined.

Before execution

  • The work package is complete enough for execution.
  • Materials and tools are available.
  • Personnel are assigned and competent.
  • Production access and shutdown requirements are agreed.
  • Required permits and authorizations are ready.
  • Isolation and energy-control arrangements are confirmed.
  • Contingencies and recovery requirements are understood.

After execution

  • Work is completed according to the approved scope.
  • Required functional and safety checks are completed.
  • Equipment is restored and handed over properly.
  • Actual labor, material usage, and findings are recorded.
  • Defects requiring further work are documented.
  • Configuration and technical records are updated where required.
  • The work order is completed according to site procedure.
  • Lessons learned are used to improve future planning.

23. Final Principles

  • Planned maintenance is the preparation and organization of maintenance work before execution.
  • Maintenance can be planned regardless of whether the original trigger was preventive, condition-based, predictive, or corrective.
  • Planning defines the scope, method, resources, safety controls, and acceptance criteria.
  • Scheduling assigns the execution window and coordinates resources and operational constraints.
  • Work readiness requires more than an open work order; essential materials, information, personnel, and access must be confirmed.
  • Good planning includes isolation, testing, commissioning, and handover, not just the physical repair.
  • SAP PM and other maintenance systems are useful only when asset data, work orders, material records, and completion information are reliable.
  • Actual execution results should improve future job plans, labor estimates, and material readiness.
  • Safety requirements and technical acceptance criteria take precedence over pressure to finish within a planned window.
  • Effective planned maintenance turns a maintenance request into a prepared, executable, traceable job.

The central principle: Define the work, prepare the resources, coordinate the execution, verify the result, and use the evidence to improve the next job.
