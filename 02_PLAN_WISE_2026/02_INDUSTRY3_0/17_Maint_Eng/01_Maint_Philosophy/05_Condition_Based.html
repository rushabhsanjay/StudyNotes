1. Definition

Condition-based maintenance (CBM) is a maintenance strategy in which maintenance actions are initiated or scheduled according to the observed condition of equipment, rather than relying only on fixed calendar intervals or operating hours.

The condition is assessed through inspections, measurements, diagnostics, or monitoring systems. When a measured parameter reaches an approved decision criterion, the responsible person evaluates the result and determines the required action.

Examples include:

  • Replacing a filter when its differential pressure reaches the specified limit.
  • Lubricating or inspecting a bearing when a condition indicator reaches its intervention criterion.
  • Investigating a motor when its vibration exceeds the approved threshold.
  • Cleaning an electrical cooling filter when airflow or differential pressure indicates restriction.
  • Replacing a worn belt when inspection shows that it has reached its wear limit.
  • Servicing a hydraulic system when oil contamination exceeds its permitted level.
  • Calibrating an instrument when its measured error exceeds the accepted tolerance.
  • Investigating a pump when flow or pressure falls outside its required operating range.

The defining feature is that the observed condition determines the maintenance decision.

Condition-based maintenance does not necessarily predict the exact date of failure. It identifies the current condition and uses established criteria to determine whether maintenance or further investigation is required.

2. Objectives of Condition-Based Maintenance

The main objectives are:

  • Perform maintenance when equipment condition justifies intervention.
  • Avoid replacing components that remain serviceable solely because a calendar interval has elapsed.
  • Detect deterioration before it becomes a functional failure.
  • Reduce unnecessary maintenance and component handling.
  • Improve equipment availability and reliability.
  • Prioritize work according to condition severity and risk.
  • Improve spare-parts planning by identifying emerging maintenance requirements.
  • Support maintenance decisions with recorded evidence.
  • Reduce secondary damage caused by continued operation with a deteriorating component.
  • Maintain required safety, quality, and process performance.

Condition-based maintenance does not mean waiting until a component fails or until a condition becomes dangerously abnormal. The intervention criterion should provide an appropriate margin for assessment, planning, and repair.

3. How Condition-Based Maintenance Works

A typical CBM process follows this sequence:

Equipment Operates
        ↓
Condition Is Inspected or Measured
        ↓
Measurement Quality Is Verified
        ↓
Measured Condition Is Compared With Criteria
        ↓
Condition Is Classified
        ↓
Required Action Is Selected
        ↓
Maintenance Is Planned and Executed
        ↓
Condition Is Rechecked
        ↓
Results Are Recorded

The sequence contains several important controls.

  1. Measurement: Obtain the relevant condition information using an approved inspection or measurement method.
  2. Validation: Confirm that the measurement is credible and was taken under suitable conditions.
  3. Evaluation: Compare the result with the applicable acceptance or intervention criterion.
  4. Decision: Determine whether to continue monitoring, inspect further, schedule maintenance, or take urgent action.
  5. Execution: Carry out the authorized work using the correct procedure.
  6. Verification: Confirm that the condition has returned to an acceptable state or that the intended maintenance result has been achieved.
  7. Documentation: Record the findings, action, parts used, and post-maintenance result.

A threshold crossing is evidence requiring an appropriate response. It is not automatically proof of a particular root cause.

4. Condition-Based Maintenance Versus Other Strategies

Strategy Basis for maintenance Example
Breakdown maintenance Equipment has failed Replace a motor that no longer runs
Corrective maintenance A defect or deficiency has been identified Repair a leaking valve
Preventive maintenance A predetermined time or usage interval Replace a component after a specified number of operating hours
Condition-based maintenance Actual condition reaches a defined criterion Replace a filter after differential pressure exceeds its intervention limit
Predictive maintenance Condition data and analysis indicate developing degradation or likely future failure Analyze a bearing trend to estimate when intervention may be needed
Planned maintenance Work is organized in advance with an agreed scope, resources, and timing Schedule an approved pump repair during a shutdown
Proactive maintenance Root causes or weaknesses are removed to prevent recurrence Redesign a recurring cable-routing problem

These categories are not mutually exclusive.

For example, a condition-based inspection may identify an abnormal bearing. The work may then be planned for the next suitable shutdown. The maintenance is condition-based because the condition triggered the need, and planned because the work was organized in advance.

5. Condition Indicators and Decision Criteria

A condition indicator is a measured or observed parameter that provides information about equipment health or performance.

A decision criterion is the rule used to interpret that indicator and determine the required response.

Condition indicator Possible criterion Potential action
Vibration Approved warning or intervention limit exceeded Verify measurement and investigate machinery
Bearing temperature Temperature or temperature trend exceeds approved criteria Check load, lubrication, cooling, and bearing condition
Filter differential pressure Pressure drop exceeds specified limit Inspect or replace filter according to procedure
Hydraulic oil contamination Contamination exceeds the equipment's permitted limit Investigate contamination source and condition the oil or system
Motor current Current or imbalance falls outside expected operating limits Check load, supply, motor, and drive diagnostics
Instrument error Error exceeds permitted tolerance Adjust, repair, or replace the instrument as appropriate
Belt condition Wear, cracking, or tension exceeds acceptance criteria Adjust or replace the belt
Compressed-air leakage Leakage exceeds the site's action criterion Locate and repair the leak
Battery condition Capacity or internal resistance fails the applicable criterion Investigate or replace the battery
CNC spindle condition Relevant vibration, temperature, or diagnostic criterion is exceeded Assess spindle and operating conditions

The criteria must be appropriate to the equipment, application, measurement method, and manufacturer's instructions. A limit used for one motor, bearing, or filter cannot automatically be applied to another.

6. Types of Condition Assessment

Condition-based maintenance can use simple inspections, manual measurements, or continuous monitoring.

6.1 Visual inspection

Visual inspection identifies observable defects such as:

  • Cracked insulation.
  • Oil leakage.
  • Corrosion.
  • Loose or damaged guards.
  • Discolored electrical components.
  • Broken cable supports.
  • Damaged hoses.
  • Abnormal wear.
  • Blocked ventilation openings.

Visual findings should be assessed using documented acceptance criteria where practical. An observation such as "looks bad" is less useful than a description of the defect, its location, extent, and required action.

6.2 Manual measurement

A technician may periodically measure:

  • Temperature.
  • Vibration.
  • Pressure.
  • Flow.
  • Belt tension.
  • Electrical parameters.
  • Insulation-related quantities using approved test methods.
  • Instrument accuracy.
  • Oil contamination.

The result is compared with the relevant criterion and previous readings.

6.3 Online condition monitoring

Sensors or connected devices continuously or periodically measure selected parameters.

Examples include:

  • Vibration sensors on critical rotating equipment.
  • Temperature sensors on motors or bearings.
  • Differential-pressure transmitters across filters.
  • Oil-condition monitoring systems.
  • Drive diagnostic data.
  • PLC measurements recorded in SCADA or a historian.

Online monitoring can detect changes between manual inspections, but it introduces sensor, data-quality, communication, and configuration requirements.

6.4 Functional and performance checks

Some condition assessments evaluate whether equipment still performs its required function.

Examples include:

  • Checking whether a pump achieves the required flow and pressure.
  • Verifying that a pneumatic cylinder reaches its expected position within the allowed time.
  • Checking CNC axis positioning performance.
  • Confirming that a cooling system maintains the required temperature.
  • Testing an instrument against a traceable reference.

Functional checks are particularly useful when a component's condition cannot be assessed reliably from a single physical measurement.

7. Understanding Thresholds and Condition Zones

A condition-based maintenance program often classifies condition into several zones. The number of zones and their limits depend on the asset and risk.

Zone Meaning Typical response
Normal Condition is within the accepted operating range Continue operation and routine monitoring
Warning Condition is approaching an intervention criterion or showing a meaningful deviation Verify the reading, investigate, and increase monitoring if appropriate
Action A defined maintenance criterion has been reached Plan or perform the specified maintenance action
Critical Condition presents an unacceptable risk or violates a required limit Follow the approved urgent response, including stopping equipment where required

These zones are an example of a decision framework, not a universal industrial standard.

7.1 Warning limit

A warning limit indicates that the condition needs attention before it reaches the action criterion.

For example, a warning may be generated when a temperature begins rising unusually quickly, even though the absolute temperature remains within the equipment's permitted range.

A warning should define what the recipient is expected to do, such as verify the measurement, check operating conditions, or inspect a related component.

7.2 Action limit

An action limit defines the condition at which a specified intervention or decision is required.

The intervention might be:

  • Cleaning a filter.
  • Inspecting a bearing.
  • Correcting a belt tension problem.
  • Taking a confirmatory measurement.
  • Scheduling a repair.
  • Reducing load under an approved procedure.
  • Stopping the equipment when continued operation is unacceptable.

An action limit must have an associated response. A threshold that generates an alert but has no owner or defined action is an incomplete maintenance control.

7.3 Critical limit

A critical limit may be associated with equipment protection, process safety, quality, or a manufacturer's maximum permitted operating condition.

If the criterion indicates an immediate hazard or a condition outside the approved operating envelope, the response must follow the applicable safety and operating procedure. A maintenance dashboard must never be treated as a substitute for a required protective function.

8. Establishing Suitable Condition Limits

Condition limits should be technically justified rather than selected merely because a value looks convenient.

Useful sources include:

  • Manufacturer documentation.
  • Applicable standards and technical guidance.
  • Engineering calculations.
  • Equipment design limits.
  • Historical measurements from healthy equipment.
  • Inspection and failure history.
  • Process and product-quality requirements.
  • Site risk assessments.
  • Validated monitoring methods.

8.1 Absolute limits and baseline deviation

An absolute limit compares a measurement with a specified permitted value.

A baseline-deviation limit compares the current measurement with expected behavior for that particular asset and operating state.

Both can be useful.

For example, a motor may remain below an absolute temperature limit while its temperature rises abnormally relative to its established baseline. The deviation may justify investigation before the absolute limit is reached.

Conversely, a stable measurement is not necessarily acceptable if it is already outside the required operating range.

8.2 Account for operating conditions

Condition measurements can change normally with:

  • Load.
  • Speed.
  • Ambient temperature.
  • Production recipe.
  • Duty cycle.
  • Cooling airflow.
  • Lubricant temperature.
  • Material being processed.
  • Startup and shutdown conditions.

A meaningful criterion must account for these effects when they materially influence the measurement.

For instance, a motor operating at a higher load may draw more current than it does at low load. Comparing the two readings without context may lead to an incorrect diagnosis.

8.3 Hysteresis

Hysteresis uses different thresholds for entering and leaving a condition state. It helps prevent repeated switching when a measurement fluctuates around a single threshold.

Example:

  • Enter warning state when temperature rises above \(80^\circ\text{C}\).
  • Clear the warning only after temperature falls below \(75^\circ\text{C}\), subject to the approved logic and equipment requirements.

The values are illustrative only. They are not general limits for industrial motors.

Hysteresis is useful for alert stability, but it must not delay a required protective action.

8.4 Persistence and debounce

A condition may need to remain abnormal for a defined period before a maintenance alert is generated. This can reduce false alarms caused by brief transients.

However, persistence filtering must be designed carefully. A rapidly developing hazardous condition may require immediate action rather than waiting for a timer.

The monitoring rule should distinguish routine condition alerts from protective trips and safety-related responses.

9. Condition-Based Maintenance of Electrical Panels

Electrical panels are suitable for several forms of condition assessment, provided the methods are safe and appropriate.

9.1 Useful indicators

  • Abnormal thermal patterns.
  • Discoloration or signs of overheating.
  • Damaged insulation.
  • Corrosion or moisture ingress.
  • Cooling-fan condition.
  • Filter blockage.
  • Abnormal supply voltage.
  • Repeated protective-device trips.
  • Loose or damaged external cable supports.
  • Unusual electrical noise or odor, assessed under the site's safety procedure.

9.2 Typical actions

  • Investigate a thermal anomaly using an approved inspection procedure.
  • Replace a blocked filter.
  • Repair damaged cooling equipment.
  • Investigate repeated trips using fault history and approved testing.
  • Correct moisture ingress.
  • Replace damaged components after appropriate isolation and verification.

Electrical connections should not be tightened indiscriminately as a condition-based maintenance action. The required isolation, torque specification, inspection method, and re-energization procedure must be followed.

Condition assessment does not authorize unsafe work on energized equipment.

10. Condition-Based Maintenance of Motors and Rotating Equipment

Motors, pumps, fans, gearboxes, and similar assets can be assessed using combinations of vibration, temperature, current, lubrication, noise, and performance measurements.

10.1 Motor condition

Potential indicators include:

  • Bearing temperature.
  • Vibration.
  • Current imbalance.
  • Abnormal starting behavior.
  • Cooling-system condition.
  • Repeated drive faults.
  • Changes in operating noise.

A high motor current may be caused by increased mechanical load, supply problems, motor faults, or drive-related issues. The measurement identifies a condition to investigate, not necessarily the root cause.

10.2 Pump condition

Potential indicators include:

  • Flow.
  • Discharge pressure.
  • Suction pressure.
  • Bearing vibration.
  • Bearing temperature.
  • Motor current.
  • Leakage.
  • Noise.
  • Energy consumption for comparable operating conditions.

For example, reduced flow combined with abnormal vibration may justify an investigation into suction conditions, impeller condition, cavitation, or another fault. The diagnosis should be based on the actual pump design and operating data.

10.3 Bearing condition

Bearing assessments may include:

  • Vibration measurements.
  • Temperature trends.
  • Lubrication condition.
  • Audible or ultrasonic indications.
  • Inspection findings.
  • Operating history.

A bearing should not be replaced solely because one unexplained measurement is slightly different from a previous reading. Measurement validity, trend, severity, and the applicable criterion must be considered.

11. Condition-Based Maintenance of VFDs and Servo Systems

VFDs and servo drives provide useful diagnostic information, but the meaning of each value depends on the drive model and application.

11.1 VFD indicators

  • Heatsink temperature.
  • Cooling-fan status, where available.
  • Repeated overtemperature warnings.
  • Output-current trend.
  • Repeated overload events.
  • Fault history.
  • Abnormal DC-bus diagnostic values.
  • Environmental and cabinet cooling conditions.

A recurring drive overtemperature warning may require checking ambient temperature, airflow, fan condition, filter blockage, load, and installation conditions.

11.2 Servo indicators

  • Following error.
  • Torque demand.
  • Motor current.
  • Feedback faults.
  • Overload events.
  • Positioning deviations.
  • Repeated drive alarms.
  • Changes in movement time or settling behavior.

A rising torque demand may indicate increased mechanical resistance, but the cause may also be a changed process load, an incorrect parameter, or another operating change.

11.3 Maintenance cautions

  • Confirm the exact drive and motor model.
  • Use manufacturer-specific diagnostic definitions.
  • Preserve the approved parameter backup before authorized configuration changes.
  • Do not change tuning parameters merely to suppress a symptom.
  • Use approved isolation and discharge procedures before electrical work.
  • Verify operation after maintenance without bypassing safety functions.

12. Condition-Based Maintenance of PLC, HMI, SCADA, and OT Networks

Automation equipment can be assessed through diagnostic events, operating statistics, environmental measurements, and system performance.

12.1 PLC and remote I/O

Monitor:

  • Repeated module faults.
  • Communication errors.
  • Power-supply diagnostics.
  • Abnormal cabinet temperature.
  • Controller restart history.
  • Unstable input readings.
  • Repeated loss of remote I/O.
  • Device disconnections.

12.2 HMI and industrial PC

Monitor:

  • Repeated application crashes.
  • Storage capacity.
  • Disk-health warnings.
  • CPU and memory utilization.
  • Service restart history.
  • Communication timeouts.
  • Database latency.
  • Backup status and recovery readiness.

12.3 Industrial network

Monitor:

  • Link flapping.
  • Port errors.
  • Packet loss.
  • Communication retries.
  • Latency changes.
  • Repeated device reconnects.
  • Switch diagnostic events.
  • Unexpected changes in network traffic.

A communication error does not automatically prove that a cable is defective. Possible causes include configuration errors, power interruptions, overloaded devices, duplicate addresses, electromagnetic interference, switch problems, or application-level issues.

12.4 Applying condition criteria

For example, a remote I/O adapter may produce repeated communication faults. A condition-based response could require:

  1. Confirming that the faults are genuine and not caused by stale diagnostic data.
  2. Reviewing timestamps and event frequency.
  3. Checking device power and network status.
  4. Inspecting approved diagnostics and relevant physical connections.
  5. Determining whether the problem is recurring and operationally significant.
  6. Planning corrective work based on risk.
  7. Verifying stable communications after the intervention.

The fault history should be preserved before clearing diagnostics when it is needed for analysis.

13. Condition-Based Maintenance of Sensors and Instruments

Instruments may continue producing values while their accuracy deteriorates. A plausible-looking value is not proof that the instrument remains accurate.

13.1 Useful indicators

  • Calibration error.
  • Drift relative to a reference.
  • Unstable output.
  • Signal saturation.
  • Repeated out-of-range alarms.
  • Noise or intermittent signal loss.
  • Process readings inconsistent with independent measurements.
  • Physical damage or contamination.

13.2 Example: Pressure transmitter

Suppose a pressure transmitter is compared with an approved reference instrument during a verification activity. If the measured error exceeds the permitted tolerance, the instrument requires an appropriate response.

Possible actions include:

  • Confirming the test setup and reference instrument.
  • Checking the measurement range and configuration.
  • Adjusting the instrument if permitted.
  • Repairing or replacing the instrument.
  • Reviewing affected process measurements if the error could have influenced production or safety decisions.
  • Recording as-found and as-left results.

An adjustment should not erase the as-found evidence. That information helps determine whether earlier measurements may have been unreliable.

13.3 Calibration interval adjustment

Condition and calibration history may support an engineering review of calibration intervals where the applicable quality system and regulations permit it.

However, a condition-based approach must not override mandatory calibration requirements or required verification intervals.

14. Condition-Based Maintenance of Pneumatic and Hydraulic Systems

Fluid-power systems often provide visible and measurable signs of deterioration.

14.1 Pneumatic systems

Potential indicators include:

  • Air leakage.
  • Pressure drop.
  • Slow cylinder movement.
  • Inconsistent actuator position.
  • Excessive air consumption.
  • Filter condition.
  • Regulator instability.
  • Valve faults.

Possible actions include leak repair, filter servicing, regulator investigation, or valve replacement after confirming the cause.

14.2 Hydraulic systems

Potential indicators include:

  • Oil contamination.
  • Oil temperature.
  • Filter differential pressure.
  • Pressure instability.
  • Abnormal pump noise.
  • Leakage.
  • Slow actuator movement.
  • Abnormal cycle time.
  • Changes in pump or motor load.

A high filter differential pressure may indicate restriction, but the measurement must be interpreted using the filter specification and system operating conditions. A failed sensor or incorrect installation can also produce misleading readings.

14.3 Example: Hydraulic filter

  1. Measure differential pressure across the filter.
  2. Verify that the transmitter and pressure connections are functioning correctly.
  3. Compare the reading with the specified warning and replacement criteria.
  4. Check whether the system is operating in a representative state.
  5. Replace or service the filter when the approved criterion requires it.
  6. Investigate the source of abnormal contamination if relevant.
  7. Verify that differential pressure returns to an acceptable range.

Replacing filters repeatedly without investigating abnormal contamination may treat the symptom while allowing the underlying problem to continue.

15. Condition-Based Maintenance in CNC and Robotic Equipment

15.1 CNC machines

Potential indicators include:

  • Spindle vibration.
  • Spindle temperature.
  • Abnormal spindle load.
  • Axis following error.
  • Lubrication alarms.
  • Hydraulic or pneumatic pressure.
  • Tool-changer cycle time.
  • Repeated servo alarms.
  • Positioning performance.

The assessment must account for the machine model, operating sequence, tooling, workpiece, cutting parameters, and manufacturer's diagnostic guidance.

15.2 Industrial robots

Potential indicators include:

  • Servo overload events.
  • Abnormal motor temperature.
  • Repeated axis alarms.
  • Increased torque demand.
  • Positioning deviations.
  • Lubrication-related findings.
  • Changes in cycle time under comparable conditions.

The robot's payload, speed, path, posture, and duty cycle can affect measured behavior. A condition comparison is meaningful only when relevant operating context is considered.

15.3 Safety requirements

Maintenance and condition checks must follow the machine's approved isolation, access, and safeguarding procedures.

A maintenance alert does not authorize entry into a hazardous robot workspace or intervention inside a CNC machine while dangerous motion remains possible.

16. Condition-Based Maintenance Versus Predictive Maintenance

These approaches overlap, but their primary decision logic differs.

Aspect Condition-based maintenance Predictive maintenance
Primary basis Current measured or observed condition Condition trends, patterns, and analytical evidence
Typical trigger Defined criterion is reached Evidence indicates developing degradation or likely future failure
Analysis complexity Can be simple or sophisticated Often includes trend analysis, diagnosis, or prognosis
Example Replace a filter at its specified differential-pressure limit Estimate filter restriction progression from differential-pressure and operating trends
Failure-date estimate Not normally required May be estimated when sufficient evidence and a suitable model exist
Maintenance action Triggered by condition and risk Planned using condition, likely progression, and uncertainty

A CBM program can use predictive analysis to improve its decisions. The distinction is the role of the evidence in determining the maintenance response, not whether a computer or sensor is present.

17. Inspection Frequency and Monitoring Interval

Condition-based maintenance requires a suitable measurement frequency.

The interval depends on:

  • How quickly the condition can deteriorate.
  • The consequences of missing a developing problem.
  • The sensitivity and reliability of the measurement method.
  • The time needed to respond to a warning.
  • The stability of the operating conditions.
  • Applicable inspection requirements.
  • The cost and practicality of measurement.

A slowly changing condition may be assessed through periodic manual inspection. A rapidly developing fault on a critical asset may require continuous monitoring or much more frequent checks.

The inspection interval must be short enough to provide a reasonable opportunity for action. It should not be selected merely because a weekly or monthly schedule is convenient.

17.1 Practical interval selection

A sensible approach is to:

  1. Identify the failure mode and its likely progression.
  2. Determine how early the selected method can detect the condition.
  3. Assess the consequence of delayed detection.
  4. Estimate the time required to verify and act on a finding.
  5. Select an interval that provides adequate intervention opportunity.
  6. Review the interval using actual inspection results and failure history.

Where the failure progression is uncertain, use a conservative, risk-based approach rather than assuming that the condition will deteriorate slowly.

18. Condition-Based Maintenance Workflow and Work Orders

Condition assessment must connect to an accountable maintenance process.

18.1 Recommended workflow

Inspection or Measurement
        ↓
Record the Result and Operating Context
        ↓
Validate Measurement Quality
        ↓
Compare With Approved Criteria
        ↓
Classify Severity and Risk
        ↓
Assign an Owner and Required Response
        ↓
Create or Update Maintenance Notification
        ↓
Plan and Execute Work
        ↓
Verify the Result
        ↓
Close the Work With Evidence

18.2 What the work order should contain

  • Asset number and equipment location.
  • Date and time of the finding.
  • Measurement or observation.
  • Engineering unit and measurement method.
  • Applicable criterion.
  • Operating condition at the time of measurement.
  • Description of the abnormality.
  • Risk and priority.
  • Required inspection or corrective action.
  • Required tools, parts, and permits.
  • Responsible person or team.
  • Findings after intervention.
  • Post-maintenance verification result.

A vague work order such as "check motor" provides little guidance. A useful work order identifies the symptom, location, evidence, criterion, and required assessment.

18.3 SAP PM application

In SAP Plant Maintenance, the exact transaction and workflow depend on the site's SAP configuration.

A typical process may involve:

  • Recording an inspection result or notification.
  • Identifying the functional location or equipment.
  • Assigning a priority and planning group.
  • Creating a maintenance order when work is required.
  • Planning components, labor, and operations.
  • Scheduling execution.
  • Recording findings, actual work, and component usage.
  • Completing technical confirmation and closure according to local procedures.

Condition-based findings should be linked to the asset's history so that repeated deviations can be recognized.

19. Condition-Based Maintenance KPIs

Useful performance indicators include:

KPI Purpose
Condition inspection compliance Measures whether required assessments were completed
Action-limit exceedances Tracks how often measured conditions cross intervention criteria
Response time Measures time from condition identification to assessment or action
Overdue condition actions Identifies unresolved maintenance findings
Repeat abnormal findings Helps identify recurring defects or ineffective interventions
Unplanned downtime Tracks disruption associated with equipment failure
Condition-related work orders Measures maintenance demand triggered by observed condition
False alarm rate Tracks alerts that do not correspond to the intended abnormal condition
Post-maintenance verification rate Measures whether the repaired condition was rechecked
Maintenance cost Tracks the cost of inspections, repairs, and monitoring
MTBF Measures average operating time between defined failures
MTTR Measures average time to restore equipment under the site's definition

No single KPI demonstrates success by itself. For example, a reduction in condition-related work orders might indicate improved equipment health, but it could also indicate missed inspections or under-reporting.

KPIs should be interpreted alongside reliability, quality, safety, and maintenance execution evidence.

20. Practical Industrial Example: Motor Bearing Temperature

Consider a motor that drives a critical production conveyor. The maintenance team monitors bearing temperature during operation.

20.1 Establish the condition criterion

The team documents:

  • The motor and bearing identification.
  • The sensor location and measurement method.
  • Normal temperature behavior at representative loads.
  • The manufacturer's applicable limits.
  • The site's approved warning and action criteria.
  • The required response to each condition level.

The values below are illustrative and must not be applied directly to actual equipment.

Condition Example reading Response
Normal \(55^\circ\text{C}\) Continue normal monitoring
Warning \(70^\circ\text{C}\) Verify the reading and assess operating conditions
Action \(80^\circ\text{C}\) Initiate the defined inspection or maintenance response
Critical Equipment-specific critical criterion reached Follow the approved urgent response, including shutdown where required

The response must also account for the rate of temperature rise, operating load, ambient temperature, and any applicable manufacturer limits. A more restrictive required limit takes precedence.

20.2 Investigate the abnormal condition

Suppose the measured temperature rises above the action criterion.

The maintenance team should:

  1. Verify the sensor reading and data quality.
  2. Confirm the motor's operating state and load.
  3. Check cooling airflow and ventilation.
  4. Review lubrication and bearing inspection history.
  5. Examine relevant vibration and drive diagnostics.
  6. Assess the risk of continued operation.
  7. Schedule or perform the required maintenance according to the approved procedure.
  8. Record the actual cause found during inspection.
  9. Recheck the temperature after the intervention.

Possible causes include lubrication problems, bearing deterioration, excessive load, restricted cooling, or an instrumentation fault. The temperature reading alone does not identify which cause is responsible.

20.3 Verify effectiveness

After maintenance, compare the new measurement with the established baseline under comparable operating conditions.

If the temperature remains abnormal, the team should not close the problem merely because a component was replaced. Further investigation may be necessary.

The recorded history should include the initial condition, diagnosis, corrective action, and verification result. This evidence helps improve future maintenance decisions.

21. Practical Example: Filter Differential Pressure

A filter in a hydraulic system has a differential-pressure transmitter measuring the pressure difference across the filter.

The system uses the manufacturer's approved criteria to determine when investigation or replacement is required.

The maintenance response should consider:

  • Differential pressure at the current operating flow.
  • Whether the transmitter and impulse connections are functioning correctly.
  • The filter specification and permitted operating range.
  • Oil temperature and viscosity where relevant.
  • The condition of the oil and possible contamination sources.
  • Whether the reading is rising progressively or changed suddenly.

If the action criterion is reached, the team follows the approved filter-service procedure. After replacement, it verifies the differential pressure and investigates the reason for unusually rapid filter restriction.

This is condition-based maintenance because the observed differential pressure triggers the intervention decision. Trend analysis may help estimate when the limit will be reached, but that additional forecasting is not required for the basic CBM decision.

22. Common Mistakes in Condition-Based Maintenance

Mistake Why it is a problem Correct approach
Using generic thresholds for every asset Equipment and measurement conditions differ Use applicable manufacturer guidance and approved engineering criteria
Acting on an unverified reading Sensor faults and data errors can trigger unnecessary work Validate measurement quality first
Waiting until functional failure The intervention window may be lost Define suitable warning and action criteria
Treating every warning as an emergency Resources are diverted from higher-risk problems Apply severity and risk-based response rules
Ignoring warning trends below a fixed limit Developing deterioration may be overlooked Review trends as well as absolute limits
Clearing alarms without investigation Recurring defects remain unresolved Preserve evidence and document the cause
Replacing components without confirming the fault The actual problem may remain Diagnose and verify the suspected failure mechanism
Ignoring operating context Normal load changes may appear abnormal Compare equivalent operating conditions
Measuring without recording results History and repeatability are lost Record values, units, conditions, and actions
Creating alerts without assigned ownership Findings may remain unresolved Assign responsibility and response times
Failing to verify after maintenance Repair effectiveness remains uncertain Repeat the appropriate measurement or functional check
Ignoring mandatory maintenance requirements Condition monitoring may not satisfy required inspection or test obligations Retain all required statutory, safety, and quality controls

24. Condition-Based Maintenance Checklist

Program setup

  • Target assets and their criticality are identified.
  • Relevant failure modes and condition indicators are documented.
  • Inspection and measurement methods are defined.
  • Condition limits and warning criteria are technically justified.
  • Operating conditions are recorded where they affect interpretation.
  • Required inspection intervals are established.
  • Every alert has a defined response and responsible owner.

Inspection and evaluation

  • The correct asset and measurement point are confirmed.
  • Instruments and sensors are suitable for the task.
  • Units, timestamps, and data quality are checked.
  • Readings are compared with approved criteria.
  • Relevant trends and historical findings are reviewed.
  • Abnormal results are investigated before assigning a root cause.
  • Risks are evaluated before continued operation or maintenance is authorized.

Execution and verification

  • Notifications and work orders contain actionable details.
  • Required labor, parts, tools, and permits are arranged.
  • Work follows approved safety and engineering procedures.
  • Corrective action addresses the identified condition.
  • Post-maintenance measurements or functional checks are completed.
  • Findings and actual work are recorded against the asset.
  • Recurring abnormalities trigger a review of the maintenance strategy.

25. Final Principles

  • Condition-based maintenance uses actual equipment condition to trigger or determine maintenance action.
  • Condition indicators must be compared with appropriate acceptance or intervention criteria.
  • A threshold crossing requires a suitable response, but does not automatically prove a specific root cause.
  • Limits must account for equipment design, operating conditions, measurement quality, and risk.
  • Condition-based maintenance can use visual inspection, manual measurement, online monitoring, or functional checks.
  • Condition-based maintenance and predictive maintenance overlap, but predictive maintenance places greater emphasis on interpreting degradation and anticipating future condition.
  • Maintenance decisions must consider severity, uncertainty, and the consequences of continued operation.
  • Every actionable finding should have an owner, a response, a record, and a verification step.
  • Condition monitoring does not replace mandatory inspections, calibration, safety functions, or approved operating limits.
  • The goal is not to react to every changing number. It is to recognize meaningful changes and act at the right time.

The central principle: Measure the actual condition, compare it with a justified criterion, take the appropriate action, and verify that the intervention worked.
