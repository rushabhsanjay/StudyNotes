1. What is concept development?

Concept development is the engineering process of converting customer requirements, process-study findings, site-survey results, and existing-system assessment into one or more feasible technical solutions.

It defines what the proposed system should do, how it should operate, how its major components will interact, and why the selected solution is appropriate.

At this stage, the engineering team develops and compares possible approaches before committing to detailed designs, component selection, software implementation, and installation.

For industrial automation, concept development may involve decisions such as:

  • Whether to modify an existing PLC or introduce a separate controller.
  • Whether a machine should communicate through hardwired I/O, industrial Ethernet, or a gateway.
  • Whether to retain an existing robot or replace it.
  • Whether production data should be collected directly from a PLC or through an existing SCADA system.
  • Whether a sequence should be implemented in PLC logic, a robot program, or a coordinated combination.
  • Whether a complete machine replacement is more appropriate than a retrofit.

A good concept is not simply an attractive architecture diagram. It is a technically feasible, safe, maintainable, and economically justified approach to meeting the defined requirements.

2. Position in the engineering lifecycle

Concept development follows the collection and assessment of requirements and existing conditions.

Customer requirements
        ↓
Process study
        ↓
Site survey
        ↓
Existing-system assessment
        ↓
Concept development
        ↓
Automation architecture
        ↓
Detailed engineering
        ↓
Implementation and integration
        ↓
Testing and commissioning
        ↓
Handover and lifecycle support

These stages may overlap or iterate. For example, concept development may reveal that a proposed cycle time is unrealistic, that a required interface is unavailable, or that the existing system needs a more detailed assessment.

Such findings should trigger a controlled review of the affected assumptions and requirements.

3. Objectives of concept development

The concept should establish:

  • The proposed system scope and boundaries.
  • The functional behavior and operating sequence.
  • The main hardware and software approach.
  • The interaction between PLCs, HMIs, CNCs, robots, drives, sensors, and other devices.
  • The required control and communication interfaces.
  • The intended operating modes and recovery behavior.
  • The high-level safety approach and required safety assessments.
  • The expected performance and quality outcomes.
  • The installation, commissioning, and production constraints.
  • The major risks, dependencies, and unresolved decisions.
  • The approximate cost, schedule, and lifecycle implications.
  • The acceptance criteria used to evaluate the completed solution.

The concept must provide enough clarity for the next engineering stage without pretending that every cable, terminal, parameter, or program instruction has already been designed.

4. Inputs required for concept development

Concept development depends on reliable information from earlier lifecycle activities.

Input Contribution to the concept
Customer requirements Defines required functions, constraints, and acceptance expectations
Process study Establishes process sequence, timing, material flow, and quality needs
Site survey Identifies installation space, utilities, access, and environmental constraints
Existing-system assessment Establishes current capabilities, limitations, and compatibility
Production data Provides baseline performance and improvement opportunities
Safety requirements Identifies hazards, safeguarding constraints, and required safety engineering
Plant standards Defines preferred hardware, networks, documentation, and approved practices
Maintenance requirements Influences diagnostics, access, spare parts, and serviceability
IT/OT requirements Constrains connectivity, data access, cybersecurity, and remote support
Budget and schedule Limits feasible scope, implementation options, and downtime

If a critical input is unknown, the concept should state the uncertainty and identify how it will be resolved.

5. Define the problem before choosing the technology

The concept must begin with the problem to be solved, not with a preferred product or programming platform.

For example, consider a machine that frequently stops because the transfer between two stations is unreliable.

A weak approach is to immediately propose a new PLC.

A stronger approach is to establish:

  • How often the stoppage occurs.
  • Which sequence step is active when it occurs.
  • Whether the fault is mechanical, electrical, logical, or communication-related.
  • Whether the existing controller and interface are capable of supporting a correction.
  • Whether better diagnostics or a revised handshake would solve the problem.
  • Whether the proposed solution introduces new dependencies or risks.

The selected concept should address the verified cause and required future behavior, rather than merely replacing the component most visible during a breakdown.

6. Establish the functional concept

The functional concept describes the system's intended behavior independently of unnecessary implementation details.

Define:

  • Inputs and operating conditions.
  • Commands and expected responses.
  • Sequence steps and transitions.
  • Permissives and interlocks.
  • Operating modes.
  • Completion criteria.
  • Fault detection.
  • Stop and abort behavior.
  • Recovery and restart behavior.
  • Operator actions.
  • Required feedback and diagnostics.

For a machine-transfer application, a high-level functional sequence could be:

Verify source part available
        ↓
Verify destination ready
        ↓
Request transfer
        ↓
Receive acknowledgement
        ↓
Execute transfer
        ↓
Verify part arrival
        ↓
Confirm transfer complete
        ↓
Return to ready state

This is a functional description, not a complete PLC program. Each transition still needs defined signals, timing requirements, failure behavior, and safety-related constraints.

7. Develop alternative technical concepts

For significant design decisions, create more than one technically credible option.

Consider an existing machine that must exchange production data with a new controller.

Option Concept Advantages Limitations
A Direct hardwired I/O Simple behavior, easy to inspect electrically Limited data capacity and increased wiring
B Direct industrial network communication Richer data exchange and reduced point-to-point wiring Requires compatible interfaces and network engineering
C Industrial protocol gateway Can connect otherwise incompatible systems Adds another device, configuration, and failure dependency
D Replace or upgrade the controller May simplify long-term integration Higher cost, migration effort, and commissioning risk

The options must be evaluated against actual equipment capabilities. A theoretically attractive protocol is not a valid option if the existing controller cannot support it and no approved interface is available.

The purpose of alternatives is to make trade-offs explicit before design decisions become expensive to reverse.

8. Evaluate and select the preferred concept

Use criteria that reflect the project's real priorities.

Criterion Evaluation question
Functional suitability Does it meet all required functions?
Performance Can it achieve the required cycle time and response?
Safety Can the design satisfy applicable safety requirements?
Compatibility Does it work with the installed equipment and interfaces?
Reliability What new failure points or dependencies are introduced?
Maintainability Can plant personnel diagnose and repair it?
Availability Are spare parts, software tools, and technical support available?
Expandability Can future requirements be accommodated?
Cybersecurity Can access and communication be managed appropriately?
Installation Can the solution be installed within physical and operational constraints?
Cost What are the total implementation and lifecycle costs?
Schedule Can the solution be delivered within the required timeframe?

Weighted decision matrix

A weighted matrix helps compare options systematically.

Suppose the team assigns a weight \(w_i\) to each criterion and gives each option a score \(s_i\). A weighted score can be calculated as:

\[ S = \sum\_{i=1}^{n} w_i s_i \]

If the weights are normalized so that their sum equals 1, the total score can be compared directly across alternatives using the same scoring scale.

Example:

Criterion Weight Option A score Option B score
Functional suitability 0.30 4 5
Maintainability 0.20 5 4
Lifecycle cost 0.20 5 3
Expandability 0.15 2 5
Implementation risk 0.15 4 3

Option A:

\[ S_A = (0.30 \times 4)+(0.20 \times 5)+(0.20 \times 5)+(0.15 \times 2)+(0.15 \times 4)=4.10 \]

Option B:

\[ S_B = (0.30 \times 5)+(0.20 \times 4)+(0.20 \times 3)+(0.15 \times 5)+(0.15 \times 3)=4.00 \]

Under these illustrative assumptions, Option A scores slightly higher.

However, a scoring matrix is a decision aid, not a substitute for engineering judgment. A safety requirement, mandatory compatibility constraint, or unacceptable risk cannot be overridden by a high overall score.

9. Develop the high-level control concept

Define which controller or subsystem owns each major function.

For example:

Operator
   ↓
HMI
   ↓
Main PLC
   ├── Remote I/O
   ├── VFD and motor control
   ├── Servo or motion controller
   ├── Robot interface
   ├── CNC interface
   └── Production data interface

Safety devices
   ↓
Safety controller or safety relays
   ↓
Validated safety-related functions

The diagram is conceptual. Actual safety architecture, communications, and controller responsibilities depend on the risk assessment and applicable design requirements.

The concept should define:

  • Primary control ownership.
  • Subsystem responsibilities.
  • Command and feedback boundaries.
  • Interface types.
  • Required diagnostics.
  • Operating modes.
  • Fault ownership and escalation.
  • Required system behavior when a subsystem becomes unavailable.

Avoid distributing ownership ambiguously. If both the main PLC and a robot program independently control the same sequence decision without a defined authority, the system can develop inconsistent states.

10. Develop the integration concept

Integration is often the most important part of a multi-device automation concept.

For each interface, identify:

  • The source system.
  • The destination system.
  • The data or signal exchanged.
  • The communication method.
  • The direction of information flow.
  • The update or response requirement.
  • The timeout behavior.
  • The recovery behavior.
  • The system responsible for each command.
  • The required validation and diagnostic information.

Example interface definition:

Signal or data Source Destination Purpose
Machine_Ready CNC interface Main PLC Indicates machine readiness under defined conditions
Load_Request Main PLC Robot controller Requests a loading operation
Robot_Busy Robot controller Main PLC Indicates that the robot is executing an operation
Robot_Complete Robot controller Main PLC Indicates completion of the defined robot operation
CNC_Alarm CNC interface Main PLC Reports an alarm condition
Part_ID Traceability system Main PLC or designated controller Associates the operation with the required part identifier

Signal names are illustrative. The actual interface must define polarity, data type, validity conditions, ownership, and behavior during communication loss.

11. Define handshake and timeout behavior

A robust concept defines how communicating devices agree that an operation has been requested, accepted, completed, or failed.

A basic request-acknowledge exchange can be described as:

Controller A                         Controller B
     |                                    |
     |--------- Request = 1 ------------->|
     |                                    |
     |<-------- Acknowledge = 1 ----------|
     |                                    |
     |--------- Request = 0 ------------->|
     |                                    |
     |<-------- Acknowledge = 0 ----------|
     |                                    |
     |           Exchange complete        |

The real sequence must be adapted to the interface and device behavior. Some systems require separate busy, accepted, complete, or error states.

The concept should define:

  • When a request becomes valid.
  • What constitutes acknowledgement.
  • How completion is confirmed.
  • How duplicate or stale requests are handled.
  • What happens if the acknowledgement never arrives.
  • How timeout values are justified.
  • How a failed exchange can be diagnosed.
  • What conditions must be verified before retry or restart.

A timeout must not automatically trigger a blind restart. The system must first establish the actual equipment state and determine whether retrying is safe and valid.

12. Define operating modes and fault recovery

The concept should explain the intended behavior in each operating mode.

Mode Purpose Concept considerations
Automatic Execute the production sequence Permissives, sequence control, completion conditions
Manual Allow authorized individual operations Command limits, interlocks, operator visibility
Setup Prepare equipment for production Controlled setup actions and required access conditions
Maintenance Support inspection and service Isolation, authorized access, and maintenance procedures
Faulted Prevent continuation under invalid conditions Fault identification, state retention, and recovery criteria
Recovery Return the system to a valid operating state State verification, reset conditions, and restart permissions

Operating-mode names and permitted actions must follow the machine's design and applicable standards.

Fault recovery should address the physical state as well as the software state. For example, resetting a PLC fault does not prove that a gripper has released a part, a robot is clear of a transfer zone, or a CNC is ready to resume.

13. Establish the safety concept

The concept stage must identify how safety will be addressed, while leaving detailed risk assessment and validation to the appropriate engineering process.

Define the need to evaluate:

  • Hazardous movements and energy sources.
  • Operator and maintenance access.
  • Guarding and access interlocks.
  • Emergency-stop arrangements.
  • Robot and machine interaction zones.
  • Safety-related control functions.
  • Required safe states.
  • Energy isolation.
  • Restart prevention and restart conditions.
  • Validation and documentation responsibilities.

Safety-related functions must be designed according to the applicable risk assessment, standards, and required performance. Ordinary PLC logic should not be assumed to provide a safety function unless the complete design is specifically suitable and validated for that purpose.

The concept must also consider what happens when power, communication, or a subsystem fails.

14. Define performance and acceptance criteria

The concept should describe how success will be measured.

Examples include:

  • Required cycle time.
  • Required production output.
  • Acceptable failure and recovery behavior.
  • Required data accuracy.
  • Maximum permitted response time.
  • Required traceability completeness.
  • Alarm and diagnostic requirements.
  • Availability and maintainability targets.
  • Required operating modes.
  • Required integration and safety validation.

Acceptance criteria should be measurable wherever possible.

Weak criterion: “The robot communication should be reliable.”

Better criterion: “The robot-to-PLC interface shall detect a missing acknowledgement within the approved timeout and transition to the specified fault state, with the relevant diagnostic information available to the operator.”

The timeout value and exact fault response must be defined from system requirements and validated equipment behavior, not guessed during implementation.

15. Develop the data and monitoring concept

Where production data collection is required, decide what information should be collected, where it originates, and how it will be used.

Potential data includes:

  • Machine state.
  • Cycle start and completion timestamps.
  • Alarm codes.
  • Production counts.
  • Reject counts.
  • Part identifiers.
  • Recipe or program identifiers.
  • Process measurements.
  • Equipment utilization.
  • Downtime categories.
  • Maintenance events.

A conceptual data path may be:

PLC / CNC / Robot
        ↓
Approved industrial data interface
        ↓
Data collection service
        ↓
Historian or database
        ↓
Dashboard and reporting

The concept must define data ownership, timestamps, sampling or event rules, data quality checks, retention, access control, and behavior during communication or storage outages.

A dashboard is only as reliable as the data and state definitions behind it. If one machine reports RUNNING while another reports CYCLE_ACTIVE, the concept must establish how those states will be interpreted and normalized for reporting.

16. Assess maintainability and lifecycle support

A concept should account for the people who will operate, troubleshoot, and modify the system after commissioning.

Consider:

  • Familiarity with the selected PLC and engineering tools.
  • Availability of spare parts.
  • Manufacturer support and product lifecycle.
  • Access to software licenses and configuration tools.
  • Backup and restoration procedures.
  • Alarm quality and diagnostic depth.
  • Standardized components and naming conventions.
  • Modularity and code reuse.
  • Required training.
  • Documentation and change-control requirements.
  • Ease of testing modifications.
  • Future expansion and migration paths.

A solution that works only when its original developer is present is a long-term maintenance risk.

Standardization is useful, but it should not be applied blindly. A plant standard should guide the design while still allowing justified exceptions where technical or safety requirements demand them.

17. Estimate cost, schedule, and downtime

Concept development should produce an initial estimate with clearly stated assumptions.

Consider:

  • Hardware and software licenses.
  • Engineering and programming effort.
  • Mechanical and electrical modifications.
  • Panel fabrication.
  • Cabling and network infrastructure.
  • External vendor services.
  • Installation and commissioning.
  • Production downtime.
  • Testing and validation.
  • Training and documentation.
  • Spare parts and contingency.
  • Future maintenance and support.

Distinguish between a concept-stage estimate and a detailed quotation. Accuracy improves as equipment selection, interface requirements, and implementation details become better defined.

Production downtime deserves special attention. A low-cost solution that requires an extended unplanned shutdown may be worse than a more expensive option that can be installed during a scheduled outage.

18. Identify technical risks and assumptions

Record risks before the concept is approved.

Examples include:

  • Existing PLC firmware may not support the proposed communication method.
  • Source code may be unavailable.
  • The machine builder may not permit modification of the CNC interface.
  • Production cannot tolerate the estimated shutdown duration.
  • Existing safety documentation may be incomplete.
  • The required spare parts may be obsolete.
  • The network may not permit the proposed data path.
  • The process cycle time may not meet the required target.

Each important risk should have an owner, an action, and a closure condition.

Distinguish among:

  • Confirmed fact: Verified through suitable evidence.
  • Assumption: Used temporarily to progress engineering.
  • Constraint: A condition the solution must satisfy.
  • Dependency: Something required from another system, team, or supplier.
  • Open decision: A matter that has not yet been resolved.
  • Risk: An uncertain event or condition that could affect objectives.

This classification prevents assumptions from gradually becoming undocumented design requirements.

19. Concept review and approval

Before progressing to detailed engineering, review the concept with the relevant stakeholders.

Participants may include:

  • Process engineering.
  • Electrical and controls engineering.
  • Mechanical engineering.
  • Production.
  • Maintenance.
  • Quality.
  • Safety.
  • IT/OT or cybersecurity representatives.
  • Machine builders and equipment suppliers.
  • Project management.

The review should verify that the proposed concept meets the requirements, respects site constraints, addresses major risks, and has an acceptable implementation plan.

Typical approval questions include:

  • Are all required functions covered?
  • Are system boundaries and interface responsibilities clear?
  • Is the concept technically feasible with the identified equipment?
  • Are safety-related requirements identified?
  • Are production and installation constraints addressed?
  • Are critical assumptions and risks visible?
  • Are performance and acceptance criteria measurable?
  • Is the selected concept justified against alternatives?
  • Are the remaining open items assigned to owners?

Approval should be recorded with the applicable document revision and any conditions that must be resolved before detailed design.

20. Common concept-development mistakes

  • Selecting a PLC or robot before defining the actual requirement.
  • Copying an existing design without checking whether it fits the new application.
  • Designing only the normal operating sequence.
  • Ignoring timeout, abort, and recovery behavior.
  • Treating safety as a later programming task.
  • Assuming that similar products have compatible interfaces.
  • Ignoring the lifecycle cost of proprietary software and obsolete components.
  • Failing to involve maintenance and production personnel.
  • Defining vague acceptance criteria.
  • Treating estimated cycle-time improvement as a guaranteed result.
  • Ignoring production downtime and commissioning constraints.
  • Failing to document assumptions and dependencies.
  • Proceeding to detailed engineering while major design decisions remain unresolved.

The most important discipline is to distinguish a plausible idea from a verified and justified engineering concept.

21. Practical example: retrofitting a machine for production-data collection

Suppose an existing machine uses a PLC and HMI, but production reporting is manual. The objective is to capture machine states, cycle counts, alarms, and timestamps for analysis.

Step 1: Define the requirement

Specify which machine states, counts, alarms, and identifiers are needed, how frequently they are updated, and how the data will be used.

Step 2: Assess the existing system

Identify the PLC model, supported communication interfaces, tag availability, current machine-state logic, network constraints, and backup status.

Step 3: Develop alternatives

Consider an approved direct PLC interface, an existing SCADA integration point, or an industrial gateway where appropriate.

Step 4: Compare alternatives

Evaluate data quality, implementation cost, controller loading, cybersecurity, maintenance requirements, and supportability.

Step 5: Define the concept

Specify the data source, collection method, state definitions, timestamp rules, database or historian destination, and dashboard requirements.

Step 6: Define failure behavior

Determine how the solution behaves if the PLC becomes unavailable, the network disconnects, or the database cannot accept data. The production control system should not be made dependent on a reporting service unless that dependency is explicitly required and engineered.

Step 7: Define acceptance criteria

Verify that the required tags are correctly interpreted, events are timestamped consistently, data gaps are detected, and recovery from communication loss behaves as intended.

Step 8: Prepare the next-stage inputs

Produce the approved concept, proposed interfaces, data-flow diagram, risk register, preliminary cost estimate, and open-item list.

This is the difference between merely connecting a PLC to a database and engineering a maintainable production-data system.

22. Concept development deliverables

A concept package may contain:

  • Problem statement and objectives.
  • Functional scope and system boundaries.
  • Functional sequence description.
  • Alternative concepts and decision matrix.
  • Preferred concept and engineering justification.
  • High-level control architecture.
  • Preliminary integration and interface definitions.
  • Operating-mode and recovery philosophy.
  • Safety requirements and assessment actions.
  • Data collection and monitoring concept.
  • Performance and acceptance criteria.
  • Preliminary bill of materials or equipment categories.
  • Cost, schedule, and downtime estimate.
  • Risk, assumption, dependency, and open-item registers.
  • Concept review and approval record.

The next engineering stage should use these documents as controlled inputs rather than relying on informal discussions or memory.

23. Interview questions and answers

1. What is concept development in industrial automation?

It is the process of converting requirements and existing-system information into a feasible, justified high-level technical solution before detailed engineering.

2. Why should alternative concepts be evaluated?

Different approaches have different trade-offs in cost, compatibility, reliability, maintainability, safety, performance, and implementation risk. Evaluating alternatives makes the selection defensible.

3. What is the difference between a concept and detailed design?

A concept establishes the proposed solution, responsibilities, interfaces, and key design decisions. Detailed design defines the exact hardware, wiring, configuration, code, drawings, and implementation details.

4. What is a weighted decision matrix?

It is a structured method for comparing alternatives using weighted criteria and consistent scoring. Mandatory requirements and unacceptable risks remain constraints regardless of the total score.

5. Why must fault recovery be included in the concept?

Real equipment experiences sensor failures, timeouts, communication loss, and interruptions. Recovery must account for the physical machine state and defined operating conditions, not merely reset software errors.

6. What is an interface definition?

It specifies how systems exchange commands, status, data, and acknowledgements, including signal ownership, communication method, validity conditions, and failure behavior.

7. Why is maintainability a concept-stage concern?

Controller selection, software architecture, diagnostics, standardization, backup procedures, and spare-parts availability can strongly influence the long-term cost and reliability of the installation.

8. When should concept development be reconsidered?

When new evidence invalidates an assumption, a critical interface proves incompatible, a requirement changes, a safety concern emerges, or the selected solution no longer meets the project's constraints.

9. What is the purpose of acceptance criteria?

They define measurable conditions used to verify that the implemented solution meets the agreed requirements.

10. What should be available before detailed engineering begins?

An approved concept with defined scope, functional behavior, major interfaces, selected technical approach, acceptance criteria, known risks, and identified open items.

24. Final engineering principles

  • Begin with the problem and measurable requirements, not a preferred product.
  • Use the process study, site survey, and existing-system assessment as evidence.
  • Compare technically credible alternatives before selecting a solution.
  • Define controller responsibilities and interface behavior clearly.
  • Include abnormal conditions, timeouts, recovery, and operating modes.
  • Identify safety, cybersecurity, and lifecycle requirements early.
  • Make performance criteria measurable.
  • Document assumptions, risks, dependencies, and unresolved decisions.
  • Evaluate total lifecycle cost and production downtime.
  • Obtain the appropriate review and approval before detailed engineering.

Concept development establishes the engineering direction. Automation architecture, the next topic, turns that direction into a structured view of the system's major components, layers, interfaces, and responsibilities.
