1. Definition of a Panel General Arrangement Drawing

A Panel General Arrangement (GA) drawing is an engineering drawing that shows the overall dimensions, physical construction, component placement, mounting arrangements, access provisions, and cable-entry locations of an electrical control panel or cabinet.

It answers the question: Where will everything physically fit, how will the panel be constructed, and how will technicians access, install, wire, and maintain it?

A GA drawing is primarily concerned with physical arrangement rather than the detailed electrical connections between components.

For example, a GA drawing may show where a PLC, VFD, circuit breakers, power supply, terminal blocks, cooling fan, and cable glands are mounted. The electrical schematic defines how those devices are electrically connected.

2. Objectives and Applications

Panel GA drawings are used during:

  • Electrical panel design and enclosure selection.
  • Component placement and mounting-plate design.
  • Sheet-metal fabrication, drilling, cutting, and machining.
  • Door cutout planning for HMI screens, push buttons, selector switches, and indicators.
  • Cable entry, gland-plate, and wiring-duct planning.
  • Thermal management and electrical segregation.
  • Factory inspection and customer approval.
  • Site installation and equipment integration.
  • Maintenance, troubleshooting, retrofit, and future expansion.

A well-prepared GA drawing reduces fabrication errors, component interference, wiring congestion, installation delays, and maintenance difficulties.

3. GA Drawing Versus Other Electrical Documents

Document Primary purpose Typical information
Panel GA drawing Defines physical arrangement Overall dimensions, views, component positions
Electrical schematic Defines electrical logic and connections Contacts, coils, power circuits, control circuits
Wiring diagram Defines point-to-point wiring Device terminals, wire connections, wire numbers
Panel layout drawing Defines component placement DIN rails, ducts, component footprints
Terminal plan Defines terminal arrangement Terminal numbers, bridges, connections
Cable schedule Defines cable requirements Cable ID, origin, destination, cores
Single-line diagram Defines power distribution Incoming supply, feeders, protection
Fabrication drawing Defines manufacturing details Hole sizes, cutouts, bends, tolerances
Bill of materials (BOM) Defines required materials Part numbers, quantities, descriptions
Door layout Defines door-mounted devices HMI cutouts, operators, indicators, labels

A GA drawing may include several of these views or refer to separate drawings. The project drawing standard determines the actual document set.

4. Types of Views in a Panel GA Drawing

A complete GA package may contain several views depending on panel complexity.

4.1 Front external view

Shows the outside of the panel from the front.

Typical information:

  • Enclosure width and height.
  • Door arrangement and door gaps.
  • HMI, push buttons, selector switches, and indicators.
  • Emergency-stop operator where applicable.
  • Panel identification labels and warning labels.
  • Locks, handles, hinges, and ventilation openings.
  • Plinth or mounting arrangement.

Example:

FRONT EXTERNAL VIEW

+--------------------------------------+
| PANEL ID: MCC-01                     |
|                                      |
|   POWER ON       FAULT               |
|     [LED]         [LED]              |
|                                      |
|             +------------+           |
|             |    HMI     |           |
|             +------------+           |
|                                      |
|  START       STOP       RESET        |
|   [PB]        [PB]        [PB]       |
|                                      |
|                         [HANDLE]     |
+--------------------------------------+
|               PLINTH                 |
+--------------------------------------+

This is an illustrative arrangement, not a fabrication drawing. Actual dimensions, clearances, and device locations must be engineered.

4.2 Side view

Shows the depth and side construction of the enclosure.

Important details include:

  • Overall cabinet depth.
  • Door projection and swing.
  • Mounting plate position.
  • Side ventilation or cooling equipment.
  • Cable-entry arrangement.
  • Wall clearance and access requirements.

4.3 Rear view

Useful for panels with rear doors, rear cable access, or rear-mounted components.

It can show rear access doors, hinges, locks, cable glands, service openings, and external connections.

4.4 Internal mounting-plate view

Shows components mounted inside the cabinet with the door opened or the mounting plate viewed directly.

Typical items include:

  • Main isolator and incoming protection.
  • Distribution circuit breakers.
  • Control transformers and power supplies.
  • PLC, I/O modules, relays, and communication equipment.
  • VFDs, servo drives, and associated reactors or filters.
  • DIN rails and wiring ducts.
  • Terminal blocks and PE terminals.
  • Cooling fans, filters, heat exchangers, and thermostats.
INTERNAL MOUNTING-PLATE VIEW

+------------------------------------------------+
| Incoming power                                 |
| [Isolator] [Main protection] [Distribution]     |
|                                                |
| [24 VDC PSU] [PLC] [I/O] [Network switch]      |
|                                                |
| [Relay bank] [Control devices] [Drive]         |
|                                                |
|=============================================== |
| Terminal blocks: power / control / signal / PE  |
+------------------------------------------------+

Actual segregation depends on electrical design, applicable standards, equipment instructions, heat dissipation, and EMC requirements.

4.5 Top, bottom, and gland-plate views

These views establish where cables enter and leave the enclosure.

They may show:

  • Gland-plate dimensions and thickness.
  • Cable-gland hole sizes and spacing.
  • Incoming power cable entry.
  • Motor, sensor, field I/O, and communication cable entry.
  • Cable tray or conduit approach.
  • Removable gland-plate fixing points.
  • Sealing and environmental-protection requirements.

The drawing must distinguish between the enclosure's top or bottom surface and the removable gland plate. These are not necessarily the same physical part.

5. Essential Information on a GA Drawing

A professional GA drawing should contain the information needed to manufacture, inspect, install, and maintain the panel.

The GA does not necessarily contain every detail. Dimensions, hole patterns, and manufacturing tolerances may be defined in separate fabrication drawings.

6. Dimensions, Datums, and Measurement

6.1 Overall dimensions

The three primary enclosure dimensions are:

  • Width (W): Left-to-right dimension when viewed from the front.
  • Height (H): Bottom-to-top dimension.
  • Depth (D): Front-to-rear dimension.

A cabinet specified as \(800 \times 2000 \times 600\) mm usually means width × height × depth, but the drawing or specification must explicitly define the order.

Do not confuse overall external dimensions with usable internal dimensions. The enclosure frame, door, mounting plate, wiring ducts, and cooling equipment reduce usable space.

6.2 Datums

A datum is a reference surface, edge, axis, or point from which other dimensions are measured.

Typical cabinet datums include:

  • Bottom-left corner of the mounting plate.
  • Top-left corner of the enclosure.
  • Centreline of a door-mounted device.
  • Centreline of a mounting hole.
  • Reference edge of a gland plate.

Using a consistent datum prevents cumulative measurement errors.

6.3 Dimensioning example

Suppose a panel mounting plate is 700 mm wide and 1600 mm high. A DIN rail is positioned 150 mm from the left datum and 300 mm below the top datum.

The drawing should make the measurement references unambiguous. If the rail length is 500 mm, its right endpoint is 650 mm from the left datum.

\[ x\_{\text{right}} = x\_{\text{left}} + L \]
\[ x\_{\text{right}} = 150 + 500 = 650\text{ mm} \]

These are nominal layout dimensions. Fabrication tolerances and component-specific mounting requirements still apply.

6.4 Dimensioning practices

  • Prefer dimensions from defined datums instead of long chains of accumulated dimensions.
  • Specify hole diameter and centre-to-centre spacing.
  • Identify cutout dimensions and corner radii where required.
  • Avoid duplicate or contradictory dimensions.
  • Identify whether dimensions apply to the enclosure exterior, mounting plate, door, or gland plate.
  • Define tolerances when the fabrication process or fit requires them.
  • Do not scale the drawing with a ruler to obtain missing dimensions. Use the stated dimensions or request clarification.

7. Scale and Drawing Interpretation

Scale communicates the relationship between the drawing size and actual dimensions.

Scale Meaning Common use
1:1 Drawing size equals actual size Small component details
1:2 Drawing is half actual size Medium-sized details
1:5 Drawing is one-fifth actual size Cabinet arrangements
1:10 Drawing is one-tenth actual size Large panels or assemblies
NTS Not to scale Conceptual or schematic arrangements

The drawing's stated dimensions take precedence over measurements estimated from its printed or displayed size.

For a drawing at scale 1:5, an actual 1000 mm dimension is represented by 200 mm on the drawing, assuming the drawing is reproduced at its intended scale.

\[ L\_{\text{drawing}} = \frac{L\_{\text{actual}}}{5} \]

A PDF displayed at an arbitrary zoom level cannot reliably be measured using a screen ruler.

8. Internal Component Arrangement

The arrangement must consider electrical function, heat generation, wiring access, maintenance, and interference.

8.1 Incoming power and distribution

Incoming power devices are commonly grouped near the designated cable-entry area, subject to safe access and the overall design.

Typical components:

  • Main isolator or circuit breaker.
  • Incoming terminals or busbars.
  • Distribution protection.
  • Control-circuit protection.
  • Surge protection where required.

The GA must allow for the actual footprint, mounting requirements, cable space, and safe working access.

8.2 PLC and control components

PLC-related equipment may include:

  • CPU and I/O modules.
  • Remote I/O interfaces.
  • Interposing relays.
  • 24 V DC power supply.
  • Ethernet switches.
  • Communication gateways.
  • Signal-conditioning devices.

Provide space for module removal, connectors, front access, cable routing, and future service.

8.3 VFDs and servo drives

Drives can generate substantial heat and may require specific mounting orientations and clearances.

Check:

  • Manufacturer-specified top, bottom, and side clearances.
  • Cooling airflow direction.
  • Ambient temperature and derating.
  • Cable bending radius.
  • Motor cable and control cable routing.
  • EMC filters, reactors, and braking components where required.
  • Access for parameter adjustment and replacement.
  • Separation from temperature-sensitive devices.

A drive should not be placed in a convenient empty rectangle if that rectangle blocks its cooling path. Empty space is not automatically usable space.

8.4 Terminal blocks and wiring ducts

Terminal blocks should be positioned to support field wiring, identification, testing, and maintenance.

Provide sufficient space for:

  • Terminal identification.
  • End stops and end plates.
  • Jumper bars and cross-connectors.
  • PE and shield terminals.
  • Test access.
  • Wire entry and bend radius.
  • Wiring-duct covers to be removed.

Wiring ducts must not obstruct component ventilation, mounting screws, connector access, or safe separation.

8.5 Electrical segregation and EMC

The layout should consider separation between:

  • Power circuits and low-level analog signals.
  • VFD or servo motor cables and sensitive communication cables.
  • High-voltage and extra-low-voltage circuits.
  • Noisy switching devices and sensitive electronics.
  • Incoming and outgoing conductors where separation is required.

Required clearances and segregation must follow the applicable standards, design specifications, and equipment instructions. A universal spacing value cannot safely be assumed for every panel.

9. Door Layout and Mechanical Interference

Door-mounted devices must be positioned with both external usability and internal clearance in mind.

Examples include HMIs, push buttons, selector switches, pilot lights, meters, and operator interfaces.

Check the following:

  • Door thickness and cutout requirements.
  • Rear projection of each device.
  • Clearance from internal wiring ducts and components.
  • Door-hinge location and opening angle.
  • Door-handle and lock interference.
  • Cable flexibility for door-mounted equipment.
  • HMI viewing height and operator reach.
  • Label visibility and spacing.
  • Minimum bending radius of flexible door wiring.

An HMI may fit perfectly on the front view while its rear housing collides with a PLC or wiring duct. Therefore, the external and internal views must be reviewed together.

10. Cable Entry and Gland-Plate Design

The GA drawing must coordinate physical cable entry with the wiring design and the actual installation.

10.1 Cable-entry planning

Identify:

  • Incoming supply cables.
  • Motor and drive cables.
  • Field sensor and actuator cables.
  • PLC I/O cables.
  • Industrial Ethernet and communication cables.
  • Protective earth conductors.
  • Shielded cables.
  • Spare entries for approved future use.

Cable entry should match the actual cable tray, trench, conduit, or machine connection route.

10.2 Gland-plate spacing

The gland plate must allow adequate space for gland bodies, locknuts, cable diameters, and installation tools.

Avoid:

  • Holes too close to plate edges.
  • Glands colliding with adjacent glands.
  • Power and signal entries arranged contrary to the segregation plan.
  • Entries positioned below components that prevent cable routing.
  • Unused holes that compromise the required enclosure protection.

Hole dimensions must be based on the selected gland manufacturer's requirements and the actual cable dimensions, not guessed from the nominal cable size.

10.3 Cable bending radius

The GA and cable-routing design must allow cables to bend without exceeding the minimum bending radius specified by the cable manufacturer.

Insufficient space can lead to:

  • Damaged insulation.
  • Excessive mechanical stress on terminals.
  • Poor shielding continuity.
  • Difficult cable replacement.
  • Unreliable communication or premature cable failure.

For moving machine connections, the required bending radius and flex-life requirements may differ from those of fixed cabinet wiring.

11. Enclosure Selection and Environmental Requirements

A GA drawing is linked to the enclosure specification.

Important parameters include:

  • Required IP rating.
  • Indoor or outdoor installation.
  • Ambient temperature and humidity.
  • Dust, oil mist, coolant, and chemical exposure.
  • Corrosion resistance.
  • Mechanical impact requirements.
  • Enclosure material and surface finish.
  • Ventilation or air-conditioning arrangement.
  • Wall-mounted, floor-mounted, or machine-mounted construction.

An IP rating is not simply a property of an empty cabinet. Installed doors, glands, vents, cable entries, seals, and cutouts must preserve the required protection level.

For example, installing an ordinary ventilation opening may reduce the achievable ingress protection unless a suitable rated ventilation solution is used.

12. Thermal Management and Space Allocation

Component placement affects heat distribution and equipment reliability.

12.1 Heat sources

Common heat-generating devices include:

  • VFDs and servo drives.
  • Transformers.
  • Power supplies.
  • Braking resistors where installed inside the enclosure.
  • Contactors and power-distribution components.
  • High-density electronic assemblies.

The GA should reserve the space required for the selected cooling arrangement and the manufacturer's clearance requirements.

12.2 Basic heat calculation

If component heat losses are known, total internal heat dissipation can be estimated by summing the individual losses.

\[ Q\_{\text{total}} = \sum\_{i=1}^{n} Q_i \]

For example, assume a cabinet contains:

Component Heat loss
VFD 180 W
Power supply 25 W
PLC and I/O 35 W
Contactors and relays 30 W
Total 270 W

The estimated internal heat load is:

\[ Q\_{\text{total}} = 180 + 25 + 35 + 30 = 270\text{ W} \]

This is only a preliminary heat-load estimate. Final cooling selection must account for ambient temperature, enclosure size and material, permissible internal temperature, installation conditions, losses from other components, and the selected cooling method.

13. Coordination with the BOM and Electrical Schematic

The GA drawing must agree with the other engineering documents.

A typical coordination process is:

Electrical requirements
        ↓
Electrical schematic and device list
        ↓
Component selection and BOM
        ↓
Physical panel GA and layout
        ↓
Fabrication and wiring drawings
        ↓
Panel assembly and inspection
        ↓
As-built drawings and handover

Engineering is often iterative. If a VFD model changes, its footprint, heat loss, clearance, wiring connections, and protection requirements may also change. The GA and related documents must be reviewed together.

13.1 Device tags

A device tag provides a consistent identifier across drawings and physical equipment.

Examples:

  • QF1 for a circuit breaker, depending on the project's tag convention.
  • PS1 for a power supply.
  • PLC1 for a PLC assembly.
  • K1 for a relay or contactor, depending on the convention.
  • VFD1 for a variable-frequency drive.

These examples are not universal. Use the project's approved tagging standard and the applicable reference-designation rules.

The physical label, GA drawing, schematic, BOM, and maintenance records should identify the same equipment consistently.

14. Practical Worked Example: PLC Control Cabinet

Assume an industrial control cabinet with the following equipment:

  • Main isolator.
  • Incoming and outgoing protection.
  • 24 V DC power supply.
  • PLC CPU and I/O modules.
  • Ethernet switch.
  • Interposing relays.
  • One VFD.
  • Terminal blocks for field wiring.
  • PE terminals and shield terminals.
  • HMI mounted on the door.

14.1 Preliminary arrangement

Area Equipment Design consideration
Incoming section Isolator and incoming protection Safe access and incoming cable routing
Power distribution Branch protection Clear circuit grouping and maintenance

14.2 Design checks before release

  1. Confirm the actual enclosure dimensions and usable mounting area.
  2. Obtain dimensional drawings for every selected component.
  3. Place heat-generating devices and verify required clearances.
  4. Reserve space for wiring ducts, terminal blocks, and cable bending.
  5. Confirm door cutouts and rear-device clearance.
  6. Verify incoming and outgoing cable-entry positions.
  7. Check required segregation, earthing, and EMC arrangements.
  8. Confirm the BOM and device tags match the layout.
  9. Review mounting, lifting, transport, and site-access requirements.
  10. Release the drawing with the correct revision and approvals.

15. Common GA Drawing Errors

Error Consequence Prevention
Incorrect enclosure dimensions Fabrication or installation failure Verify the enclosure manufacturer's drawing
Missing door cutout dimensions Rework during assembly Specify cutouts and mounting details
Component collision Equipment cannot be mounted or serviced Check footprints and three-dimensional clearances
Insufficient duct space Congested wiring Estimate wiring volume and reserve routing space
Poor VFD placement Overheating or difficult maintenance Follow drive-specific clearance requirements
Incorrect gland-plate layout Cable entry interference Check actual cable and gland dimensions
No cable bending allowance Stress and difficult termination Use manufacturer bending-radius data
Inconsistent device tags Confusing maintenance documentation Coordinate GA, schematic, and BOM

Uncontrolled drawing revision Fabrication from obsolete information Apply formal revision control
No access allowance Difficult replacement and inspection Review service and tool access
Unverified site dimensions Installation clashes Confirm the actual installation envelope

16. GA Drawing Review and Inspection Checklist

Use this checklist before approving a panel GA drawing.

Drawing control

  • Correct project, panel ID, and drawing number.
  • Revision, date, author, and approval information present.
  • Units, scale, and dimension conventions identified.
  • Applicable standards and project specifications identified.

Mechanical design

  • Overall enclosure dimensions verified.
  • Door arrangement, hinges, locks, and opening clearance checked.
  • Mounting plate and mounting holes defined.
  • Cable-entry and gland-plate dimensions checked.
  • Plinth, foundation, and mounting arrangements defined where required.
  • Lifting and transport requirements addressed where applicable.

Electrical and automation layout

  • Components match the approved BOM.
  • Device tags agree with the schematic.
  • DIN rails, ducts, and terminal blocks are correctly located.
  • Power, control, analog, and communication segregation reviewed.
  • Protective earth and shield-termination provisions defined.
  • Drive and power-supply cooling requirements checked.
  • Cable bend radius and wiring access reviewed.

Installation and maintenance

  • Site cable routes and entry directions confirmed.
  • Operator devices are accessible and identifiable.
  • Components can be inspected and replaced.
  • Environmental protection is maintained after installation.
  • Spare space is identified if required by the project.
  • Final as-built changes will be incorporated into the drawing set.

17. Manufacturing, Installation, and Retrofit Use

17.1 During panel manufacturing

The GA drawing is used to confirm enclosure dimensions, component positions, rail and duct placement, door cutouts, and gland-plate machining.

The fabricator must use the approved manufacturing details and tolerances rather than infer critical dimensions from a visual layout.

17.2 During installation

Compare the drawing against the physical installation.

Check whether the cabinet fits the allocated footprint, doors can open, cable trays align with the intended entries, and adequate maintenance access remains.

17.3 During retrofit

When adding a PLC module, replacing a drive, or modifying terminal blocks:

  1. Verify the existing physical arrangement.
  2. Check the replacement component's dimensions, heat loss, wiring, and mounting requirements.
  3. Identify conflicts with ducts, doors, terminals, and adjacent components.
  4. Update the GA, schematic, BOM, and wiring documentation as applicable.
  5. Verify the revised assembly before returning it to service.
  6. Retain the approved as-built drawing revision.

A retrofit is not complete merely because the new component powers on. The physical arrangement and controlled documentation must also remain correct.

18. Revision Control and As-Built Drawings

A drawing revision records an approved change to the engineering definition.

Examples of changes that may require revision include:

  • Enclosure size or type changes.
  • Relocation of components.
  • New door-mounted devices.
  • Changes to cable-entry positions.
  • Replacement of obsolete equipment.
  • Addition of cooling equipment.
  • Revised mounting or installation details.

The revision record should identify what changed, why it changed, and who approved it, according to the project's document-control procedure.

Design intent versus as-built condition: The released GA defines the approved arrangement. The as-built drawing records the final installed condition after approved changes. They should not be allowed to drift apart.

19. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a panel GA drawing?

A panel General Arrangement drawing defines the physical dimensions, external views, internal component arrangement, mounting details, and cable-entry provisions of an electrical panel.

Q2. What is the difference between a GA drawing and an electrical schematic?

The GA shows where equipment is physically located. The schematic shows electrical relationships, contacts, coils, power paths, and control logic.

Q3. Why are multiple views required?

Front, side, rear, and internal views reveal different physical features, including enclosure depth, door-mounted devices, cable entry, and mounting-plate arrangement.

Q4. What is a mounting plate?

It is the internal plate on which components such as circuit breakers, PLCs, drives, relays, and DIN rails are mounted.

Q5. What is a gland plate?

It is a plate or designated entry surface used to accommodate cable glands and cable-entry openings. Its design must support the required mechanical strength and environmental protection.

Q6. Why is a VFD not placed arbitrarily in the panel?

Its placement affects heat dissipation, airflow, cable routing, EMC, service access, and the clearances required by the manufacturer.

Q7. What is the difference between external dimensions and usable internal dimensions?

External dimensions describe the enclosure's outside size. Usable internal dimensions account for the space consumed by the enclosure construction, mounting plate, ducts, and other internal features.

Q8. What is a datum in a GA drawing?

A datum is a defined reference point, edge, surface, or axis from which dimensions are measured.

Q9. Why must the GA agree with the BOM and schematic?

To ensure that the installed components, device identifiers, electrical connections, and physical layout describe the same approved design.

Q10. What causes door interference?

Incorrect cutout placement, excessive device projection, inadequate rear clearance, interference with wiring ducts, or insufficient door swing space can cause interference.

Q11. Can a GA drawing be used as the only wiring document?

No. A GA drawing does not normally provide all the point-to-point electrical connection details required for wiring.

Q12. What must be checked when replacing a component during a retrofit?

Check the footprint, mounting, electrical compatibility, heat loss, ventilation clearance, cable connections, bend radius, EMC requirements, and effects on related drawings.

Q13. Why is revision control important?

It prevents fabrication, installation, and maintenance work from being performed against obsolete or inconsistent engineering information.

Q14. What is an as-built GA drawing?

It records the final approved physical arrangement of the installed panel, including changes made during manufacturing or installation.

Q15. What is the most important principle when preparing a GA drawing?

The arrangement must be physically buildable, electrically coordinated, safe, maintainable, and consistent with the approved engineering documents.

20. Final Engineering Principles

  • A GA drawing is a physical design document, not a substitute for the electrical schematic.
  • Every important dimension must have an unambiguous reference.
  • Component footprints, cooling clearances, cable bend radii, and maintenance access must be verified.
  • Door layouts and internal mounting layouts must be checked together.
  • Cable-entry locations must match the actual installation and wiring design.
  • Device tags and component selections must agree with the BOM and schematic.
  • Environmental protection must be maintained after all cutouts, glands, and ventilation equipment are installed.
  • Approved changes must be reflected in controlled as-built documentation.
