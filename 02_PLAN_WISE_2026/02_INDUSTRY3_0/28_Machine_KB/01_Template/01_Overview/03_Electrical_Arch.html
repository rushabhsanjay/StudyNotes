1. Purpose of Electrical Architecture Documentation

Electrical architecture documentation explains how electrical power enters a machine, is distributed to different loads, is converted into usable forms, and is controlled and protected throughout the machine.

It provides the structural overview needed to understand the relationship between the incoming supply, main isolator, protective devices, distribution circuits, control power supplies, drives, motors, PLCs, instrumentation, and protective earthing.

For industrial maintenance, this record is essential for:

  • Troubleshooting complete machine power failures and partial power loss.
  • Identifying which loads are affected by a tripped protective device.
  • Understanding power distribution between panels and subsystems.
  • Locating the source of control power and its downstream loads.
  • Planning electrical isolation and verifying safe working conditions.
  • Understanding dependencies between drives, PLCs, remote I/O, and machine actuators.
  • Supporting electrical modifications, panel replacement, and obsolescence management.
  • Maintaining traceability between drawings, installed equipment, and spare parts.

The electrical architecture record is a high-level map. It does not replace detailed wiring diagrams, protection calculations, terminal plans, or approved isolation procedures.

2. What Is Electrical Architecture?

Electrical architecture describes the functional arrangement of the electrical system, including its power sources, distribution paths, protective devices, conversion stages, and major consumers.

A typical industrial machine may receive three-phase AC power for motors and drives, use a control power supply to generate 24 V DC, and distribute that DC supply to PLCs, sensors, remote I/O, relays, and communication devices.

A simplified architecture may look like this:

Incoming Plant Supply
        ↓
Main Isolator / Disconnect
        ↓
Main Protection and Distribution
        ↓
        ├── Motor Drives → Motors and Motion Systems
        ├── Transformer or Power Supply → Control Power
        │                              ↓
        │                         24 V DC Distribution
        │                              ↓
        │                         PLC, Sensors, I/O
        ├── Auxiliary Circuits → Pumps, Fans, Lighting
        └── Other Protected Feeders → Approved Subsystems

This is a conceptual example, not a wiring design. Actual arrangements depend on the machine, supply system, protection scheme, and manufacturer documentation.

3. Electrical Architecture Versus Related Documents

Document Primary purpose Typical contents
Electrical architecture overview Explains the overall electrical structure Power sources, panels, distribution, major loads
Single-line diagram Represents the power distribution system using simplified symbols Supply, switching, protection, feeders, ratings
Schematic or circuit diagram Shows electrical circuit relationships in detail Contacts, coils, control logic, protective devices
Wiring diagram Shows physical electrical connections Conductors, terminals, connectors, device pins
Terminal plan Identifies terminal blocks and connection assignments Terminal numbers, wire numbers, destination
Panel layout Shows the physical arrangement inside a cabinet Devices, mounting positions, ducting, clearances
Cable schedule Records cable identity and routing information Cable number, source, destination, type
Load schedule Records electrical loads and their characteristics Load rating, voltage, current, circuit reference
Earthing and bonding drawing Shows protective earth and bonding arrangements PE connections, bonding points, conductor details
Isolation procedure Defines how hazardous energy is isolated and verified Isolation points, lockout steps, verification requirements

These documents serve different purposes. A single-line diagram can show that a feeder supplies a drive, but the detailed schematic and wiring documents are needed to trace the control circuit and individual terminals.

4. Incoming Electrical Supply

The incoming supply is the electrical source feeding the machine or its main distribution panel.

The machine overview should identify the supply characteristics from approved electrical documentation and nameplate information.

4.1 Supply characteristics

Parameter Meaning
Nominal voltage Intended supply voltage
Phase arrangement Single-phase, three-phase, or another specified arrangement
Frequency Nominal AC supply frequency
Rated or design current Current value associated with the equipment or design
Available fault current Prospective fault current at the relevant point, used in protection design
Earthing system Supply earthing arrangement relevant to protection
Supply origin Distribution board, transformer, or other approved source
Isolation point Approved device or location used to isolate the supply
Feeder identification Reference used to trace the supply circuit

For example, a machine may use a three-phase AC supply for its main power system and an internally generated 24 V DC supply for control devices. The actual values must be taken from the machine's approved documentation.

Do not assume the supply voltage, current rating, phase arrangement, or earthing system from another machine in the same production area.

4.2 Supply origin and downstream distribution

The electrical architecture should show where the machine's incoming feeder originates and which protective device or distribution board supplies it.

This is especially important when several machines share a distribution panel. The location of a machine's main isolator does not necessarily identify every upstream source or auxiliary supply.

Some systems have separate feeds for control power, cabinet cooling, external pumps, test equipment, or other auxiliary systems. These must be identified where applicable.

5. Main Isolator and Protection

5.1 Main isolator or disconnect

The main isolator provides a defined means of disconnecting the relevant electrical supply, subject to the installed design.

The documentation should identify:

  • Device tag and location.
  • Circuit reference.
  • Number of poles and applicable ratings.
  • Upstream supply and downstream distribution.
  • Whether separate or auxiliary supplies exist.
  • Associated approved isolation instructions.

Switching a machine off is not the same as safely isolating it. Stored electrical energy, separate supplies, external interfaces, and other hazardous energy sources must be considered under the approved isolation procedure.

5.2 Protective devices

Common protective devices include:

  • Circuit breakers.
  • Fuses.
  • Motor protection circuit breakers.
  • Overload relays.
  • Residual-current protective devices where specified.
  • Electronic protection integrated into drives or power supplies.
  • Surge protective devices.
  • Protective devices for control and auxiliary circuits.

Their functions are not interchangeable. A motor overload relay, for example, does not automatically provide all the short-circuit protection required for its circuit.

The architecture record should identify the protective device and its circuit. Detailed ratings, settings, coordination, and suitability belong in the approved design documentation.

5.3 What to record for a protective device

Field Purpose
Device tag Unique reference on the drawing and panel
Device type Breaker, fuse, overload relay, or other device
Circuit supplied Identifies the downstream load
Rating or setting References the approved design value
Breaking capacity Relevant to short-circuit protection where applicable
Drawing reference Links to the detailed schematic
Trip indication Identifies available status or diagnostic information
Replacement specification Supports correct spare selection

Never change a breaker rating, fuse size, or protection setting merely to stop nuisance tripping. Determine the cause and use the approved engineering process for any change.

6. Electrical Panels and Their Functions

A machine may contain several electrical panels or enclosures. Their roles should be recorded separately so that a technician can identify the correct cabinet and the functions it contains.

6.1 Common panel types

Panel type Typical function
Main electrical panel Incoming supply, main isolation, protection, and distribution
Power distribution panel Distributes power to major machine subsystems
Drive panel Houses VFDs, servo drives, braking components, or related equipment
PLC/control panel Houses controllers, I/O, control relays, and control power supplies
Remote I/O enclosure Houses distributed input/output modules and local interfaces
Operator station Houses HMI equipment and operator controls
Auxiliary panel Supplies auxiliary equipment such as pumps, fans, or special systems
Safety control enclosure Houses safety-related control equipment where separately installed

Actual panel functions may be combined. A drive panel may also contain the PLC, and a machine may use remote I/O enclosures instead of one central control cabinet.

6.2 Panel identity fields

Record the following for each relevant panel:

  • Panel ID and designation.
  • Machine ID or machines served.
  • Physical location.
  • Panel manufacturer or builder, if known.
  • Incoming supply source.
  • Main isolator and protective device references.
  • Major devices and subsystems housed inside.
  • Panel cooling or environmental-control requirements.
  • Drawing number and revision.
  • Applicable inspection or maintenance procedure.

Example:

Field Illustrative entry
Panel ID PNL-CNC-002
Panel designation CNC Main Control Panel
Associated machine MCH-CNC-002
Location Machining Cell 02
Main function Control power, PLC, interfaces, and auxiliary circuits
Incoming supply Refer to approved single-line diagram
Main drawing Refer to controlled electrical drawing register

The example identifier is illustrative. Plant naming conventions take precedence.

7. Power Distribution and Conversion

Industrial machines commonly distribute electrical power through several stages, with each stage serving a different class of load.

7.1 Main power circuits

Main power circuits supply higher-power loads such as:

  • Spindle motors.
  • Conveyor motors.
  • Pumps and hydraulic power units.
  • Servo drives and motion systems.
  • Heating equipment.
  • Test-bench loads and auxiliary machinery.

The architecture should identify the feeder, protective device, and major consumer for each important branch.

7.2 Control power

Control power supplies provide the voltage required by control and instrumentation devices.

A common arrangement uses an AC input and a regulated 24 V DC output for:

  • PLC CPUs and I/O modules.
  • Sensors and electronic transmitters.
  • Industrial Ethernet switches.
  • Relays and interface modules.
  • HMI and other control devices, where supported.
  • Remote I/O and communication equipment.

Other equipment may require 5 V DC, 12 V DC, 48 V DC, or different AC supplies. Record actual requirements rather than assuming all control devices use 24 V DC.

7.3 Transformers and power supplies

A transformer changes AC voltage according to its design. A DC power supply converts its input into a regulated DC output.

These devices should be documented with:

  • Device tag and manufacturer.
  • Input voltage and frequency.
  • Output voltage and rated capacity.
  • Downstream circuits supplied.
  • Relevant protection.
  • Redundancy or monitoring arrangements, if present.

If several power supplies are used, identify which loads belong to each supply. This helps diagnose partial control-power loss.

8. Understanding the Single-Line Diagram

A single-line diagram, often called an SLD, is a simplified representation of an electrical power system. It uses standardized symbols and line connections to show the relationships between supply sources, switching devices, protective devices, distribution points, and loads.

It generally does not show every individual conductor or control contact.

8.1 Example single-line representation

Plant Distribution Board
          |
     Feeder Protection
          |
    Machine Main Isolator
          |
     Main Distribution
          |
     +----+------------------+
     |                       |
 Drive Feeder            Control Supply
     |                       |
 VFD / Servo Drive        24 V DC Supply
     |                       |
 Motor / Axis             PLC and I/O

This is a conceptual illustration. The actual drawing must show the relevant equipment, protective devices, circuit references, ratings, and connections required by the approved design.

8.2 Common SLD elements

Element Information represented
Supply source Origin of electrical power
Feeder line Connection between distribution points
Circuit breaker or fuse Circuit protection
Isolator Means of electrical disconnection
Transformer AC voltage transformation
Power supply Conversion to a specified DC output
Distribution bus Shared electrical distribution point
Motor or drive Major power-consuming equipment
Earth or PE connection Protective earthing arrangement
Circuit reference Link to detailed drawings or device tags

Symbols and drawing conventions vary by standard and organization. Read the drawing legend and applicable standards rather than interpreting unfamiliar symbols from appearance alone.

8.3 What an SLD can and cannot tell the technician

An SLD can help determine:

  • Which supply feeds a particular circuit.
  • Which protective device is associated with a feeder.
  • Which major loads share a distribution point.
  • Where the detailed circuit diagram should be consulted.
  • Which upstream sections may be affected by a power interruption.

An SLD usually cannot, by itself, establish:

  • The exact terminal wiring of every component.
  • The complete PLC logic or interlock sequence.
  • The full physical cable routing.
  • Whether a circuit is safe to touch.
  • Whether stored energy has been discharged.
  • The complete safety-related behavior of the machine.

Use the appropriate detailed drawings and approved safety procedures for those questions.

9. Protective Earthing and Bonding

Protective earthing and bonding provide a controlled arrangement for managing fault-current paths and reducing hazardous touch voltages, in accordance with the applicable electrical design.

The electrical architecture should identify the main protective earth arrangement and reference the relevant drawings.

Important concepts include:

  • Protective earth (PE): Connection intended for electrical safety.
  • Bonding: Electrical connection between conductive parts to maintain the required electrical continuity.
  • Functional earth: Connection used for functional purposes, such as electromagnetic compatibility, according to the equipment design.
  • Shield termination: Connection of cable screens according to the approved EMC and equipment design.

These concepts are related but are not interchangeable. Cable shields should not automatically be treated as protective-earth conductors.

Maintenance records should identify required bonding points and the approved inspection or testing method. Never remove a protective-earth conductor to investigate noise, communication errors, or nuisance tripping.

10. Electrical Loads and Their Dependencies

A load register connects the high-level architecture to the equipment installed in the machine.

Load category Examples Useful information
Motion Servo drives, spindle drives, VFDs Rating, supply circuit, motor served
Control PLC, remote I/O, relays Supply source, voltage, affected functions
Instrumentation Sensors, transmitters, measurement systems Supply voltage, circuit reference
Networking Managed switches, gateways Power source, redundant feed where applicable
Pneumatic/hydraulic auxiliaries Compressors or power units where machine-integrated Supply and control dependencies
Thermal management Cabinet fans, heat exchangers, air conditioners Supply circuit and temperature-control role
Operator interface HMI, panel PC Supply source and associated control system
Test equipment Data acquisition systems, programmable instruments Supply, protection, and process dependency

10.1 Why load mapping matters

Suppose a PLC and an Ethernet switch share a control-power supply. If that supply fails, the machine may lose both control execution and communication.

If separate supplies are used, the PLC may remain operational while the network switch becomes unavailable. The resulting symptoms and troubleshooting path differ.

Record the actual dependency instead of assuming that all devices fail together.

11. Power Ratings and Basic Electrical Calculations

Calculations support understanding and verification, but they do not replace approved design calculations or electrical engineering review.

11.1 Single-phase AC apparent power

For a single-phase circuit using RMS voltage and current:

\[ S = V I \]

Where:

  • \(S\) is apparent power in volt-amperes (VA).
  • \(V\) is RMS voltage in volts.
  • \(I\) is RMS current in amperes.

11.2 Three-phase AC apparent power

For a balanced three-phase system using line-to-line voltage:

\[ S = \sqrt{3} V_L I_L \]

Where:

  • \(S\) is apparent power in volt-amperes.
  • \(V_L\) is line-to-line RMS voltage.
  • \(I_L\) is line current.

For a balanced three-phase load, real power can be expressed as:

\[ P = \sqrt{3} V_L I_L \cos \phi \]

Where \(P\) is real power in watts and \(\cos \phi\) is the power factor under the applicable conditions.

For nonlinear loads such as drives and switched-mode power supplies, total power factor and harmonic effects must be considered appropriately. Do not infer actual input current solely from a motor's output rating.

11.3 DC power

For a DC load:

\[ P = V I \]

A nominal 24 V DC supply rated at 10 A has a nominal output capacity of 240 W, assuming the rating applies under the specified operating conditions.

The total load, startup behavior, environmental derating, wiring limits, and protection must still be checked against the manufacturer's specifications and approved design.

11.4 Why these calculations matter

These relationships help with:

  • Understanding equipment nameplate ratings.
  • Reviewing power-supply capacity.
  • Interpreting load schedules.
  • Recognizing obvious mismatches between documented ratings and installed equipment.
  • Supporting an engineering review of a proposed modification.

They do not independently establish conductor sizing, breaker selection, short-circuit protection, or safe operating limits.

12. Electrical Architecture and Control Systems

Electrical power architecture is closely related to the machine's control architecture.

A typical dependency is:

Incoming Power
    ↓
Control Power Supply
    ↓
PLC and Remote I/O
    ↓
Output Devices and Interfaces
    ↓
Drives, Valves, Contactors, and Motors
    ↓
Machine Operation

Feedback signals return from sensors, drives, contactors, and other equipment to the control system.

This is a simplified functional view. Actual machines may use distributed supplies, networked drives, safety controllers, and independently powered subsystems.

12.1 Power failure versus control failure

Observation Possible area to investigate
Entire machine has no power Incoming supply, feeder protection, main isolator, upstream supply
Main power present but PLC is off Control-power supply, branch protection, PLC power wiring
PLC is operational but one drive is off Drive feeder, branch protection, drive supply, local fault
Sensors are inactive across one section Local 24 V DC supply, branch fuse, distribution wiring
HMI is off but PLC is running HMI supply, local protection, HMI hardware
Ethernet devices are unreachable Switch power, network connection, addressing, configuration
Breaker repeatedly trips Fault investigation required; do not simply increase the rating

These are initial diagnostic directions, not definitive fault diagnoses. Confirm the circuit against the drawing and follow approved isolation and measurement procedures.

13. Electrical Architecture During Troubleshooting

A disciplined investigation starts with the documented power path and narrows the problem to a particular branch.

13.1 Example: PLC has no power

  1. Confirm the reported symptom and affected equipment.
  2. Consult the SLD to identify the PLC's supply source.
  3. Identify the relevant control-power supply and protective device.
  4. Check available status indicators and diagnostic information without opening energized equipment unless specifically authorized and controlled.
  5. Follow the approved electrical isolation and verification procedure before physical inspection or work.
  6. Verify the supply and wiring using the approved method and suitable instruments.
  7. Compare the observed condition with the approved schematic.
  8. Correct the identified fault under the authorized procedure.
  9. Verify PLC startup, I/O status, communication, and machine readiness.
  10. Record the fault cause and corrective action.

Do not replace the PLC merely because it is dark. Loss of power upstream is a far more basic possibility, and expensive components have a habit of receiving blame for problems they did not cause.

13.2 Example: One subsystem has lost power

If a machine's PLC and HMI remain operational but a hydraulic power unit is unavailable, use the architecture to identify the subsystem feeder, local protection, starter or drive, and associated control circuit.

Confirm whether the problem is electrical supply loss, an intentional interlock, a drive or motor fault, or a process condition preventing operation.

The process record and control documentation help distinguish these cases.

14. Electrical Safety and Isolation Documentation

Electrical architecture must support safe maintenance, but a drawing alone is not permission to work on equipment.

The isolation procedure must account for the actual machine design and applicable plant rules.

Relevant hazards may include:

  • Incoming AC supplies.
  • Separate control or auxiliary supplies.
  • Stored energy in capacitors and DC bus circuits.
  • Regenerative energy from drives or moving machinery.
  • UPS supplies and external interfaces.
  • Pneumatic or hydraulic energy that can move machinery.
  • Unexpected automatic restart or remote commands.
  • Backfeed through connected equipment.

Before work, use the approved isolation and lockout/tagout procedure, identify all relevant energy sources, and verify the required safe condition with approved methods.

Some circuits can remain energized after the main isolator is opened. A drive's DC bus, for example, may retain hazardous energy until the specified discharge period has elapsed and the required verification has been completed.

Do not rely solely on indicator lamps, HMI messages, or the position of a switch to establish absence of hazardous voltage.

17. Practical Case Studies

Case 1: PLC and HMI both lose power

Situation: The machine stops and both the PLC and HMI appear unpowered.

Investigation: The architecture shows that both devices depend on a shared control-power branch. The incoming supply is present, but the control-power supply output is unavailable.

Action: Follow the approved isolation procedure, inspect and test the relevant circuit using the authorized method, and identify the cause of the control-power failure.

Lesson: A common supply dependency can explain simultaneous failure of several devices.

Case 2: PLC is healthy but a motor does not run

Situation: The PLC is running and the HMI is available, but the conveyor motor does not operate.

Investigation: The SLD identifies the motor feeder and drive. Drive diagnostics and the control schematic are used to distinguish between missing power, a protective trip, a drive fault, and a control permissive.

Action: Correct the verified cause, then validate the motor circuit and machine process under the approved procedure.

Lesson: Control-system availability does not prove that the power circuit for an actuator is healthy.

Case 3: Remote I/O section loses communication

Situation: The PLC remains operational, but a remote I/O station is unavailable.

Investigation: The architecture shows the station's control-power source. The electrical record is checked alongside network topology and I/O diagnostics to determine whether the issue is loss of power or a communication fault.

Action: Investigate the confirmed fault path rather than immediately replacing the remote I/O module.

Lesson: Electrical power and industrial communication are separate dependencies that can produce similar symptoms.

Case 4: Repeated protective-device trips

Situation: A branch protective device trips repeatedly after a maintenance intervention.

Investigation: The circuit is identified from the drawing. The load, wiring, device ratings, and possible fault conditions are reviewed using the approved electrical procedure.

Action: Correct the identified cause and verify the circuit before returning it to service.

Lesson: Repeated tripping is evidence requiring investigation, not a reason to install a larger protective device without engineering approval.

18. Knowledge Check and Interview Questions

1. What is the purpose of an electrical architecture overview?

It explains the overall supply and distribution structure, major panels, protection, conversion stages, and electrical loads of a machine.

2. What is the difference between an SLD and a wiring diagram?

An SLD summarizes the power distribution structure. A wiring diagram shows more detailed electrical connections, conductors, and terminals.

3. Why should control-power supplies be documented separately?

They may feed several control devices, and their failure can cause partial or widespread loss of machine control.

4. Why is the machine's main isolator not necessarily the only isolation point?

The machine may have separate auxiliary supplies, external interfaces, stored energy, or other energy sources requiring independent isolation.

5. What is the difference between protective earth and functional earth?

Protective earth serves electrical safety requirements. Functional earth serves specified functional or EMC purposes. The approved design determines their arrangement.

6. Why should a breaker not be replaced with a higher-rated device just to stop tripping?

The trip may indicate a fault, and changing the rating without design review can compromise conductor and equipment protection.

7. What information helps identify the cause of a PLC power failure?

The documented supply path, upstream protection, control-power supply, branch distribution, detailed schematic, and verified electrical measurements.

8. Why must the panel layout and SLD be maintained together?

The SLD shows electrical relationships, while the panel layout helps locate the physical devices involved.

9. What should be recorded for each major feeder?

Its circuit identity, protective device, downstream load, supply characteristics, and relevant drawing reference.

10. What is the most important principle of electrical architecture documentation?

The documentation must reflect the approved installed system and support both accurate troubleshooting and safe maintenance.

19. Final Verification Checklist

  • Incoming supply and source are identified.
  • Main isolator and protective-device references are documented.
  • Electrical panels have unique IDs and defined functions.
  • Major power feeders and loads are mapped.
  • Control-power sources and downstream loads are identified.
  • The single-line diagram matches the approved architecture.
  • Detailed schematics, wiring diagrams, and panel layouts are referenced.
  • Protective earthing and bonding documentation is available.
  • Separate and auxiliary supplies are identified.
  • Actual ratings and device details have been verified.
  • Isolation procedures and stored-energy hazards are referenced.
  • Drawing revisions and record ownership are controlled.
  • Known gaps or discrepancies are recorded for engineering review.
