1. Purpose of RCA and CAPA

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is a structured investigation used to determine why a failure, defect, incident or other unwanted event occurred. Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) is the controlled process for addressing the identified problem, reducing recurrence, and verifying that the actions are effective.

In industrial maintenance, RCA/CAPA connects breakdown history to permanent technical and organizational improvement.

For example, repeatedly replacing a failed proximity sensor may restore a machine temporarily. If the sensor is failing because its cable is exposed to repeated mechanical damage, replacing sensors alone does not address the underlying problem. RCA investigates the failure mechanism and its contributing conditions; CAPA implements and verifies appropriate changes to eliminate or reduce recurrence.

RCA/CAPA is useful for:

  • Repeated machine breakdowns.
  • Major production downtime.
  • Safety-related events and dangerous failures.
  • Quality defects, scrap and process instability.
  • Repeated PLC, CNC, HMI, VFD, servo or network faults.
  • Component failures despite regular preventive maintenance.
  • Incorrect settings, configuration errors and software changes.
  • Failures caused by inadequate installation, maintenance or design.
  • Problems that affect multiple machines or production areas.

Core principle: Restoring the machine resolves the immediate operational problem. RCA/CAPA addresses why the problem occurred, why existing controls did not prevent or detect it, and whether the implemented solution actually works.

2. RCA, Correction, Corrective Action and Preventive Action

These terms describe different activities and should not be used interchangeably.

Term Meaning Industrial example
Containment Limits immediate consequences while the cause is investigated Inspect comparable cables and restrict operation where damage creates an unacceptable risk
Correction Fixes the specific detected problem Replace the damaged cable
Root Cause Analysis Investigates the failure mechanism, causes and control weaknesses Determine why the cable was repeatedly damaged
Corrective action Addresses the cause of an existing problem to prevent recurrence Reroute and protect the cable using an approved design
Preventive action Addresses a potential similar problem before it occurs elsewhere Apply a validated cable-protection standard to comparable machines
Effectiveness verification Checks whether the action achieves the intended result Confirm that the cable remains protected and the fault does not recur over an appropriate observation period

Terminology can vary between organizational quality systems. Some systems use CAPA as the overall process for both existing and potential problems, while others distinguish corrective action from preventive action more strictly. Follow the site's approved definitions.

A useful distinction is:

  • Correction: Fix this failure.
  • Corrective action: Address the cause of this failure.
  • Preventive action: Reduce the risk of a similar failure elsewhere.
  • Effectiveness verification: Prove that the action worked.

3. RCA/CAPA Workflow

Failure, Defect or Incident
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
Analyze Failure Mechanisms and Contributing Factors
        ↓
Identify and Verify Root Cause
        ↓
Select Corrective and Preventive Actions
        ↓

        ↓
Implement Actions Under Change Control
        ↓
Verify Technical Results and Effectiveness
        ↓
Update PM, Machine Knowledge Base and Related Records

RCA and CAPA are related but not identical. An investigation can identify a cause without an effective action being implemented. Likewise, an immediate repair can be completed while the wider RCA/CAPA remains open.

4. When Should RCA/CAPA Be Started?

Not every minor fault needs a formal, resource-intensive investigation. Investigation depth should match the consequences, recurrence risk and uncertainty.

4.1 Example escalation criteria

Trigger Example Typical response
Safety significance Failure or defect affecting a protective function Immediate safety controls and formal investigation under site procedures
Major downtime Production bottleneck stops for an unacceptable period Structured RCA and recovery improvement
Repeated breakdown Same failure mode occurs repeatedly Investigate recurrence and action effectiveness
Quality impact Measurement error could release nonconforming product Containment, traceability and quality investigation
High repair cost Expensive component repeatedly fails Technical and economic investigation
Unexplained failure Failure cause remains uncertain and consequences are significant Preserve evidence and perform deeper diagnosis
Multiple assets affected Similar faults appear across machines Investigate shared causes and common controls
PM failure A failure occurs despite a task intended to detect or prevent it Review task scope, interval, method and follow-up
Change-related failure A fault follows a program, parameter, firmware or hardware change Review the change, compatibility and validation evidence
Supplier or design concern A component or design repeatedly fails under specified conditions Engineering and supplier investigation

Use the site's actual escalation thresholds. A safety-related event may require formal investigation regardless of downtime, cost or recurrence.

5. Define the Problem Before Searching for Causes

A weak problem statement leads to a weak investigation. Statements such as "PLC problem," "operator error" or "machine not working" describe broad areas or symptoms rather than a measurable problem.

A useful problem statement defines:

  • What: The observed failure or defect.
  • Where: The machine, subsystem and component.
  • When: Date, time, operating state and relevant history.
  • Impact: Downtime, quality, safety or other consequence.
  • Expected condition: What the equipment should have done.
  • Actual condition: What it did instead.
  • Evidence: Fault codes, readings, logs, photographs or test results.
  • Scope: Which assets, products, cycles or operating conditions are affected.

5.1 Weak versus strong problem statements

Weak statement Stronger statement
PLC fault PLC input I:2/3 lost the expected sensor state during automatic operation, causing three conveyor stops during the recorded shift
VFD tripping Drive fault history recorded an overcurrent trip during acceleration; the event timestamps and motor operating conditions are preserved for investigation
Sensor problem Position sensor signal became intermittent during repeated machine cycles; the sensor, cable, supply and PLC input remain to be distinguished by testing
Bad maintenance The same failure mode recurred twice after the previous repair; the previous corrective action and verification evidence require review
Operator error The machine was started with a condition outside the approved operating procedure; the event, procedure, interface and relevant training controls require examination

The stronger examples avoid declaring a root cause before the investigation establishes one.

6. Collect and Preserve Evidence

RCA should be based on observable evidence rather than the most convenient explanation.

6.1 Evidence sources

Evidence source What it can establish
PLC diagnostic buffer Fault sequence, timestamps, controller state and diagnostic messages
HMI alarm history Operator-visible alarms and event order
VFD or servo fault history Drive faults, operating state and relevant recorded measurements
CNC or robot logs Controller alarms, axis conditions and program state
Network diagnostics Link loss, communication errors, device availability and topology-related clues
Electrical measurements Actual values measured at specified points and under stated conditions
Mechanical inspection Wear, misalignment, damage, obstruction or other physical conditions
Pneumatic or hydraulic readings Pressure, leakage, temperature or flow-related evidence
PM history Previous inspections, recorded defects and maintenance intervals
Breakdown history Recurrence patterns, repair duration and previous actions
Spare-part history Component replacement frequency, supplier information and part revisions
Change records Recent wiring, program, parameter, firmware or hardware changes
Operator observations What occurred immediately before and during the failure
Quality records Defective product, measurement deviations and affected production
Photographs and test reports Physical condition and documented test results

6.2 Evidence-handling rules

  • Capture fault codes and timestamps before clearing them when safe and practical.
  • Preserve the original log or file where possible.
  • Record measurement units, test points, operating conditions and instrument identification where relevant.
  • Separate observed facts from assumptions.
  • Record both positive and negative test results.
  • Document any reset, restart, disconnection, replacement or configuration change that could alter the evidence.
  • Protect confidential production, cybersecurity and personnel information according to site rules.
  • Preserve failed components when useful for engineering or supplier analysis.
  • Do not delay urgent safety controls merely to collect evidence.

A negative result can be valuable. For example, a sensor may pass a bench test but fail when installed because of vibration, mounting, target distance, contamination or electrical interference. The difference between test conditions can be an important clue.

7. Failure Analysis Methods

Different RCA methods serve different purposes. Select the method that matches the problem rather than applying every available technique to every failure.

7.1 Five Whys

The Five Whys method repeatedly asks why a failure occurred, following evidence through the causal chain. Five is a guide, not a mandatory stopping point.

Example: a sensor cable is damaged on a conveyor.

Stage Investigation question Illustrative finding
Problem Why did the conveyor stop? The PLC lost the position sensor signal
Why 1 Why was the signal lost? The sensor cable conductor was damaged
Why 2 Why was the conductor damaged? The cable repeatedly contacted a moving bracket
Why 3 Why could it contact the bracket? The cable route did not maintain adequate clearance throughout the motion
Why 4 Why was the route inadequate? The routing design did not account for the full movement envelope
Why 5 Why was this not detected before recurrence? Installation validation did not verify cable clearance across the complete motion range

This example suggests potential design and validation weaknesses, but each finding must be confirmed on the actual machine. If the evidence does not support a step, revise the chain rather than forcing an answer.

Limitations: Five Whys can oversimplify complex failures, encourage blame or stop too early. Use multiple causal branches when several conditions contributed.

7.2 Fishbone (Ishikawa) Analysis

Fishbone analysis organizes possible causes into categories. It is particularly useful when a problem may have several interacting causes.

Common categories include:

  • Machine: Hardware defects, wear, alignment, cooling or mechanical design.
  • Method: Procedures, setup, maintenance instructions and validation.
  • Material: Components, cables, lubricants, raw materials or consumables.
  • Measurement: Instrument suitability, calibration, signal quality and measurement method.
  • People: Competence, communication, workload and task execution.
  • Environment: Temperature, dust, vibration, moisture, electromagnetic interference or contamination.
  • Management and system controls: Change control, spare strategy, scheduling, design review and supervision.

Not every category applies to every problem. Treat entries as hypotheses until they are tested.

7.3 Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)

Fault Tree Analysis starts with an undesired top event and works backward through combinations of conditions that could cause it.

Example:

Top event: Conveyor stops unexpectedly
        ↓
PLC does not receive a valid position signal
        ↓
Possible causes:
  - Sensor does not detect the target
  - Cable or connector interrupts the signal
  - Sensor power supply is lost
  - PLC input or communication path fails
  - Control logic rejects or ignores the signal

A real fault tree should show logical relationships between causes, including AND/OR conditions where appropriate. The simple hierarchy above is only a starting list, not a completed quantitative fault tree.

FTA is useful for complex systems, safety-related analysis and cases where combinations of failures matter.

7.4 Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)

FMEA systematically examines how a component or process can fail, what happens when it fails, and what controls detect or prevent the failure.

Typical fields include:

  • Item or process step.
  • Intended function.
  • Potential failure mode.
  • Failure effect.
  • Potential cause or mechanism.
  • Existing prevention controls.
  • Existing detection controls.
  • Severity, occurrence and detection ratings where the chosen method uses them.
  • Recommended action.
  • Responsible owner and due date.
  • Verification of action effectiveness.

FMEA is primarily a structured risk-analysis tool. It can inform RCA and CAPA, but completing an FMEA alone does not prove the root cause of a specific breakdown.

7.5 Change-point and trend analysis

When failures begin after a particular event, compare the failure history with the change history.

Potential change points include:

  • New firmware or software version.
  • PLC program or drive parameter modification.
  • Cable routing or component replacement.
  • Machine relocation or retrofit.
  • New supplier or part revision.
  • Production-rate increase.
  • Changes in ambient temperature or utility supply.
  • Revised maintenance method.

A time correlation is a clue, not proof of causation. Confirm the relationship through technical evidence and controlled testing.

8. Verify the Root Cause

A plausible explanation is not automatically a verified root cause.

A root-cause statement should explain the failure mechanism and identify evidence that supports the conclusion. It should also account for important contradictory evidence.

8.1 Root-cause verification checklist

  • The problem statement is precise and evidence-based.
  • The failure mode is identified separately from the symptom.
  • The failed component or process condition is established where possible.
  • The proposed cause explains the observed failure.
  • Relevant alternative explanations have been considered.
  • Tests, measurements or inspections support the conclusion.
  • Contradictory findings have been addressed.
  • Contributing factors and failed controls are documented.
  • The conclusion does not rely solely on replacing a component and observing that the machine runs.
  • Remaining uncertainty is recorded.
  • The need for additional testing or a deeper investigation has been assessed.

8.2 Confidence classification

Status Meaning Appropriate use
Confirmed Evidence strongly establishes the cause under the approved investigation standard Root cause can be recorded as verified
Probable Available evidence supports the cause, but material uncertainty remains Record supporting evidence and outstanding uncertainty
Under investigation Evidence is incomplete or several explanations remain possible Keep the investigation open
Unknown The cause could not be established with the available evidence Document work completed and assess further action based on risk

Do not use "confirmed" simply because the machine works after a reset. A reset may temporarily clear a symptom without removing the cause.

9. Immediate Containment and Risk Control

Containment limits consequences while the investigation continues. It does not replace permanent corrective action.

Examples include:

  • Stop and isolate equipment when a hazardous condition requires it.
  • Segregate potentially affected product and initiate the applicable quality disposition process.
  • Inspect comparable components or machines when a shared defect is plausible.
  • Increase monitoring or inspection under an approved temporary plan.
  • Restrict equipment operation when required by risk assessment or site procedure.
  • Preserve diagnostic evidence and failed components.
  • Inform the responsible production, maintenance, engineering, quality and safety personnel as required.

Temporary controls must have a defined owner, scope, review date and removal criteria. Do not allow a temporary workaround to become a permanent, undocumented operating condition.

10. Develop Effective Corrective Actions

A corrective action should address the verified cause, not just the visible symptom.

10.1 Action hierarchy

Action level Example Typical strength
Eliminate the failure mechanism Remove a proven interference or mechanical contact point Strong where the mechanism is fully removed
Engineering redesign Add suitable cable routing and mechanical protection Strong when validated under actual conditions
Improve a technical control Add a validated diagnostic or protective measure Depends on coverage and failure mode
Improve maintenance method Add a specific inspection or measurement Depends on detectability and execution
Improve documentation Update drawings, troubleshooting steps or installation standards Supports consistent execution
Training and competency Train personnel on a revised approved method Necessary where competence is a verified factor, but insufficient for a physical design defect
Reminder or awareness Issue a notice or communicate lessons learned Usually weak as the sole action for a recurring technical failure

Choose the highest practical control that addresses the verified cause and meets engineering, safety, quality and operational requirements.

10.2 Good action versus weak action

Weak action Stronger action
Tell technicians to be careful Redesign the work method or physical arrangement that created the hazard or failure mechanism
Replace the sensor again Verify the sensor failure mechanism and address the actual cause
Check the cable more often Remove the cable-damage mechanism where feasible and validate the routing
Train operators Verify whether the procedure, interface, workload and competence controls contributed
Monitor the machine Define what to monitor, measurement method, trigger limit, owner and response
Update the checklist Define the exact task, acceptance criteria, frequency and abnormal-result response
Inform the supplier Provide evidence, request technical investigation and track the agreed corrective response

Training, instructions and inspections are valid actions when they address the cause. They are not substitutes for engineering changes when the problem is fundamentally caused by a deficient design or unsuitable component.

An action should not be marked effective merely because the work order is closed. Implementation and effectiveness are different milestones.

12. CAPA Prioritization and Ownership

CAPA priorities should reflect the consequences of leaving the cause unresolved, not just the ease of completing the action.

12.1 Practical priority model

Priority General condition Expected control
Critical Immediate or unacceptable safety, regulatory or major operational exposure Apply immediate controls and required escalation
High Serious recurring failure, major production loss or significant quality risk Assign an accountable owner and prioritize resources
Medium Meaningful reliability loss with manageable interim controls Schedule action with an approved target date
Low Limited impact with justified longer-term improvement potential Track through the improvement process

These are example labels. The site's risk matrix, escalation rules and mandatory requirements take precedence.

12.2 Responsibility model

Role Typical responsibility
Maintenance technician Collect evidence, report findings and perform authorized work
Maintenance engineer Lead technical diagnosis and propose corrective actions
Controls or automation engineer Investigate PLC, HMI, drive, CNC, robot and network causes
Production representative Explain process conditions, operating impact and restart requirements
Quality representative Manage suspect product, quality impact and required disposition
Safety representative Support risk assessment, incident handling and safety validation
Engineering or design owner Approve technical modifications and assess wider applicability
Stores or procurement Support correct part identification, availability and supplier follow-up
CAPA owner Coordinate action delivery, deadlines, evidence and escalation
Independent reviewer Verify evidence and action effectiveness where required

The person who performs an action may also provide technical evidence, but independent review is valuable for significant, safety-related or systemic issues. Apply the site's formal approval requirements.

13. Effectiveness Verification

Effectiveness verification determines whether the action actually prevents or reduces recurrence and achieves the intended result.

13.1 Define effectiveness before implementation

A useful verification plan specifies:

  • What result must be achieved.
  • Which failure mode or risk is being monitored.
  • Which machine or population is included.
  • How results will be measured.
  • What operating conditions must be covered.
  • How long or how many cycles the observation period will last.
  • What counts as a recurrence.
  • Who reviews the evidence.
  • What happens if the action fails the test.

For example, "monitor the machine" is too vague. A stronger criterion might require completion of a specified number of representative production cycles, inspection of the modified cable route across the full movement range, and no recurrence of the identified signal interruption during the defined observation period.

The appropriate duration and sample size depend on the failure frequency, operating exposure and consequences. Zero observed failures over a short period does not prove that a rare failure has been eliminated.

13.2 Verification levels

Level Question Example evidence
Implementation Was the action completed as approved? Work order, drawing revision and inspection
Technical verification Does the modified system meet its specified requirements? Test results, measurements and validation records
Operational verification Does the system work under representative operating conditions? Completed production cycles and operating data
Effectiveness verification Has recurrence or the targeted risk been reduced as intended? Defined monitoring period, failure trends and documented review
Systemic verification Were similar assets and processes addressed where applicable? Review of comparable machines and closure of required actions

13.3 If the action is ineffective

If the same failure returns or the acceptance criteria are not met:

  1. Apply containment and safety controls as required.
  2. Record the recurrence against the original CAPA.
  3. Preserve new evidence and compare it with the previous investigation.
  4. Check whether the original root cause was correct and sufficiently complete.
  5. Review whether the action was implemented correctly.
  6. Investigate new or interacting failure mechanisms.
  7. Revise the action plan and risk assessment.
  8. Repeat technical and effectiveness verification.
  9. Keep the CAPA open or reopen it according to the site's procedure.

An ineffective action is new evidence about the problem, not a reason to change the status to closed and hope the machine develops better manners.

14. Engineering Change Control

Some CAPA actions alter machine hardware, electrical design, PLC logic, drive parameters, CNC settings, robot programs, safety systems or industrial networks. These changes require controlled engineering review.

14.1 Before the change

  • Define the problem and approved technical solution.
  • Identify affected machines, components and interfaces.
  • Review electrical, mechanical, software, process, quality and safety impacts.
  • Check compatibility of hardware, firmware and engineering software.
  • Confirm backups and recovery arrangements.
  • Define the test plan and acceptance criteria.
  • Identify required permits, production windows and qualified personnel.
  • Obtain the required technical and management approvals.

14.2 After the change

  • Record installed components and revisions.
  • Update drawings, bills of material and configuration records.
  • Update PLC, HMI, drive, CNC or robot backups as applicable.
  • Perform required functional and safety validation.
  • Verify product and process acceptance where relevant.
  • Update PM instructions, spare records and troubleshooting procedures.
  • Train affected personnel where required.
  • Record the change reference in the CAPA and machine knowledge base.
  • Monitor effectiveness under representative operating conditions.

Never treat a software download, parameter adjustment or safety-system modification as a routine corrective action without the required authorization and validation. A change that removes one failure mode can introduce another.

15. Example: Repeated VFD Overcurrent Trip

Assume a conveyor drive repeatedly trips during acceleration. Replacing the drive previously restored operation, but the problem returned.

The objective is to investigate the actual failure mechanism rather than automatically replace the same component again.

15.1 Investigation record

Stage Example activity
Problem definition Record drive fault code, event times, operating state and production impact
Evidence collection Preserve fault history and review relevant current, load and configuration information
Hypothesis development Consider mechanical binding, acceleration profile, motor or cable faults, and drive hardware
Controlled testing Perform approved inspections and tests to distinguish competing explanations
Finding Suppose the investigation confirms excessive mechanical resistance in the conveyor mechanism
Root-cause review Determine why the resistance developed and why existing maintenance controls did not detect it
Correction Remove the immediate obstruction or repair the confirmed mechanical defect
Corrective action Address the confirmed source of recurring mechanical resistance
Preventive action Review comparable mechanisms and the relevant inspection strategy
Verification Validate operation under specified load and representative production conditions
Effectiveness review Monitor the defined fault signature and recurrence criteria over the approved observation period

This is an illustrative scenario, not a diagnosis of any actual drive. Excessive mechanical resistance is only one possible explanation for an overcurrent trip.

15.2 Example action register

Action Owner Evidence required Closure condition
Repair the confirmed mechanical defect Maintenance Completed work order and inspection Mechanical condition meets specification
Validate conveyor operation Maintenance and production Recorded test under representative conditions Required operation and process criteria pass
Review similar mechanisms Engineering Documented assessment of comparable equipment Applicable defects and required actions identified
Review PM task effectiveness Maintenance engineering Approved revision or documented justification Task scope and criteria address the verified failure mechanism
Monitor recurrence CAPA owner Fault history and operating exposure Defined effectiveness criteria satisfied

The action register should use actual assigned owners and dates in a live investigation. Example entries must never be copied as evidence that a real action has been completed.

16. CAPA and Preventive Maintenance Integration

RCA/CAPA should improve the maintenance system when an investigation reveals that existing PM tasks, intervals or inspection methods were insufficient.

16.1 When PM needs revision

Review the relevant PM plan if evidence shows that:

  • A degradation mechanism develops between inspections.
  • The current checklist does not inspect the failed component.
  • The task is too vague to identify the defect reliably.
  • The acceptance criteria cannot distinguish acceptable from unacceptable condition.
  • The inspection method cannot detect the failure before functional loss.
  • A maintenance activity introduces damage or incorrect adjustment.
  • Findings were recorded but not escalated.
  • A new component, retrofit or design change alters maintenance requirements.

16.2 Avoid automatic frequency increases

A failure during a PM interval does not automatically mean that PM should occur more frequently. First determine whether the task is technically capable of detecting or preventing the failure.

Possible responses include:

  • Add a relevant inspection point.
  • Use a different measurement or diagnostic method.
  • Change the acceptance criterion.
  • Introduce condition monitoring.
  • Improve the installation or component design.
  • Improve spare availability or recovery procedures.
  • Revise the frequency when evidence supports the change.

If a cable is damaged because it rubs against a moving structure, inspecting it daily may reduce detection delay, but correcting the routing may be the stronger permanent action.

17. RCA/CAPA Integration with Breakdown and Spare Records

CAPA should preserve the relationship between the failure, technical investigation, replacement parts and the resulting engineering changes.

Related record Information to connect
Breakdown history Original event, downtime, symptoms and fault evidence
Work order Repair steps, labor, materials and technical restoration
Spare register Part number, revision, compatibility, replacement frequency and supplier information
PM history Relevant inspection tasks, prior findings and approved revisions
Backup register Configuration before and after authorized changes
Obsolescence register Part availability, replacement compatibility and migration risk
Engineering change record Approved design, software, hardware or process modifications
Quality record Product impact, inspection and disposition
Safety record Required hazard review, incident investigation and validation
CAPA register Verified cause, action owners, due dates and effectiveness evidence

For example, if a component fails because it is unsuitable for the actual operating environment, the investigation may need to address the part specification, approved alternatives, stock inventory and installation standards together.

18. RCA/CAPA Performance Indicators

Measure both execution and effectiveness. Closing actions quickly is not the same as solving problems permanently.

KPI Example definition Interpretation
RCA completion on time Investigations completed by their approved due dates divided by investigations due Investigation execution
CAPA on-time closure Actions closed by their approved due dates divided by actions due Action delivery
Repeat failure rate Defined repeat failures divided by the relevant failure population Recurrence performance
Action effectiveness rate Actions passing effectiveness verification divided by actions evaluated Whether implemented actions work
Overdue high-risk actions Count of overdue actions above the site's defined risk threshold Outstanding exposure
Average investigation duration Total elapsed investigation duration divided by completed investigations Investigation lead time
Repeat breakdown downtime Downtime attributable to defined repeat failure modes Operational impact of recurrence
Unknown-cause rate Events with unknown causes divided by the defined event population Diagnostic coverage and investigation capability
CAPA reopen rate Closed CAPAs reopened due to ineffectiveness divided by closed CAPAs in the reporting population Quality of investigations and actions
Systemic action coverage Comparable assets addressed divided by comparable assets identified as requiring action Extent of wider risk control

Define each KPI precisely before reporting it. For example, a high unknown-cause rate can indicate weak diagnostics, but it can also reflect a population of highly intermittent faults or cases where evidence was irretrievably lost. Interpret the metric alongside risk and case complexity.

19. Common RCA/CAPA Failures

Failure Why it is ineffective Better practice
Stopping at the first plausible cause Important contributing conditions may remain Test alternatives and confirm the causal chain
Blaming a person without examining the system Procedures, interfaces, workload or design may be contributing Investigate human and system factors using evidence
Treating a reset as a permanent repair The underlying failure may remain Verify the actual failure mechanism and recurrence risk
Replacing components repeatedly Repetition may hide a system-level defect Determine why each component failed
Using training as the only action Training cannot correct every design or hardware problem Match action type to the verified cause
Defining no effectiveness criteria Closure becomes subjective Specify measurable acceptance and observation criteria
Closing CAPA after implementation alone The action may be ineffective under real conditions Separate implementation from effectiveness verification
Ignoring similar assets The same defect may remain elsewhere Assess the wider installed population
Failing to control engineering changes A fix may introduce compatibility or safety problems Apply formal change control and validation
Allowing temporary controls to become permanent Residual risk may be forgotten Assign owners, expiry or review dates and removal criteria
Keeping evidence in personal notes only Knowledge disappears during handover or personnel changes Store controlled evidence with linked records
Tracking actions without accountability Due dates pass without resolution Assign a named owner, target date and escalation path

21. Practical Exercise

Select one repeated or technically significant failure from a real machine's breakdown history.

  • Write a measurable problem statement.
  • Separate symptoms, alarms, failure modes and confirmed causes.
  • Preserve relevant diagnostic evidence and operating conditions.
  • Compare the current event with previous failures.
  • Develop multiple possible causes where appropriate.
  • Select a suitable RCA method and test the hypotheses.
  • Document the verified cause or clearly state remaining uncertainty.
  • Define immediate containment and risk controls.
  • Propose corrective actions that address the actual cause.
  • Assess whether comparable machines or processes are affected.
  • Assign owners, resources, due dates and implementation evidence.
  • Define effectiveness criteria before closing the actions.
  • Link required changes to PM tasks, drawings, backups and spare records.
  • Review the results after the approved observation period.
  • Reopen the investigation if the action fails its effectiveness criteria.

22. Final Acceptance Checklist

An RCA/CAPA record is ready for closure when the applicable requirements below are satisfied:

  • The problem and impact are clearly defined.
  • Immediate safety, quality and operational risks have been controlled.
  • Relevant evidence has been preserved and reviewed.
  • The failure mode is distinguished from the symptom.
  • The root cause is evidence-based, or remaining uncertainty is formally addressed.
  • Corrective actions address the identified cause.
  • Wider preventive or systemic actions have been assessed.
  • Actions have owners, deadlines and required approvals.
  • Engineering changes have passed the required review and validation.
  • Implementation evidence is available.
  • Effectiveness criteria have been met.
  • Similar assets and processes have been reviewed where appropriate.
  • Required PM, breakdown, spare, backup and engineering records have been updated.
  • Residual risks and outstanding tasks are documented.
  • Required independent review and closure approval are complete.

Expected outcome: The RCA/CAPA register should provide a traceable link from the original failure to the verified cause, approved actions, implementation evidence and demonstrated effectiveness. The goal is not simply to explain a breakdown after it happens. It is to use the evidence to make the machine and its maintenance system more reliable.
