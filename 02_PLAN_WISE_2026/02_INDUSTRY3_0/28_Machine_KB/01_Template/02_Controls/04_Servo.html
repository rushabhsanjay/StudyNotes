1. Purpose of Servo Documentation

A servo system is used when a machine requires controlled motion with defined speed, position, acceleration, torque, or synchronization. Unlike a basic variable-frequency drive application, a servo system is commonly designed for dynamic response and accurate motion control, often using feedback from an encoder or another position-sensing device.

In industrial machinery, servo systems are used for:

  • Precision positioning and indexing.
  • CNC feed axes.
  • Robotic and automated handling mechanisms.
  • Assembly and press-fit operations.
  • Electronic camming and synchronized motion.
  • Tool changers and automatic loading systems.
  • Packaging, cutting, and material-handling equipment.
  • Rotary tables and coordinated machine axes.

The machine knowledge base must explain each servo axis as a complete motion system, not merely list the servo drive model.

A useful servo record identifies the controller, drive, motor, feedback device, mechanical transmission, axis limits, tuning parameters, homing method, safety arrangements, and recovery procedure.

The objective is to make each axis understandable, diagnosable, maintainable, and recoverable after a failure or component replacement.

2. Servo System Architecture

A typical servo axis contains a motion controller, servo drive, servo motor, feedback device, and mechanical transmission.

Motion Controller / PLC / CNC
              ↓
        Motion Command
              ↓
          Servo Drive
              ↓
          Servo Motor
              ↓
      Mechanical Transmission
              ↓
        Machine Axis Motion
              ↑
        Feedback Device
              ↑
          Servo Drive
              ↑
    Position / Speed Feedback

This is a simplified functional view. Actual feedback connections and control loops depend on the servo system architecture. Some systems connect feedback directly to the drive, while others use additional controller-side feedback or multiple feedback devices.

2.1 Motion controller

The motion controller determines the requested movement and coordinates the axis with other machine operations.

It may be implemented in:

  • A CNC controller.
  • A PLC with motion-control capabilities.
  • A dedicated motion controller.
  • A robotic controller.
  • A specialized positioning controller.

The controller may generate position commands, velocity commands, torque commands, or coordinated motion trajectories depending on the system.

2.2 Servo drive

The servo drive converts the controller's motion command into controlled electrical power for the motor.

It typically manages current, torque, speed, and position-related control functions according to its architecture and configuration.

The drive also monitors faults, feedback signals, motor conditions, and communication status where supported.

2.3 Servo motor

The servo motor converts electrical power into mechanical motion.

Common motor technologies include permanent-magnet synchronous motors and other motor designs supported by the installed drive.

Record the exact motor model because motor feedback, electrical characteristics, mechanical dimensions, and compatible drive combinations can be manufacturer-specific.

2.4 Feedback device

A feedback device reports motor or axis movement to the control system.

Common devices include:

  • Incremental encoders.
  • Absolute encoders.
  • Resolver-based feedback devices.
  • Linear scales.
  • Separate motor and load feedback devices in selected systems.

Feedback helps the controller or drive compare requested motion with measured motion and correct the error.

2.5 Mechanical transmission

The motor may be connected to the machine through:

  • A direct coupling.
  • A gearbox.
  • A ball screw.
  • A belt and pulley system.
  • A rack-and-pinion arrangement.
  • A rotary table transmission.
  • A linear motor arrangement.

Mechanical backlash, compliance, friction, wear, coupling damage, and load variation can affect axis accuracy even when the electrical servo system appears healthy.

4. Servo Axes in Different Machines

An axis is a controlled movement dimension or mechanism. Axis names depend on the machine's coordinate system and functional design.

4.1 CNC machine

A conventional three-axis CNC milling machine commonly uses:

  • X-axis: movement along the machine's X coordinate.
  • Y-axis: movement along the machine's Y coordinate.
  • Z-axis: movement along the machine's Z coordinate.

Additional axes may include rotary A, B, or C axes, depending on the machine design and coordinate convention.

The physical component that moves along each coordinate depends on the machine construction. For example, a machine may move the table along X and Y while the spindle head moves along Z.

4.2 Assembly machine

A servo-driven assembly station might use:

  • A horizontal transfer axis.
  • A vertical insertion axis.
  • A rotary indexing axis.
  • A servo press axis.

Each axis has a distinct function, travel range, load profile, and process requirement.

4.3 Robotic and handling systems

A robot may use coordinated servo axes for joint movement. A gantry or transfer system may use independent linear axes.

The controller may coordinate multiple axes as a group. An individual axis fault can therefore affect the entire coordinated movement.

4.4 Test bench

A test bench may use servo motion to position a fixture, control a mechanical loading mechanism, or operate a specialized actuator. A separate spindle or test motor may use a different drive architecture.

Do not classify every motor on a test bench as a servo axis. Record the actual control system and purpose.

5. Essential Servo Motion Concepts

5.1 Position

Position is the location of an axis relative to its coordinate reference.

Depending on the system, position may be expressed in:

  • Millimetres.
  • Degrees.
  • Encoder counts.
  • Internal engineering units.

For example, a linear axis may be commanded to move to 125.0 mm, while a rotary axis may be commanded to 90 degrees.

The displayed value must be interpreted according to the controller's coordinate system and scaling.

5.2 Velocity

Velocity describes the rate of position change.

For a linear axis, the unit may be mm/s or mm/min. For a rotary axis, the unit may be degrees/s or rpm.

The maximum permitted velocity depends on the mechanical design, motor and drive capabilities, process requirements, and approved machine limits.

5.3 Acceleration and deceleration

Acceleration describes how quickly velocity changes. Deceleration describes the reduction in velocity.

Aggressive acceleration can cause excessive torque demand, following errors, mechanical shock, or drive faults. Aggressive deceleration can produce high regenerative energy or instability.

Motion profiles must be established and validated according to the machine design.

5.4 Torque

Torque is the rotational force produced by the motor. It is required to accelerate the load, overcome friction, and perform the intended mechanical work.

High torque demand may indicate a normal process load or a developing mechanical problem. Interpretation requires the operating context, motor characteristics, and the controller's or drive's torque definition.

5.5 Following error

Following error is the difference between the commanded position and the measured or estimated actual position.

A simplified representation is:

\[ e(t) = x\_{\text{commanded}}(t) - x\_{\text{actual}}(t) \]

Where \(e(t)\) is the position error at time \(t\).

If the error exceeds the configured threshold for the specified duration, the controller or drive may generate a following-error fault.

Possible causes include:

  • Excessive mechanical load.
  • Obstruction or binding.
  • Incorrect tuning.
  • Feedback signal problems.
  • Insufficient torque or current capability.
  • Incorrect motion limits or profile.
  • Mechanical transmission damage.

A following error is a symptom, not a root-cause diagnosis.

6. Servo Operating Modes

Servo systems support different command modes depending on the controller, drive, motor, and application.

Mode General function Typical application
Position control Moves to a requested position Indexing and precision positioning
Velocity control Regulates commanded speed Continuous motion applications
Torque control Regulates torque according to the drive's control architecture Force-related or tension-related applications
Synchronized motion Coordinates multiple axes or follows a master axis Coordinated machine mechanisms
Electronic gearing Maintains a configured motion relationship between axes Synchronized transfer systems
Electronic camming Follows a defined position relationship to a master motion Rotary and packaging mechanisms

The exact implementation and terminology vary by manufacturer. Record the actual mode configured in the installed system.

Changing the mode can alter how commands are interpreted and how the axis behaves. It must be treated as an engineering change, not a routine troubleshooting experiment.

7. Homing and Reference Position

Homing establishes a known reference for the axis coordinate system when required by the machine architecture.

The method may use:

  • A reference or home switch.
  • An encoder index pulse.
  • An absolute encoder reference.
  • A mechanical reference position.
  • A combination of a switch and encoder reference.
  • A controller-specific referencing sequence.

7.1 Why homing matters

The controller needs a reliable reference to interpret machine positions correctly. If the reference is lost or incorrectly established, the displayed coordinate may not correspond to the actual mechanical position.

Potential consequences include:

  • Incorrect positioning.
  • Collision risk.
  • Incorrect workpiece or fixture alignment.
  • Invalid machine-coordinate values.
  • Incorrect automatic sequence behavior.

7.2 Homing record

Field What to document
Homing method Exact method configured
Reference device Switch, encoder, absolute reference, or other device
Reference direction Approved direction of travel
Homing speed Configured speed and unit
Search sequence Relevant controller or drive sequence
Reference offset Approved offset value and units

Do not invent a homing speed or offset. Capture the approved settings from the installed system.

Homing must only be performed under the machine's authorized procedure with the required safety conditions satisfied. A reference switch is not automatically a safety-rated limit device.

8. Absolute and Incremental Feedback

8.1 Incremental encoder

An incremental encoder generates signals representing movement. The controller or drive counts those signals to determine relative position.

Depending on the system, position information may be lost after a power interruption unless a separate reference or retained-position arrangement is provided.

8.2 Absolute encoder

An absolute encoder reports a position value within its supported range. Depending on the encoder and system, this value may be retained across power cycles.

An absolute encoder does not automatically guarantee that the machine coordinate is correct after every intervention. The system may require a reference validation or specific recovery procedure.

8.3 Feedback comparison

Characteristic Incremental feedback Absolute feedback
Position representation Relative movement counts or equivalent signals Encoded position value
Reference requirement Commonly requires referencing after position loss May preserve position information across power cycles
Battery or backup dependency Depends on the wider system Depends on encoder technology and system design
Failure investigation Signal integrity, counting, reference, wiring Communication, position validity, encoder status, configuration
Machine-coordinate validity Depends on referencing and controller state Depends on encoder reference, mounting, configuration, and system requirements

Feedback type, resolution, communication protocol, and replacement compatibility must be recorded accurately.

9. Servo Drive and Motor Configuration

Servo parameters are model-specific. Parameter identifiers, scaling, defaults, and access methods differ between manufacturers and product families.

9.1 Motor and feedback configuration

Common configuration categories include:

  • Motor model and rated characteristics.
  • Feedback device type.
  • Encoder resolution or feedback scaling.
  • Motor direction.
  • Electronic gearing.
  • Position and velocity units.
  • Motor identification data.
  • Feedback interface and communication configuration.
  • Brake control for motors with holding brakes, where applicable.

Incorrect feedback or scaling data can cause position errors, unexpected movement, or faults.

9.2 Motion limits

Important configuration categories include:

  • Maximum velocity.
  • Acceleration and deceleration limits.
  • Position limits.
  • Torque or current limits.
  • Software travel limits.
  • Homing parameters.
  • Following-error thresholds.
  • Motion-profile settings.

Software limits do not replace mechanical stops, guards, limit switches, or safety-rated functions required by the machine design.

10. Servo Tuning

Servo tuning adjusts the control response so the axis follows its command while maintaining the required stability and performance.

The controller or drive may use nested control loops, such as:

  • Current or torque loop.
  • Velocity loop.
  • Position loop.

The exact arrangement depends on the servo architecture.

10.1 Common tuning concepts

Gain: Determines aspects of the controller's response to an error. Excessive gain can contribute to oscillation, noise, or instability.

Damping: Influences how oscillation is reduced and how the axis settles after movement.

Inertia ratio: Relates the effective mechanical load inertia to the motor inertia according to the manufacturer's definition.

Feedforward: Uses the commanded motion profile to improve tracking performance without relying solely on feedback error correction.

Resonance suppression: Certain drives provide filters or suppression functions to address mechanical resonance within supported limits.

10.2 Symptoms of tuning or mechanical problems

Symptom Possible areas to investigate
Axis oscillates at standstill Tuning, feedback noise, mechanical resonance, load condition
Axis overshoots target Tuning, trajectory settings, load characteristics, control configuration
Axis settles slowly Motion profile, tuning, friction, mechanical compliance
Motor produces abnormal noise Tuning, resonance, motor condition, transmission or mounting
Following error during acceleration Torque demand, acceleration profile, load, tuning, feedback
Position repeatability degrades Mechanical wear, backlash, feedback, thermal effects, mounting
Servo fault appears intermittently Wiring, feedback connection, supply, load, configuration, fault history

Do not adjust gains randomly to suppress symptoms. Record the original values, follow the manufacturer's tuning method, obtain the required authorization, and validate the complete motion system.

11. Mechanical Factors Affecting Axis Performance

Electrical and mechanical conditions are closely connected in a servo system.

11.1 Backlash

Backlash is lost motion between mechanical components when direction changes. It may arise from gearboxes, ball screws, couplings, or other transmissions.

Backlash can cause positioning errors even when the servo feedback indicates that the motor reached its commanded position.

11.2 Mechanical binding

Binding increases the force required to move the axis. It may result from damaged bearings, misalignment, lubrication problems, contamination, a damaged screw, or an obstruction.

11.3 Coupling and transmission condition

A loose coupling, damaged belt, worn gearbox, or slipping mechanical connection can create a difference between motor movement and actual load movement.

If feedback is measured only at the motor, the control system may not directly detect every mechanical error between the motor and the load.

11.4 Thermal effects

Thermal expansion can affect machine geometry, particularly on precision axes. Machine builders may implement warm-up cycles, compensation tables, or temperature-dependent corrections.

Record the approved procedure rather than assuming that a thermal position error should be corrected by changing servo gain.

12. PLC, CNC, and Motion Controller Interface

A servo axis may be commanded by a CNC, a PLC motion system, a robot controller, or a dedicated motion controller.

The knowledge base should distinguish motion commands from axis status and safety-related signals.

12.2 In-position is not proof of process completion

An in-position signal usually indicates that the axis meets a configured motion condition. It does not necessarily prove that:

  • A clamp has securely held the workpiece.
  • A press operation achieved the required force.
  • A tool is correctly engaged.
  • A fixture is mechanically locked.
  • The process quality requirement has been met.

The machine sequence should use the required independent process feedback and interlocks.

  • Controller and drive identities.
  • Axis configuration in the engineering project.
  • Drive and feedback addresses where applicable.
  • Data exchange configuration.
  • Firmware and software compatibility.
  • Relevant network diagnostics.
  • Approved project and parameter backups.
  • Recovery and commissioning instructions.

13. Servo Safety and Motion Hazards

Servo systems can generate unexpected movement, high torque, and hazardous mechanical forces. A stationary axis is not necessarily safe to approach.

Relevant hazards include:

  • Unexpected restart.
  • Gravity-driven movement of vertical axes.
  • Stored energy in mechanical systems.
  • Automatic movement after enabling the drive.
  • Incorrect position reference.
  • Failure of mechanical holding arrangements.
  • Unexpected movement during homing.
  • High force during collision or obstruction.

13.1 Safety principles

  • Follow the plant's approved lockout/tagout and machine-isolation procedure.
  • Verify all relevant energy sources and stored-energy hazards.
  • Use approved procedures for checking electrical isolation and DC-bus discharge.
  • Apply the machine's defined safety functions and operating modes.
  • Never bypass an interlock or safety-rated function to make an axis move.
  • Do not assume that a normal PLC stop or software position limit is a safety function.
  • Treat vertical axes and holding brakes according to the machine builder's documented requirements.
  • Perform commissioning and motion tests only under the authorized procedure.

Where functions such as Safe Torque Off (STO), Safe Stop, or safe limited speed are installed, record the actual implemented function, its safety design, and the validation documentation.

STO prevents the drive from generating motor torque under its specified conditions. It does not necessarily isolate electrical power, prevent gravity-driven movement, or eliminate stored energy. Mechanical restraint and electrical isolation must be handled according to the risk assessment and applicable procedure.

14. Servo Fault Diagnosis

A servo fault should be investigated using the controller alarm, drive fault history, actual axis conditions, and the machine's mechanical state.

Fault or symptom Initial diagnostic areas
Servo does not enable Enable chain, active faults, controller state, drive readiness, configured permissions
Encoder fault Feedback device, wiring, connector, power, configuration, compatibility
Following error Mechanical load, obstruction, motion profile, tuning, feedback
Overcurrent or overload Load, acceleration demand, motor data, wiring, mechanical condition
Motor overheating Duty cycle, cooling, load, motor compatibility, settings
Homing failure Reference switch, sensor signal, travel conditions, homing configuration
Position offset Reference validity, scaling, coordinate system, mechanical movement
Abnormal vibration Tuning, resonance, transmission, mounting, feedback quality
Communication fault Network, device configuration, cabling, controller status
Brake-related fault Brake wiring, control sequence, feedback where available, mechanical condition

These are initial investigation paths, not proof of root cause.

14.1 Fault evidence to capture

Record:

  • Exact alarm and fault code.
  • Axis identity.
  • Controller and drive state.
  • Commanded and actual position.
  • Commanded and actual velocity.
  • Following error.
  • Available current or torque data.
  • Operating mode and sequence step.
  • Homing or reference state.
  • Mechanical condition and recent maintenance.
  • Relevant parameter changes.
  • Corrective action and validation result.

Capturing the state before resetting the system can preserve evidence that would otherwise be lost.

15. Servo Recovery and Axis Recommissioning

Recovery after a drive, motor, encoder, controller, or mechanical replacement requires more than restoring power.

A controlled recovery process includes:

  1. Identify the failed component and affected axis.
  2. Record the original alarm and available diagnostic evidence.
  3. Confirm the replacement component's compatibility.
  4. Follow the approved energy-isolation procedure before physical intervention.
  5. Restore wiring, feedback connections, grounding, and mechanical interfaces according to the drawings.
  6. Restore the approved parameter and controller configuration.
  7. Verify feedback scaling, motor direction, and reference configuration according to the authorized commissioning procedure.
  8. Check the relevant safety functions and protective devices.
  9. Perform the prescribed reference or homing procedure when required.
  10. Conduct authorized motion tests within the defined limits.
  11. Verify position, repeatability, synchronization, and process interlocks as applicable.
  12. Record the restored configuration and validation results.

Testing should proceed according to the machine builder's commissioning sequence. A movement test is not a substitute for validating safety functions.

17. Servo Replacement and Obsolescence Management

A servo drive or motor replacement must be checked against the complete axis design.

Verify:

  • Drive and motor compatibility.
  • Motor power and current ratings.
  • Feedback type and protocol.
  • Connector and cable compatibility.
  • Mechanical mounting and shaft dimensions.
  • Brake configuration, if fitted.
  • Controller and firmware compatibility.
  • Parameter conversion requirements.
  • Motion network compatibility.
  • Regenerative and braking arrangements.
  • Safety functions and their validation requirements.
  • Required engineering software and licenses.

A replacement component that physically fits may still be incompatible with the controller, feedback device, or approved motion performance.

The knowledge base should distinguish a verified direct replacement from an engineering-approved retrofit requiring changes and recommissioning.

20. Practical Case Studies

Case 1: Servo axis following-error alarm during acceleration

Situation: A linear axis moves slowly but faults during a rapid positioning move.

Investigation: Capture commanded and actual position, following error, velocity, torque or current information, and the operating sequence. Inspect the mechanical transmission under the approved isolation procedure.

Possible causes: Excessive acceleration demand, binding, mechanical damage, insufficient torque capability, incorrect tuning, or feedback problems.

Correct approach: Confirm the initiating condition before changing the motion profile or tuning. Apply the approved correction and validate the axis across its defined operating conditions.

Lesson: A following-error alarm identifies a tracking problem, not necessarily a defective servo drive.

Case 2: Axis position is incorrect after maintenance

Situation: The servo reports a valid position, but the machine coordinate does not match the expected mechanical reference.

Investigation: Verify the reference state, encoder configuration, coordinate system, scaling, offsets, and any mechanical movement during maintenance.

Possible causes: Lost or invalid reference, incorrect scaling, altered mechanical coupling, or a configuration mismatch.

Correct approach: Follow the authorized referencing and validation procedure. Do not compensate for an unknown mechanical or feedback error by arbitrarily changing the coordinate offset.

Lesson: A plausible position display does not guarantee a valid machine coordinate.

Case 3: Servo vibration after a motor replacement

Situation: A replacement motor operates but produces abnormal vibration or noise.

Investigation: Verify motor compatibility, feedback configuration, coupling alignment, mounting, wiring, and the approved tuning parameters.

Possible causes: Incorrect feedback settings, mechanical misalignment, resonance, or incompatible motor configuration.

Correct approach: Correct verified installation or configuration problems and perform approved tuning and validation if required.

Lesson: Replacing a motor can change the effective mechanical response and may require controlled recommissioning.

Case 4: Servo axis fails to home

Situation: The axis starts its referencing sequence but does not complete it.

Investigation: Review the controller's homing state, reference input status, configured direction, travel conditions, feedback diagnostics, and relevant interlocks.

Possible causes: Faulty reference sensor, incorrect homing settings, wiring issue, obstruction, or a feedback problem.

Correct approach: Diagnose the failed condition under the approved procedure. Never bypass the reference input or force the axis through an unsafe movement to complete homing.

Lesson: Homing is a controlled machine operation with both coordinate and safety implications.

21. Interview and Knowledge Check

1. What is the difference between a VFD and a servo system?

A VFD commonly provides variable-speed motor control. A servo system is designed for controlled motion and typically provides more demanding position, speed, or torque performance through a motion-control architecture.

2. What is following error?

It is the difference between the commanded position and the actual or estimated position. Excessive error may trigger a fault.

3. Why is feedback-device compatibility important?

The drive and controller must interpret the feedback signals and position information correctly. Incompatible feedback can cause faults, incorrect position reporting, or unexpected behavior.

4. What is homing?

Homing establishes a reference for the axis coordinate system according to the machine's configured method.

5. Why can an axis have accurate motor feedback but poor load positioning?

Mechanical backlash, coupling slip, compliance, or transmission damage can cause the load position to differ from the motor's measured position.

6. Why should servo tuning not be changed randomly?

Incorrect tuning can cause instability, oscillation, poor tracking, excessive mechanical stress, or drive faults.

7. Does STO isolate the drive's electrical supply?

No. STO is a torque-prevention safety function under its specified conditions. It does not necessarily provide electrical isolation or prevent gravity-driven movement.

8. What should be checked after replacing a servo drive?

Verify compatibility, restore the approved configuration, validate feedback and motion settings, check safety functions, and perform the authorized recommissioning tests.

9. Why should the axis backup include both controller and drive configuration?

The axis behavior may depend on configuration stored in multiple components. A drive parameter file alone may not restore the full motion system.

10. What is the most important principle in servo maintenance?

Diagnose the entire motion chain, including controller, drive, motor, feedback, mechanics, and safety arrangements, then verify the restored axis under its approved operating conditions.

22. Final Verification Checklist

  • Every servo axis has a unique identifier and documented function.
  • Controller, drive, motor, feedback, and mechanical transmission are identified.
  • Approved travel limits and motion settings are recorded.
  • Homing and reference behavior are documented.
  • Feedback scaling and motor configuration are available.
  • PLC, CNC, or motion-controller interfaces are documented.
  • Safety functions and required verification records are identified.
  • Fault codes, diagnostic evidence, and recovery procedures are recorded.
  • Controller projects and drive parameters have verified backups.
  • Replacement compatibility and obsolescence risks are assessed.
  • Maintenance and repeatability checks are defined.
  • Configuration changes are authorized and traceable.
  • As-built documentation is updated after approved changes.
  • Functional and safety validation is recorded before return to production.
