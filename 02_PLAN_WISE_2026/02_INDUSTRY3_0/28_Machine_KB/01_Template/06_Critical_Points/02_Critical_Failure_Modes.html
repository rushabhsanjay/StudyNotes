1. Purpose of Critical Failure Mode Documentation

A critical failure mode is a specific way in which a machine, component, subsystem or process can fail and produce a meaningful consequence for safety, production, quality, reliability or maintainability.

The objective is not merely to maintain a list of faults. It is to capture what can fail, how the failure appears, what causes it, how to diagnose it, how to restore the equipment and what action can prevent recurrence.

For example, “robot fault” is not a useful failure-mode description. “Intermittent robot communication loss caused by a damaged Ethernet cable near the moving dress pack, resulting in cycle interruption” is much more useful because it identifies a failure mechanism, a symptom, a likely location and an operational consequence.

A well-maintained failure-mode register helps maintenance teams:

  • Recognize recurring faults faster.
  • Avoid repeating unsuccessful troubleshooting.
  • Identify weak components and vulnerable machine designs.
  • Reduce mean time to repair (MTTR).
  • Improve mean time between failures (MTBF).
  • Prioritize preventive and condition-based maintenance.
  • Identify required spares, tools and diagnostic skills.
  • Feed evidence into root cause analysis and corrective/preventive actions (RCA/CAPA).
  • Improve machine availability without compromising safety.

Key distinction: A failure mode describes how something fails. A failure cause explains why it failed. A symptom describes what was observed. A corrective action describes what was done. These are related, but they are not interchangeable.

2. Essential Terminology

Term Meaning Industrial example
Failure mode The manner in which an item fails VFD trips on overcurrent
Failure mechanism The physical, electrical or logical process that produces failure Insulation breakdown caused by thermal stress
Failure cause The condition or event responsible for the failure Cooling fan failure leading to overheating
Symptom An observable indication of a problem Drive temperature rises before a trip
Failure effect The consequence at the component or subsystem level Motor stops responding to the speed command
End effect The consequence for the complete machine or production process Test-bed cycle stops and production is delayed
Fault An abnormal condition that can lead to loss of function Encoder signal is missing
Failure Loss of the required ability to perform a function Axis can no longer position within its required tolerance
Root cause An underlying cause established through evidence and analysis Cable routing repeatedly bends the encoder cable below its specified bend radius
Corrective action An action intended to remove the identified cause or correct the condition Reroute and secure the cable according to the approved routing requirements
Preventive action An action intended to prevent similar failures elsewhere or in future Inspect equivalent cable routes and update the design standard
Detection method How the failure is identified Drive diagnostic, trend, inspection or functional test
Failure signature A recognizable pattern of symptoms or measurements Rising motor current followed by repeated thermal trips

A fault message is not automatically the root cause. For instance, a PLC input fault may result from a defective sensor, a broken cable, a missing supply, an incorrect input configuration or an upstream mechanical condition.

3. Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis, or FMEA, is a structured method for identifying possible failures, evaluating their effects and prioritizing risk-reduction actions.

FMEA is useful when commissioning equipment, improving existing machines, investigating recurring faults, planning maintenance and evaluating engineering changes.

FMEA should be based on equipment knowledge, actual fault history, operating conditions and credible failure mechanisms. An invented cause entered into a register can become institutional misinformation if nobody challenges it later.

4. Classifying Critical Failure Modes

A useful register distinguishes failures by their consequences and required response.

Classification Meaning Example
Safety-critical May compromise a safety function or expose people to hazardous conditions Safety-rated protective function fails to operate as designed
Production-critical Stops a bottleneck or major production operation Robot controller fault prevents the line from cycling
Quality-critical Can produce defects or invalidate test results Incorrect torque transducer scaling
Equipment-critical Can damage costly or difficult-to-replace equipment Lubrication failure affecting a spindle
Reliability-critical Repeats frequently or causes substantial downtime Intermittent encoder communication
Maintenance-critical Makes diagnosis or recovery unusually difficult Missing program backup for a discontinued controller
Compliance-critical Can violate an applicable requirement or invalidate required evidence Calibration status expired for a controlled measurement system

A single failure mode can belong to several categories. A defective robot position sensor, for example, might cause production downtime, quality defects and a safety concern depending on its function and the machine design.

Prioritization must follow the site's risk assessment and safety procedures. A low occurrence rate does not make a severe safety consequence acceptable.

5.2 Separate facts from hypotheses

Use explicit confidence labels.

Status Meaning Example
Confirmed Supported by direct evidence or a validated investigation Cable continuity test identifies an open conductor
Probable Strong supporting evidence exists, but confirmation is incomplete Fault consistently occurs at a specific cable position
Suspected Plausible explanation that still requires testing Electromagnetic interference may affect the signal
Rejected Investigated and not supported by the available evidence Replacing the sensor did not change the failure
Unknown Available evidence is insufficient to identify the cause Fault occurred but no diagnostic data was captured

Never convert a suspected cause into a confirmed cause simply because a repair temporarily restored operation. The repair may have changed several conditions at once, or the fault may be intermittent.

6. Common Failure Modes in Industrial Automation

The following sections cover typical weak points across PLCs, drives, motion systems, industrial networks and field devices. They are diagnostic starting points, not universal diagnoses. Always verify the actual equipment model, manufacturer guidance and machine-specific conditions.

Overcurrent trip Mechanical jam, short circuit, incorrect motor data, unsuitable ramp or drive fault Review trip history, motor circuit condition and load behavior Remove the verified cause and validate the drive setup
Overvoltage trip during deceleration Regenerative energy, short deceleration time or unsuitable braking arrangement Review DC-bus diagnostics, stopping conditions and drive application requirements Apply an approved braking or deceleration solution

Do not repeatedly reset a drive without examining the fault history. A reset clears an indication; it does not repair a jammed mechanism, failed cooling system or insulation defect.

6.11 Mechanical systems, lubrication and cooling

Failure mode Possible causes Diagnostic checks Corrective direction
Bearing overheating Lubrication problem, misalignment, contamination or overload Review temperature trends, lubrication and mechanical condition Correct the identified bearing or system cause

Repeated coupling failure Misalignment, shock loading, incorrect installation or unsuitable coupling Inspect failure surfaces, alignment and load conditions Correct alignment or loading and validate the assembly

Replacing a failed bearing without investigating lubrication, alignment, contamination and loading often results in another bearing failure. The replacement may be correct; the system that destroyed it may still be untouched.

7. How to Diagnose a Failure Mode Systematically

Effective troubleshooting moves from the observed symptom to a verified cause. Replacing parts until the machine starts again is not a diagnostic method, even if it occasionally enjoys success.

7.1 Standard diagnostic sequence

  1. Establish the safe state. Assess the hazard, stop the equipment as required and apply the relevant isolation and energy-control procedure.
  2. Capture the original evidence. Record alarms, timestamps, operating conditions, controller status, trend data and operator observations before resetting or changing anything, when safe and feasible.
  3. Define the failure precisely. Identify what function was expected, what actually happened and under what conditions.
  4. Check the simplest credible causes. Review power, utilities, interlocks, connectors, sensors, mechanical obstruction and configuration status.
  5. Trace the complete functional chain. Follow the command, signal, device response and feedback.
  6. Develop competing hypotheses. List plausible causes rather than committing to the first explanation.
  7. Test hypotheses using evidence. Use approved measurements, diagnostics and controlled tests that distinguish one cause from another.
  8. Correct the confirmed defect. Repair or replace the identified failed item, or implement the approved engineering change.
  9. Verify the restoration. Test the affected function and required protective responses according to the approved procedure.
  10. Document the outcome. Record the cause, evidence, action, test result, downtime and any remaining risk.

7.2 Example: Robot stops during a welding cycle

Observed symptom: A welding robot stops intermittently during production and reports a communication-related alarm.

Possible hypotheses include:

  • Damaged network cable or connector.
  • Intermittent remote I/O power supply.
  • Faulty switch port.
  • Controller or device configuration mismatch.
  • Firmware or application compatibility issue.
  • Electrical interference affecting the communication path.

A disciplined investigation correlates alarm timestamps with network diagnostics, remote I/O status, power events and physical inspection. If a damaged cable is identified and the failure can be reproduced or supported by test evidence, the diagnosis becomes substantially stronger.

The corrective action should address the actual defect and any contributing installation weakness. After replacement or repair, verify communication stability, required I/O behavior and the complete affected production sequence.

Record the evidence: An entry that says “changed cable, machine OK” is weaker than one recording the intermittent link loss, identified cable damage, repaired routing, successful verification and subsequent recurrence status.

8. Failure Signatures and Diagnostic Evidence

A failure signature is a pattern of events, measurements or symptoms that helps distinguish one failure mechanism from another.

Evidence What it may reveal Important limitation
Alarm sequence Order of events preceding the fault Alarm order does not always establish causation
PLC event timestamps Interlock changes, state transitions or communication events Time synchronization and scan behavior can affect interpretation
Drive fault history Overcurrent, temperature, voltage or feedback-related events The reported trip may be a consequence rather than the initiating cause
Current trend Load increase, abnormal demand or intermittent electrical behavior Requires suitable sampling and a known operating baseline
Temperature trend Cooling degradation, overload or changing ambient conditions A single temperature reading may miss intermittent faults
Network counters Link errors, dropped packets or communication instability Device and network technologies expose different diagnostic information
Vibration measurements Mechanical degradation, looseness or imbalance patterns Diagnosis depends on sensor placement, operating state and analysis method
Physical inspection Wear, contamination, loose connections, leakage or damage Visual evidence alone may not identify the underlying cause
Product or test results Process drift, measurement error or equipment variation Measurement-system issues can mimic process failures
Repair history Repeated components, recurring locations or short repair intervals Poor historical records can distort conclusions

A useful practice is to preserve a baseline for important equipment. Without baseline measurements, it may be difficult to distinguish normal variation from emerging degradation.

9. Failure Frequency, Downtime and Risk Prioritization

Failure history becomes useful when records can be compared consistently.

9.1 Important reliability metrics

Failure count: Number of qualifying failure events during a defined period.

Downtime: Time during which the equipment is unavailable for its required function under the organization's agreed definition.

MTTR (Mean Time to Repair): Average repair duration for a defined set of repair events, using a consistent start and end definition.

\[ MTTR=\frac{\text{Total Repair Time}}{\text{Number of Repair Events}} \]

MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures): For a repairable item under an appropriate operating model, the average operating time between qualifying failures.

\[ MTBF=\frac{\text{Total Operating Time}}{\text{Number of Qualifying Failures}} \]

MTBF and MTTR should not be calculated from inconsistent event definitions. For example, mixing planned maintenance, minor alarms and major functional failures can make the results misleading.

9.2 Example

Suppose a machine experiences four qualifying failures in 200 operating hours. Total repair time is 10 hours.

  • Mean repair time is \(10/4=2.5\) hours per event.
  • A simple operating-time-per-failure estimate is \(200/4=50\) operating hours per failure.

The second figure is only a simplified estimate based on the stated period and event convention. Formal reliability reporting must define the operating-time window and failure-counting rules consistently.

9.3 Prioritization matrix

Priority Typical condition Response
Immediate Credible immediate safety hazard or compromised protective function Apply required emergency or safe-state controls and escalate
High Major production interruption, severe equipment risk or critical quality exposure Prioritize diagnosis and controlled restoration
Planned Stable, contained defect with no unacceptable immediate risk Schedule corrective work and manage the remaining risk
Monitor Degradation or anomaly without a current need for immediate intervention Define monitoring limits, owner and escalation criteria

This matrix is a practical operational aid, not a replacement for a formal risk assessment. Any credible safety hazard must be handled according to the applicable safety procedure, irrespective of production priority.

10. Distinguishing Recurring Failures from Independent Events

A recurring failure is not simply two alarms with the same text. Determine whether the events share a failure mechanism.

For example, repeated VFD overcurrent trips may result from:

  • The same mechanical jam occurring repeatedly.
  • A persistent overload condition.
  • A deteriorating motor or cable.
  • An unsuitable drive configuration.
  • Different faults that happen to trigger the same protection.

Compare the following:

  • Component and machine identity.
  • Alarm sequence and timestamp.
  • Operating state and process conditions.
  • Load, temperature, pressure or speed trends.
  • Previous corrective action.
  • Replacement part and configuration revision.
  • Time since the previous repair.
  • Whether the failure occurred under the same conditions.

A repair that works for a few hours and then fails again is evidence that the underlying cause may remain unresolved, the corrective action may be incomplete, or another failure mechanism may be present.

11. Connecting Failure Modes to RCA/CAPA

The critical failure-mode register should provide reusable technical knowledge, while the RCA/CAPA record should contain the investigation and actions for a particular incident or recurring problem.

The distinction matters because a single failure mode may have different causes on different occasions.

11.1 Example

Record element Example
Failure mode Robot loses communication with a remote I/O station
Observed effect Welding cycle stops and the line becomes unavailable
Suspected cause Intermittent communication cable defect
Investigation evidence Link errors correlate with movement of a damaged cable section
Confirmed cause Cable damage associated with repeated flexing at an unsuitable routing point
Immediate correction Replace the damaged cable and restore communication
Corrective action Implement approved cable routing and mechanical protection
Preventive action Inspect comparable moving cable routes and update the maintenance standard
Effectiveness check Review communication alarms and inspect the modified route after an appropriate operating period

The failure-mode register should link to the RCA/CAPA record and summarize the confirmed lesson. It should not copy the entire investigation into every related entry.

11.2 When to initiate a formal RCA

Formal investigation may be required when a failure:

  • Creates or could create a safety incident.
  • Causes substantial production loss.
  • Affects product quality or test validity.
  • Repeats despite previous repairs.
  • Damages costly equipment.
  • Reveals a design or maintenance-system weakness.
  • Exposes a control, documentation or compliance gap.

Follow site-specific reporting and escalation requirements. Do not wait for multiple occurrences when the first event has serious consequences.

12. Corrective Fixes Versus Long-Term Prevention

A good failure-mode record distinguishes restoration from recurrence prevention.

Action type Objective Example
Immediate containment Control the current risk or defect Stop the affected machine and secure the work area
Temporary restoration Restore limited operation under an approved temporary arrangement Install an approved temporary replacement component
Corrective repair Restore the failed function Replace a confirmed defective sensor
Root-cause corrective action Remove the identified cause Correct the cable routing that repeatedly damages the sensor cable
Preventive action Reduce similar risks elsewhere Inspect equivalent cable routes across comparable machines
Monitoring action Detect deterioration before functional failure Trend a relevant temperature or vibration indicator
Engineering improvement Reduce inherent vulnerability Redesign a mounting arrangement to reduce vibration or improve access
Management-system improvement Correct weaknesses in process or control Introduce controlled configuration review after component replacement

Temporary fixes must have explicit authorization, risk controls, ownership and an expiry or review condition where applicable. They must not become undocumented permanent modifications.

Never defeat a safety device, protective interlock or required protective function to keep a machine running.

13. Failure Modes and Preventive Maintenance

Failure history should influence the PM plan. Repeating the same inspection interval forever, despite evidence of repeated failures or changing operating conditions, is not a particularly advanced maintenance strategy.

13.1 Mapping failure modes to PM tasks

Known failure mode Potential PM or monitoring task Acceptance basis
Cooling fan degradation Inspect cooling path and verify fan operation Manufacturer requirements and approved condition criteria
Loose or damaged field wiring Inspect connectors, routing and mechanical protection Approved installation and inspection standards
Robot dress-pack damage Inspect high-flex areas and cable routing Manufacturer routing requirements and site criteria
Pneumatic leakage Inspect for leakage and verify required pressure performance Approved circuit requirements
Bearing degradation Trend suitable vibration and temperature indicators Established baseline and applicable limits
Analog scaling error Verify configuration against approved signal and engineering ranges Controlled configuration reference
Repeated drive trips Review trip history and relevant load or temperature trends Manufacturer diagnostics and approved operating limits
Sensor misalignment Verify mounting, target position and detection response Equipment-specific detection criteria

PM activities should be designed around credible failure mechanisms. “Check robot” is not an adequate maintenance task. A useful task specifies the component, inspection method, acceptance criteria and required response when the criteria are not met.

14. Replacement Parts and Known Weak Points

A known weak point is a component, interface, design feature or operating condition with an established history of failures or elevated vulnerability.

Examples include:

  • Cables repeatedly flexing at the same point.
  • Connectors exposed to contamination or vibration.
  • Sensors mounted where they receive repeated impacts.
  • Cooling fans operating in a contaminated environment.
  • Pneumatic tubing exposed to abrasion.
  • Hydraulic hoses exposed to movement or heat.
  • Unsupported or obsolete industrial PCs.
  • Aging storage devices containing important machine software.
  • Drives that repeatedly experience the same verified application-related stress.
  • Components whose failure creates an unusually long procurement delay.

A weak point should be documented with evidence, not reputation alone.

14.1 Link failure modes to spares planning

For each important failure mode, record:

  • The affected component and exact part identification.
  • Whether a replacement is stocked, repairable or vendor-supported.
  • Compatibility requirements and approved alternatives.
  • Expected supplier lead time.
  • Tools and skills required for replacement.
  • Configuration or calibration work required after replacement.
  • Required functional and safety validation.
  • Whether a replacement introduces firmware, revision or obsolescence risks.

For electronic and automation equipment, replacement is not always plug-and-play. A new PLC module, drive, HMI, IPC or robot component may require configuration, parameter restoration, firmware compatibility checks, network updates or validation.

15. Failure Modes in Backup and Configuration Recovery

Some machine failures are made much worse by inadequate configuration records.

Failure scenario Consequence Preventive control
PLC fails and the latest approved project is unavailable Extended recovery and risk of incorrect reconstruction Maintain verified, version-controlled project backups
VFD replaced with default parameters Incorrect motor or application behavior Maintain compatible parameter backups and post-replacement checks
HMI replaced without the correct application version Missing screens, alarms or communication Control application versions and dependencies
CNC controller restoration uses an incompatible backup Configuration problems or invalid machine behavior Verify hardware, firmware and backup compatibility
Robot controller replacement lacks required system data Long recovery or incorrect motion configuration Maintain the approved controller backup and restoration procedure
IPC storage fails without an image backup Loss of software environment and configuration Maintain verified system images and recovery instructions
Network device replacement introduces different configuration Communication loss or unexpected network behavior Maintain approved topology and device configuration records

A backup's existence is not proof that recovery will succeed. Recovery procedures should be checked and validated in accordance with the equipment's risk, criticality and applicable site requirements.

16. Measuring the Effectiveness of Fixes

A corrective action should have a defined way to determine whether it worked.

16.1 Useful effectiveness measures

  • Recurrence count for the same confirmed failure mode.
  • Time between comparable failures.
  • Downtime associated with the failure.
  • Repair duration.
  • Relevant alarm or diagnostic trends.
  • Product or test acceptance results.
  • Inspection findings after the corrective action.
  • Condition-monitoring measurements.
  • Evidence that similar equipment has been assessed where necessary.

Define the effectiveness period according to the failure mechanism and operating exposure. A machine that runs only a few cycles after repair has not necessarily demonstrated long-term reliability.

For intermittent failures, the absence of an alarm over a short period is weak evidence. Verification may require repeated cycles, representative loading, suitable monitoring or an inspection after a defined period.

16.2 Example effectiveness record

Field Example
Failure mode Intermittent robot communication loss
Corrective action Replace damaged cable and correct routing
Verification Confirm communication stability and run the approved functional test
Monitoring period Defined according to production exposure and failure recurrence pattern
Acceptance criterion No recurrence of the identified communication fault during the agreed verification period
Follow-up Review alarms and inspect routing at the planned follow-up
Final disposition Close only after the required evidence and approvals are complete

The acceptance criterion must be suitable for the specific failure. “Machine ran once” is rarely an adequate effectiveness test for a recurring industrial fault.

17. Failure-Mode Review and Governance

The register must remain accurate as machines change.

Review failure modes when:

  • A new machine is commissioned.
  • A significant breakdown occurs.
  • An RCA identifies a new or revised cause.
  • A component or design is modified.
  • A PLC, drive, CNC or robot is upgraded.
  • A recurring failure appears on similar equipment.
  • A spare becomes obsolete or a replacement is introduced.
  • A PM task fails to detect a known degradation mechanism.
  • A corrective action does not prevent recurrence.
  • Operating conditions change significantly.

Assign ownership for updating the register. A practical arrangement is for maintenance to maintain the technical failure history, engineering to review design and configuration changes, production to contribute operating context, and quality or safety functions to review issues within their authority.

Use the site's approved document-control and change-management process to manage revisions.

18. Practical Exercise: Build a Failure-Mode Register

Choose one machine in the plant and identify at least five credible failure modes across its important subsystems.

  • Record the machine ID, subsystem and exact component tag.
  • Describe each failure mode as a specific loss or degradation of function.
  • Record the symptoms and the operating conditions under which it occurs.
  • List possible causes without presenting unverified hypotheses as facts.
  • Identify diagnostic checks that distinguish competing causes.
  • Record the actual repair and verification results for known incidents.
  • Link confirmed root causes to the corresponding RCA/CAPA record.
  • Identify the production, quality, safety or equipment consequences.
  • Link each important failure mode to relevant spares.
  • Identify whether a PM or condition-monitoring task can detect or prevent it.
  • Define how the effectiveness of corrective actions will be checked.
  • Review whether the same failure mechanism exists on comparable machines.

For a welding robot cell, suitable starting areas include robot dress packs, welding torches, wire feeders, cooling systems, pneumatic clamps, remote I/O, industrial networks, PLC interlocks and weld-process monitoring. Record actual equipment-specific findings rather than assuming every listed issue exists on the machine.

19. Interview Questions and Technical Understanding

1. What is the difference between a failure mode and a root cause?

A failure mode describes how a function fails. The root cause is an underlying cause established through investigation and evidence.

2. Why is replacing a failed sensor not always a complete corrective action?

Because the sensor may have failed due to an underlying condition such as vibration, contamination, incorrect mounting, electrical interference or cable damage. Replacing it without correcting that condition may lead to recurrence.

3. What is FMEA used for?

FMEA systematically identifies potential failure modes, their effects and causes, existing controls, risk priorities and recommended actions.

4. How should intermittent faults be investigated?

Preserve event logs and timestamps, correlate symptoms with operating conditions, examine physical and communication evidence, test credible hypotheses and document the evidence supporting the final diagnosis.

5. How do failure history and PM relate?

Failure history identifies actual weaknesses and recurring mechanisms. PM can then be improved to detect degradation, prevent recurrence or verify that a corrective action remains effective.

6. Why must a failure-mode register distinguish confirmed and suspected causes?

It prevents unverified explanations from being treated as established facts and directs future troubleshooting toward evidence-based diagnosis.

7. What is the difference between MTBF and MTTR?

MTBF describes average operating time between qualifying failures under the adopted definition. MTTR describes average repair duration. They measure different aspects of reliability and maintainability.

8. When should a failure trigger formal RCA?

When required by site procedure, or when safety, quality, production loss, equipment damage, recurrence or systemic weaknesses justify a structured investigation.

9. Why is a successful restart insufficient proof of a repair?

A restart may temporarily clear an alarm without removing the underlying cause. Verification must show that the required function operates correctly under representative conditions.

10. What should be recorded after a critical failure?

The failure mode, symptoms, conditions, evidence, confirmed cause where known, corrective action, test results, downtime, configuration changes, related RCA/CAPA and effectiveness follow-up.

20. Final Acceptance Checklist

The critical failure-mode register is ready for practical use when:

  • Failure modes are described precisely and linked to machine components.
  • Symptoms, causes, effects and corrective actions are recorded separately.
  • Suspected causes are clearly distinguished from confirmed causes.
  • Diagnostic checks are practical and appropriate for the equipment.
  • Safety-related failure conditions have clear escalation and control requirements.
  • Corrective actions include verification and acceptance criteria.
  • Recurring faults are linked to breakdown history and RCA/CAPA.
  • Relevant PM tasks, monitoring methods and spares are linked.
  • Configuration and backup risks are documented where applicable.
  • Repair records contain enough evidence to support future troubleshooting.
  • Effectiveness checks consider appropriate operating exposure.
  • The register is reviewed after significant faults, upgrades and engineering changes.

Expected outcome: The machine knowledge base should help a technician move from a fault symptom to a structured diagnosis, understand the known weak points, restore the equipment safely, preserve useful evidence and prevent repeated failures through verified corrective action.
