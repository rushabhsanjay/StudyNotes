1. Purpose of Mechanical System Troubleshooting

An industrial robot depends on mechanical components to transmit motor torque, support joint loads, maintain positioning accuracy, and carry tools through the required motion path.

A robot may report no servo alarms and still have a mechanical fault. Gearbox wear, damaged bearings, loose couplings, cable interference, tool damage, or mounting problems can cause abnormal movement, position deviation, vibration, noise, and process defects.

This topic covers three major areas:

  • Gearbox and joint mechanics: torque transmission, backlash, wear, lubrication, and mechanical damage.
  • Cables and dress packs: power, encoder, communication, pneumatic, and process cables routed along robot axes.
  • Tools and end effectors: grippers, welding guns, dispensing heads, tool changers, and their mounting interfaces.

Feedback from encoders and other sensors allows the controller to regulate movement. Cable dress packs, pneumatic lines, tool wiring, and mechanical supports must also accommodate the robot's complete motion envelope.

A fault at any point in this system can affect robot movement or process quality.

3. Common Mechanical Fault Symptoms

Symptom Possible causes Initial checks
Abnormal noise from a joint Gearbox, bearing, coupling, or brake problem Identify axis, sound type, and operating condition
Excessive backlash Gear wear, loose coupling, mechanical damage Approved backlash or accuracy test
Vibration during movement Bearing damage, loose mounting, imbalance, or tuning issue Compare affected axes and motion conditions
Position deviation Backlash, tool movement, mastering, frame, or mechanical damage Verify references and mechanical condition
Motor overload Excessive load, binding, damaged gearbox, or cable drag Check load, current trends, and mechanical resistance
Cable damage Repeated flexing, twisting, abrasion, or crushing Inspect cable routing and bend areas

Gripper fails to hold a part Jaw wear, air leak, low pressure, or actuator fault Check gripping force and feedback

These symptoms are clues, not definitive diagnoses. Mechanical, electrical, control, and process faults can produce similar behavior.

4. Robot Gearbox and Joint System

4.1 Purpose of a gearbox

A robot gearbox reduces motor speed and increases available output torque. It also enables controlled movement of the robot's joints.

Many industrial robots use precision gear reducers, including harmonic-drive or strain-wave reducers and cycloidal reducers. Other designs use different gear arrangements.

The exact reducer type depends on the robot model and axis.

4.2 Common gearbox-related faults

Fault Typical effect Possible cause
Gear wear Increased backlash or reduced accuracy Long service life, overload, or poor lubrication
Bearing wear Noise, vibration, or rough motion Wear, contamination, or excessive loading
Lubricant degradation Increased friction and temperature Age, contamination, incorrect lubricant, or service issues
Lubricant leakage Reduced lubrication reliability Seal damage or incorrect service
Excessive backlash Position variation during direction reversal Gear wear or damaged transmission
Internal damage Noise, binding, or abnormal torque demand Collision, overload, or component failure
Mounting looseness Movement or repeatability problems Loose or damaged mounting hardware
Coupling damage Lost or inconsistent torque transmission Incorrect installation, wear, or impact

4.3 Backlash

Backlash is the amount of relative movement between mechanical transmission components when the direction of applied movement changes.

In a robot joint, excessive backlash may cause the actual output position to vary even when the motor feedback appears normal.

Typical symptoms include:

  • Position error when reversing direction.
  • Inconsistent approach to the same target from opposite directions.
  • Mechanical play at a joint.
  • Degraded path accuracy.
  • Noise or impact during direction changes.

Important: Some mechanical systems have specified permissible backlash or compliance. Do not diagnose gearbox failure from perceived movement alone. Use the manufacturer's inspection and measurement criteria.

4.4 Gearbox noise

Different sounds can provide diagnostic clues:

  • Grinding: possible gear or bearing damage.
  • Knocking: possible looseness, impact, or internal mechanical damage.
  • Whining: may be normal for some mechanisms, or may indicate lubrication, loading, or wear issues.
  • Intermittent noise at specific positions: possible localized damage, interference, or cable contact.

Compare the sound with historical operation and the manufacturer's expected behavior. Noise alone cannot establish a root cause.

4.5 Lubrication and leakage

Lubrication reduces friction, wear, and heat in the gearbox.

Maintenance considerations include:

  • Correct lubricant specification.
  • Correct quantity and filling procedure.
  • Required replacement or inspection interval.
  • Leakage around seals and joints.
  • Contamination from dust, coolant, water, or process materials.
  • Any temperature or noise changes after service.

Do not mix lubricants or use generic grease without verifying compatibility. Some reducers require specific lubricants and controlled filling quantities.

5. Bearings, Couplings and Mechanical Mounting

5.1 Bearing faults

Bearings support rotating components and help maintain alignment.

Common signs of bearing problems include:

  • Abnormal vibration.
  • Rough rotation.
  • Excessive noise.
  • Increased temperature.
  • Mechanical play.
  • Increased load or torque demand.

The bearing may be inside a gearbox or another assembly, so diagnosis may require specialist inspection.

5.2 Coupling faults

A coupling transfers torque between the motor and another mechanical component.

Potential failures include:

  • Loose fasteners.
  • Worn coupling elements.
  • Misalignment.
  • Damaged keys or interfaces.
  • Incorrect assembly.
  • Damage following a collision.

A damaged coupling can cause inconsistent motion or a difference between motor rotation and joint response.

5.3 Mounting and fasteners

Loose mounting bolts or damaged interfaces can allow components to move under load.

Check:

  • Motor mounting.
  • Gearbox-to-link interfaces.
  • Robot base and pedestal.
  • Tool flange and adapter plate.
  • End-effector mounting.
  • Tool-changer locking mechanism.

Use the specified torque, fastener grade, locking method, and inspection procedure. Randomly tightening fasteners can damage threads, distort components, or conceal a damaged interface.

6. Robot Cable and Dress-Pack System

6.1 Cable types

Robot cable systems may include:

  • Servo motor power cables.
  • Encoder or resolver feedback cables.
  • Brake cables.
  • Ethernet and industrial communication cables.
  • Digital and analog I/O cables.
  • Welding power and signal cables.
  • Pneumatic hoses.
  • Vacuum lines.
  • Hydraulic hoses.
  • Tool-changer and end-effector cables.

Some robots route cables internally through the arm. Others use external dress packs, cable carriers, or combinations of both.

6.2 Why robot cables fail

Robot cables undergo repeated movement. Depending on their routing and construction, they may experience bending, twisting, tensile loading, abrasion, and environmental exposure.

Common causes include:

  • Incorrect routing.
  • Bend radius below the specified limit.
  • Excessive torsion.
  • Repeated flexing beyond the cable's rating.
  • Abrasion against robot links or fixtures.
  • Crushing by clamps or covers.
  • Poor strain relief.
  • Connector damage.
  • Heat, oil, coolant, weld spatter, or chemicals.
  • Incompatible replacement cable.
  • Incorrect dress-pack length or support.

A cable can fail internally while its outer insulation looks acceptable.

6.3 Typical cable-fault symptoms

Symptom Possible cable issue
Intermittent encoder alarm during movement Feedback conductor or connector failure
Servo fault at a particular wrist position Cable flex or torsion near a moving axis
Intermittent tool I/O Broken conductor, loose connector, or shielding issue
Communication dropout during movement Damaged network cable or connector
Welding current or signal interruption Damaged welding or process cable
Air pressure drops during wrist rotation Pinched or damaged pneumatic hose
Tool loses power intermittently Tool power cable or connector fault
Repeated fuse or protective-device operation Cable short circuit or insulation damage

The relationship between the fault and robot position is particularly useful. If a fault consistently appears at a particular joint angle, inspect the cable sections and connectors that move or bend at that angle.

6.4 Cable inspection procedure

  1. Identify the affected axis and fault history.
  2. Determine whether the fault occurs at a repeatable joint position.
  3. Follow the site's isolation and electrical safety procedure.
  4. Inspect the accessible cable route, clamps, supports, and connectors.
  5. Look for cuts, flattening, abrasion, sharp bends, twisting, and heat damage.
  6. Check the approved bend radius and cable-routing requirements.
  7. Inspect strain relief and connector locking.
  8. Perform electrical tests only when the circuit and equipment are safely isolated and the test is permitted by the manufacturer.
  9. Replace damaged cables with the correct robot-rated type.
  10. Verify routing throughout the approved movement range before releasing the robot.

Do not pull, flex, or disconnect energized servo or encoder cables unless the specific manufacturer's diagnostic procedure explicitly requires a controlled live test performed by qualified personnel.

6.5 Shielding and grounding

Feedback and communication cables may require shielding and specific grounding arrangements to control electromagnetic interference.

Incorrect routing or termination can cause intermittent feedback and communication faults.

Check:

  • Cable type and shield construction.
  • Connector shield termination.
  • Separation from high-noise power conductors where required.
  • Grounding and bonding.
  • Manufacturer-defined cable routing.
  • Connector condition and compatibility.

Do not assume every cable shield should be grounded identically at both ends. Follow the equipment manufacturer's design.

7. Tool and End-Effector Faults

The tool is the device attached to the robot flange that performs the task.

Examples include:

  • Pneumatic grippers.
  • Electric grippers.
  • Welding guns.
  • Spot-welding guns.
  • Dispensing nozzles.
  • Adhesive applicators.
  • Vacuum grippers.
  • Machine-loading grippers.
  • Inspection cameras and measurement devices.
  • Tool changers.

Tool faults can appear as robot faults because they affect payload, position, process completion, and motion resistance.

7.1 Tool mounting faults

Possible causes:

  • Loose flange bolts.
  • Incorrect adapter plate.
  • Damaged locating features.
  • Worn tool-changer interface.
  • Incomplete tool locking.
  • Misaligned tooling.
  • Tool collision or deformation.

Symptoms include TCP shift, abnormal vibration, poor repeatability, and unexpected process errors.

Inspect the mounting interface and confirm the locking mechanism's status before operating the robot.

7.2 Pneumatic gripper faults

Common causes include:

  • Low air pressure.
  • Air leakage.
  • Damaged cylinder seals.
  • Sticky or contaminated valves.
  • Pinched hoses.
  • Worn jaws.
  • Misaligned gripper fingers.
  • Faulty open/closed sensors.

Useful checks include:

  • Supply pressure at the specified test point.
  • Valve output and electrical command.
  • Actual jaw movement.
  • Feedback sensor state.
  • Hose condition.
  • Gripping performance with the approved test piece.

A gripper-closed sensor does not necessarily prove that a part is securely held. Some systems detect actuator position rather than actual workpiece retention.

7.3 Electric gripper faults

Potential causes include:

  • Motor or drive alarm.
  • Encoder or position-feedback fault.
  • Communication loss.
  • Incorrect force or position setting.
  • Mechanical binding.
  • Damaged jaws.
  • Overload.
  • Incorrect gripper program or configuration.

Check the gripper controller's own diagnostics instead of assuming the robot controller is the source of the fault.

7.4 Welding tool faults

For a robot-mounted welding gun, check:

  • Electrode wear and alignment.
  • Gun-arm deformation.
  • Pneumatic or servo-gun actuation.
  • Cooling-water supply and leakage.
  • Welding cable condition.
  • Tool mounting and TCP.
  • Process feedback and communication.
  • Payload and center-of-gravity data.

Welding quality defects can originate in the tool even when the robot motion is correct.

7.5 Vacuum gripper faults

Possible causes include:

  • Vacuum generator failure.
  • Leakage.
  • Damaged suction cups.
  • Blocked filters.
  • Incorrect vacuum threshold.
  • Part surface or porosity issues.
  • Incorrect vacuum confirmation logic.

Verify actual vacuum level and part retention according to the approved process requirements.

8. Payload, Center of Gravity and Inertia

The robot's payload configuration describes the mass and load characteristics carried by the robot.

Depending on the controller, payload settings may include:

  • Tool mass.
  • Center of gravity.
  • Inertia.
  • Payload number or configuration selection.

An incorrect payload configuration can contribute to poor motion performance, vibration, excessive mechanical loading, or protective faults.

8.1 Common payload-related mistakes

  • Tool changed but payload data was not updated.
  • Additional hoses, cables, or adapters were added.
  • A gripper carries a part heavier than expected.
  • The center of gravity is entered incorrectly.
  • The wrong payload configuration is selected for a process.
  • The load exceeds the manufacturer's limits for the applicable pose or axis.

8.2 Diagnostic checks

  1. Verify the actual tool and workpiece mass.
  2. Check the approved payload data.
  3. Confirm the center of gravity and inertia information.
  4. Review the manufacturer's payload limits.
  5. Check whether the fault appears only with a particular tool or part.
  6. Verify mechanical condition and motion parameters.

Do not compensate for an incorrect payload by arbitrarily increasing servo limits or reducing protective sensitivity.

9. Cable Interference and Mechanical Collision

Cable interference occurs when a cable, hose, dress pack, or connected tool component contacts the robot, fixture, workpiece, or nearby equipment during movement.

Potential effects include:

  • Unexpected external force.
  • Position deviation.
  • Cable damage.
  • Tool displacement.
  • Servo overload.
  • Collision detection or protective stop.
  • Damage to connectors and pneumatic lines.

Diagnostic approach

  • Identify the robot pose when the event occurs.
  • Inspect the cable path and available clearance.
  • Check for snagging, stretching, twisting, or contact.
  • Review the approved robot motion envelope.
  • Inspect the tool and nearby fixture for impact marks.
  • Confirm the cable routing follows the manufacturer's specifications.

A motion program that is safe with a new cable dress pack may become unsafe after a cable is replaced or rerouted.

10. Distinguishing Mechanical Faults from Control Faults

Observation Likely diagnostic direction
Servo current or torque rises while movement becomes rough Binding, overload, gearbox, bearing, or payload
Feedback alarm occurs only at certain joint positions Moving cable, connector, encoder, or feedback system
Joint motion is smooth but the tool is offset TCP, frame, mastering, tool mounting, or mechanical accuracy
Abnormal play when reversing direction Backlash, coupling, gearbox, or mounting
Robot fault appears only with a heavy part Payload configuration, load limits, or mechanical condition
Gripper command is present but the part is not held Pneumatic/electric gripper, jaws, pressure, vacuum, or sensing
Fault follows a replacement cable Cable specification, routing, connector, or installation
Vibration begins after tool replacement Payload, center of gravity, mounting, or tool configuration
Noise occurs even with no process load Joint mechanism, bearing, gearbox, or brake
Robot stops at a repeatable pose with cable tension visible Cable routing or dress-pack interference

These patterns help prioritize investigation but do not replace measurements or the manufacturer's diagnostic procedure.

11. Systematic Mechanical Breakdown Workflow

Step 1: Capture the symptom

Record:

  • Robot identification.
  • Affected axis.
  • Alarm history.
  • Joint position when the fault occurs.
  • Tool and workpiece configuration.
  • Whether the fault occurs under load.
  • Whether the fault started after maintenance or collision.
  • Noise, vibration, temperature, leakage, or visible damage.

Step 2: Make the system safe

  • Follow the site's lockout/tagout and robot safety procedures.
  • Consider stored energy in gravity-loaded axes, pneumatic systems, and spring-loaded tooling.
  • Secure heavy tools or suspended components before removing fasteners.
  • Do not enter the robot's hazardous space based only on a servo-off indication.
  • Follow the manufacturer's requirements for any inspection involving powered movement.

Step 3: Perform external inspection

Inspect:

  • Robot base and mounting.
  • Joint covers and seals.
  • Cable dress pack.
  • Connectors and strain relief.
  • Tool flange and fasteners.
  • Tool changer.
  • Pneumatic and cooling lines.
  • Fixtures and nearby equipment.
  • Signs of collision, abrasion, leakage, or overheating.

Step 4: Compare symptoms with operating conditions

Determine whether the fault occurs:

  • At one specific joint angle.
  • During direction reversal.
  • At high speed.
  • Only with a payload.
  • During tool actuation.
  • After a long operating period.
  • Immediately after maintenance.

The conditions under which a fault occurs often provide more diagnostic value than the alarm description alone.

Step 5: Use approved diagnostics

Depending on the suspected fault, review:

  • Servo current or torque trends.
  • Motor and drive alarms.
  • Encoder and communication diagnostics.
  • Position deviation.
  • Temperature readings.
  • Tool I/O and pneumatic feedback.
  • Payload settings.
  • Mechanical inspection measurements.

Use only available and validated diagnostic functions for the specific robot.

Step 6: Repair the confirmed cause

Examples include:

  • Replace a damaged robot-rated cable.
  • Correct cable routing or support.
  • Repair a leaking pneumatic hose.
  • Restore tool mounting integrity.
  • Service a gearbox using the specified procedure.
  • Replace a defective component.
  • Correct payload configuration.
  • Repair or replace a damaged end effector.

Step 7: Verify the repair

  • Confirm the original fault no longer occurs.
  • Check cable routing through the required movement range.
  • Verify tool operation and feedback.
  • Check for abnormal noise, vibration, temperature, or leakage.
  • Verify position accuracy where relevant.
  • Perform controlled motion and process checks.
  • Confirm all safety functions remain operational.
  • Document the repair and release the equipment according to site requirements.

12. Practical Breakdown Case: Wrist Cable Fault

Reported condition: An encoder or communication alarm appears whenever the robot wrist rotates beyond a particular angle.

Initial suspicion: A moving cable or connector is failing under flex or torsion.

Investigation:

  1. Record the exact fault and wrist position.
  2. Review alarm history for repeatability.
  3. Inspect the wrist cable route and dress pack.
  4. Check for excessive twisting, abrasion, pinching, or connector strain.
  5. Verify the cable part number and routing against the approved configuration.
  6. Perform permitted continuity or diagnostic testing under safe isolation conditions.

Possible root cause: A conductor inside the moving cable is intermittently open because of repeated flexing.

Corrective action: Replace the damaged cable with the specified robot-rated type and correct the routing or support problem.

Verification: Check the complete approved wrist movement range, confirm feedback stability, and verify that no cable is stretched or trapped.

13. Practical Breakdown Case: Gearbox Backlash

Reported condition: The robot reaches a target from one direction but shows a different position when approaching from the opposite direction.

Investigation:

  1. Confirm the symptom using an approved repeatable test.
  2. Verify mastering and calibration status.
  3. Check the tool, frame, and payload configuration.
  4. Inspect mounting hardware and coupling condition.
  5. Perform the manufacturer's specified backlash or accuracy measurement.
  6. Compare the results with permitted limits.

Possible root cause: Excessive play in a joint transmission or a loose mechanical connection.

Corrective action: Repair the confirmed mechanical defect. If the gearbox exceeds the manufacturer's allowable limits, follow the approved repair or replacement process.

Verification: Repeat the specified measurement and verify accuracy over representative poses.

14. Practical Breakdown Case: Gripper Fails to Hold the Part

Reported condition: The robot reaches the pickup point and issues the gripper-close command, but the part slips during transfer.

Investigation:

  1. Check actual gripper movement.
  2. Verify pneumatic pressure or electric gripper status.
  3. Inspect jaws, suction cups, and contact surfaces.
  4. Check the feedback sensor and its mapping.
  5. Verify the part's dimensions and weight.
  6. Review the gripping force or pressure setting.
  7. Confirm payload configuration and the approved transfer motion.

Possible root causes:

  • Worn or damaged jaws.
  • Insufficient pressure or gripping force.
  • Air leakage.
  • Incorrect gripper setup.
  • Incorrect workpiece dimensions.
  • Feedback that indicates actuator position but not secure part retention.

Corrective action: Repair the tool or process fault and confirm that the part is securely retained under the approved test procedure.

Verification: Check pickup, transfer, placement, and part-retention confirmation through the required operating cycle.

15. Practical Breakdown Case: Robot Overload After Tool Change

Reported condition: Servo overload or motion deviation occurs after a heavier tool is installed.

Investigation:

  1. Compare the new tool's mass with the previous tool.
  2. Check center-of-gravity and inertia data.
  3. Verify that the selected payload configuration matches the installed tool.
  4. Check for cable or hose drag.
  5. Inspect the affected joint for mechanical resistance.
  6. Confirm that the load remains within the robot's specified limits.

Possible root cause: Incorrect payload data or excessive mechanical loading caused by the tool change.

Corrective action: Correct the payload configuration or redesign the loading arrangement as required. Repair mechanical damage if identified.

Verification: Confirm the correct configuration, then test the approved motion path and monitor relevant diagnostics.

16. Preventive Maintenance Checklist

Gearbox and joints

  • Check for abnormal noise and vibration.
  • Inspect seals and visible lubricant leakage.
  • Follow the prescribed lubrication schedule.
  • Inspect accessible mounting points and fasteners.
  • Record recurring position deviation.
  • Investigate abnormal load or torque trends.
  • Follow specified gearbox backlash and accuracy checks.

Cables and dress packs

  • Inspect moving cable sections for abrasion and cracking.
  • Check strain relief and connector locking.
  • Verify bend radius and torsion requirements.
  • Check for pinching, snagging, and excessive tension.
  • Inspect pneumatic, hydraulic, and cooling lines.
  • Confirm replacement cable compatibility.
  • Verify cable routing after maintenance or tool changes.

Tools and end effectors

  • Check tool mounting and flange fasteners.
  • Verify tool-changer locking and feedback.
  • Inspect gripper jaws, fingers, and contact surfaces.
  • Check pneumatic pressure, leakage, or electric gripper alarms.
  • Inspect welding electrodes, cooling, and process cables where applicable.
  • Verify TCP after tool removal or replacement.
  • Confirm payload mass and center-of-gravity data.
  • Test part-retention feedback and process confirmation.

18. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is the function of a robot gearbox?

It reduces motor speed and increases output torque while transmitting controlled movement to the robot joint.

Q2. What is backlash?

Backlash is relative mechanical movement between transmission components when the direction of motion changes. Excessive backlash can reduce positioning accuracy.

Q3. Why can a robot cable cause an intermittent encoder fault?

Repeated flexing, torsion, abrasion, or connector strain can damage conductors or connections, causing intermittent feedback loss as the robot moves.

Q4. Why should cable faults be checked at the affected robot position?

A cable may become stretched, twisted, pinched, or electrically intermittent only at particular joint angles.

Q5. What causes a gripper to fail even when the close command is present?

Possible causes include low pressure, leakage, mechanical binding, damaged jaws, actuator faults, or incorrect feedback.

Q6. Why must payload data be updated after a tool change?

The tool's mass, center of gravity, and inertia affect robot motion and mechanical loading. Incorrect data can degrade motion performance or contribute to faults.

Q7. How do you distinguish gearbox backlash from incorrect mastering?

Verify the reference and calibration data, then use the manufacturer's specified mechanical accuracy or backlash test. A mastering problem concerns the reference relationship; backlash concerns mechanical play.

Q8. What should be checked after replacing a robot cable?

Confirm the cable specification, routing, bend radius, torsion, strain relief, connector installation, and stable operation throughout the approved movement range.

Q9. Why can a tool fault appear to be a robot fault?

The tool can cause excessive load, incorrect feedback, process failures, vibration, or unexpected motion resistance even when the robot controller is functioning correctly.

Q10. What is the most important step before replacing a gearbox?

Establish the root cause using approved diagnostics and mechanical measurements. Confirm that the problem is not caused by a cable, coupling, mounting, payload, or configuration issue.

19. Completion Checklist

  • Understand robot gearbox and joint functions.
  • Recognize backlash, bearing, coupling, and lubrication faults.
  • Identify cable flex, torsion, abrasion, and connector problems.
  • Understand robot dress-pack routing and shielding.
  • Diagnose pneumatic and electric end-effector faults.
  • Verify tool mounting, TCP, payload, and center of gravity.
  • Distinguish mechanical faults from program, mastering, and feedback faults.
  • Follow safe inspection and repair procedures.
  • Verify movement, tool operation, process quality, and safety after repair.
  • Record root cause, corrective action, and final test results.
