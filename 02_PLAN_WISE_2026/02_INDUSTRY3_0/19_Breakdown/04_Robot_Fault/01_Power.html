1. Definition and Scope

A robot power fault occurs when the industrial robot's electrical supply, controller power supply, internal DC supply, or associated power-distribution system is missing, unstable, incorrect, or unable to support the required load.

Industrial robot systems typically contain several distinct power domains:

  • Incoming mains supply: Feeds the robot controller and its internal power-conversion equipment.
  • Controller power supply: Provides the required low-voltage DC supplies for control electronics and associated circuits.
  • Servo power system: Supplies the robot's servo amplifiers and motors through the controller's power-conversion architecture.
  • Peripheral power: Supplies devices such as sensors, grippers, solenoid valves, communication devices, and external equipment.
  • Protective earth and bonding: Provides the intended protective path for fault current and supports electrical safety and electromagnetic compatibility.

A robot may have a functioning controller display but still be unable to move because the servo power system is unavailable. Conversely, a complete controller shutdown may indicate an incoming supply, internal power-supply, protection, or distribution fault.

Core diagnostic principle: Identify which power domain has failed before replacing a component. A controller that is off, a controller that boots but cannot enable servos, and a robot that trips when motion starts are three different fault conditions.

2. Basic Robot Power Architecture

3. Important Power-System Components

Component Function Typical fault consequence

Main isolator Disconnects incoming electrical supply No power downstream if open or defective
Circuit breaker or fuse Protects against specified overcurrent conditions Loss of a supply branch
Main contactor Connects or disconnects power when commanded Controller or servo power unavailable
Phase monitor Detects specified supply abnormalities Power-enable inhibited or supply alarm
Transformer Changes AC voltage where required Incorrect or missing secondary voltage
AC/DC power supply Converts AC to regulated DC CPU, I/O, or peripheral supply fault
DC distribution module Distributes DC to downstream circuits One or more modules lose power
Servo power converter Supplies the servo system through the controller's power architecture Servo system unavailable or trips
DC-link capacitor Stores energy in applicable drive architectures DC-bus faults or abnormal charging behavior
Protective earth conductor Provides protective bonding Electric-shock risk and potential EMC problems

A backup battery is not the same as the controller's main power supply. Battery alarms should be investigated separately unless the manufacturer's diagnostics indicate a related power issue.

4. Understanding Incoming Mains Supply

The controller's incoming supply must match the manufacturer's specified voltage, frequency, phase arrangement, and protective requirements.

Common industrial supplies include single-phase and three-phase AC. Many robot controllers use three-phase input power, but the exact configuration depends on the equipment.

4.1 Incoming supply checks

  • Verify the controller nameplate and electrical drawings.
  • Confirm the specified nominal voltage and frequency.
  • Inspect the upstream breaker, disconnect, and protective devices.
  • Review plant distribution alarms and recent electrical disturbances.
  • Check for phase loss, phase imbalance, or incorrect phase conditions where applicable.
  • Inspect terminals for overheating or loose connections using approved isolation procedures.
  • Verify the supply under the conditions specified by the manufacturer.
  • Check whether other equipment on the same supply experienced a disturbance.

Do not assume a nominal voltage from the robot's brand or appearance. Controller models within the same product family may have different supply requirements.

4.2 Common mains-supply abnormalities

Condition Meaning Possible effect
Undervoltage Supply falls below the permitted range Reset, contactor dropout, or power alarm
Overvoltage Supply exceeds the permitted range Protection trip or equipment damage
Phase loss One required phase is absent Power fault or failure to start
Phase imbalance Phase voltages differ beyond the permitted limit Abnormal heating or protection trip
Frequency abnormality Supply frequency is outside the supported range Power-system fault, depending on design
Transient disturbance Brief voltage disturbance or surge Reset, alarm, or component damage
Intermittent connection Supply is lost momentarily Unpredictable resets or contactor dropout
High-resistance connection Connection develops excessive voltage drop and heating Intermittent faults and terminal overheating

An ordinary multimeter reading taken during a healthy period may not capture a short-duration disturbance. Use an appropriately rated power-quality instrument if the evidence points to an intermittent mains problem.

5. Controller Power Supply

The controller power supply converts the incoming electrical supply into regulated voltages needed by control electronics and other circuits.

Depending on the controller design, different supplies may serve the CPU, I/O modules, communication interfaces, fans, sensors, or other electronics.

5.1 Common symptoms

  • Controller display remains blank.
  • Controller repeatedly restarts.
  • CPU fails to complete its startup sequence.
  • I/O modules are unavailable.
  • Communication interfaces disappear intermittently.
  • A power-supply alarm is reported.
  • Some modules work while others remain unpowered.
  • The controller operates until a peripheral or output load is activated.

5.2 Possible causes

  • Missing AC input to the supply.
  • Failed power-supply module.
  • Overloaded DC output.
  • Short circuit on a downstream branch.
  • Loose terminal or connector.
  • Incorrect output-voltage setting where adjustable.
  • Overtemperature or cooling problem.
  • Defective distribution module.
  • Damaged wiring.
  • Poor electrical connections.
  • Internal protection activated by a downstream fault.

5.3 Diagnostic procedure

  1. Record the controller's state and all visible alarms.
  2. Check the electrical drawings to identify the affected supply.
  3. Verify the input supply to the relevant power-supply module.
  4. Check its status indicators and diagnostic information.
  5. Measure the output only at approved test points and against the specified limits.
  6. Compare the output with the behavior of downstream modules.
  7. Investigate overload, short circuit, or excessive current if the supply enters protection.
  8. Isolate downstream branches using the approved procedure to locate the fault.
  9. Replace the supply only after confirming the fault and checking for the underlying cause.
  10. Verify stable startup and operation after repair.

Important: A low DC output does not automatically prove that the power supply itself is defective. A shorted downstream circuit can pull the output down or trigger current limiting.

6. Robot Controller Completely Dead

A completely dead controller has no expected display, status indication, or normal startup response. The first objective is to determine where power stops.

6.1 Troubleshooting sequence

Controller Has No Power
          ↓
Verify Incoming Supply
          ↓
Check Main Disconnect and Protection
          ↓
Check Main Switching Components
          ↓
Verify Power at the Controller Input
          ↓
Check Internal Power-Supply Input and Output
          ↓
Inspect Distribution and Downstream Loads
          ↓
Identify Confirmed Failed Component

6.2 Diagnostic checks

  • Is the upstream distribution board energized?
  • Is the correct supply available at the controller input?
  • Are any breakers or fuses open?
  • Is the main contactor receiving the required command and operating correctly?
  • Are control-circuit supplies present?
  • Does the power-supply module indicate an input or output fault?
  • Is there evidence of overheating, contamination, or electrical damage?
  • Did the failure follow electrical maintenance, a supply disturbance, or a cabinet modification?

A breaker that has tripped should not simply be reset repeatedly. Determine why it tripped and follow the approved fault-clearing procedure.

7. Controller Powers Up but Robot Cannot Move

This condition differs from a completely dead controller. The CPU and operator interface may be functioning while the servo power system remains unavailable.

Possible causes include:

  • Servo power not enabled.
  • Main servo contactor or switching circuit fault.
  • Servo power converter fault.
  • DC-link charging or monitoring fault.
  • Emergency-stop or safety-system condition.
  • Drive or motor fault.
  • Incorrect servo-enable sequence.
  • A required interlock not satisfied.
  • Internal power distribution fault.

7.1 Diagnostic approach

  1. Confirm that the controller has completed its startup sequence.
  2. Read active alarms and the controller's servo-ready status.
  3. Check the safety status and required interlocks.
  4. Confirm the permitted servo-enable sequence.
  5. Check the status of the main servo power system.
  6. Review any DC-link, power-converter, or amplifier diagnostic information provided by the controller.
  7. Determine whether the problem affects all axes or only one axis.
  8. Follow the relevant manufacturer troubleshooting procedure.

If every axis is unavailable simultaneously, a common supply, controller, safety, or servo-power issue is more likely than multiple independent motor failures. It is a diagnostic clue, not proof.

8. DC Bus and Servo Power Considerations

Many servo power systems rectify incoming AC to produce a DC link, then use power electronics to control motor current. Some controller architectures use different power-conversion arrangements.

A DC-bus fault may result from:

  • Incoming supply abnormality.
  • DC-link charging failure.
  • Precharge circuit fault.
  • Main switching component fault.
  • DC-link sensing fault.
  • Excessive load or abnormal energy regeneration.
  • Internal power-converter failure.
  • Short circuit or insulation failure in a power component.

The DC link can remain at a dangerous voltage after the incoming supply has been disconnected.

8.1 Safe diagnostic requirements

  • Follow the manufacturer's isolation and discharge procedure.
  • Observe the specified waiting period.
  • Verify the electrical state with appropriately rated equipment at designated test points.
  • Do not rely solely on a display, indicator lamp, or elapsed time.
  • Do not touch DC-link terminals or internal power components unless qualified and the required safe state has been verified.
  • Do not bypass precharge circuits or power-system interlocks to force startup.

The DC-bus diagnosis must follow the controller's specific architecture and service documentation.

9. Fuses, Circuit Breakers, and Main Contactors

9.1 Fuses and circuit breakers

Protective devices may operate because of:

  • Short circuits.
  • Overloads.
  • Failed power electronics.
  • Incorrect wiring.
  • Damaged cables.
  • Faulty downstream loads.
  • Improperly selected protection.
  • A genuine abnormal supply event.

A fuse should be replaced only with the specified type and rating. Never bypass a fuse or install a higher-rated device to stop nuisance operation without an approved engineering assessment.

9.2 Main contactor

The main contactor may be responsible for switching controller or servo power, depending on the design.

Possible faults include:

  • Coil supply missing.
  • Coil open circuit.
  • Worn or damaged contacts.
  • Mechanical sticking.
  • Auxiliary contact failure.
  • Control logic or interlock preventing energization.
  • Loose power terminals.

A contactor that does not energize may be responding correctly to a missing safety condition or control command. Confirm the intended operating sequence before condemning the contactor.

10. Phase Loss and Phase Imbalance

Three-phase controllers and power converters may monitor phase conditions directly or indirectly.

Phase loss or imbalance can be caused by:

  • Upstream supply problems.
  • A failed protective device.
  • Loose or overheated terminals.
  • Damaged cables.
  • A failed switching contact.
  • Incorrect connection after maintenance.

10.1 Diagnostic approach

  • Review the controller's exact supply alarm.
  • Verify the supply requirements from the nameplate and drawings.
  • Check all required phases using approved measurement procedures.
  • Inspect upstream protection and switching devices.
  • Investigate signs of heat damage or loose connections.
  • Compare measurements with manufacturer limits.
  • Check whether the fault appears only when the system is loaded.

A phase monitor alarm may indicate a supply condition, but some faults originate in the sensing circuit or its wiring. Confirm the actual condition before replacing the monitor.

11. Protective Earth and Bonding

Protective earth is essential for electrical safety. Correct bonding also helps control unwanted electrical noise and interference.

Potential problems include:

  • Missing protective-earth conductor.
  • Loose cabinet bonding.
  • Corroded bonding points.
  • Incorrectly modified wiring.
  • Damaged earth conductor.
  • Inappropriate shield termination.
  • Improper separation of protective bonding and functional signal connections.

Protective-earth faults must be treated as safety defects, not merely as communication or noise issues.

Never disconnect protective earth to test whether an alarm disappears. Verify protective bonding using the applicable electrical safety procedure and required instruments.

12. Cooling and Power-Related Thermal Faults

Power supplies, servo amplifiers, and internal power components generate heat. Cooling problems can trigger thermal alarms or cause shutdowns.

Possible causes include:

  • Failed fan.
  • Blocked air filter.
  • Restricted ventilation.
  • High cabinet temperature.
  • Excessive ambient temperature.
  • Fan supply failure.
  • Failed temperature sensor.
  • Excessive loading or internal component degradation.

Diagnostic procedure

  1. Record the exact thermal alarm.
  2. Check fan status and airflow using the approved inspection procedure.
  3. Inspect filters and ventilation paths.
  4. Review cabinet temperature and operating conditions.
  5. Check whether the fault occurs after a predictable operating period.
  6. Verify the cooling system's supply and diagnostic signals.
  7. Investigate load or power-component issues if cooling is normal.

Do not assume that every thermal alarm is caused by a fan. A failed sensor, excessive load, or internal power-component issue may produce similar symptoms.

13. Intermittent Controller Power Faults

Intermittent power faults are difficult because the controller may recover before the technician begins testing.

Typical symptoms include:

  • Random controller restart.
  • Servo power dropping during motion.
  • Brief communication loss across several modules.
  • Faults after contactor switching.
  • Failures associated with machine vibration.
  • Problems after cabinet or wiring maintenance.
  • Faults that appear only during high electrical loading.

13.1 Investigation method

  1. Record the exact time and machine condition when the fault occurs.
  2. Preserve alarm history before resetting the controller.
  3. Check whether other machines or devices experienced a supply disturbance.
  4. Review available supply-monitoring or power-quality records.
  5. Inspect terminals, connectors, and switching devices using approved isolation procedures.
  6. Check whether the fault affects the entire controller or one power branch.
  7. Review the controller's event logs for resets, undervoltage, and power-related alarms.
  8. Use suitable instruments to capture transient conditions if necessary.
  9. Correct the confirmed cause and verify operation under representative conditions.

Do not repeatedly power-cycle the controller before collecting diagnostic evidence. Resetting may remove useful alarm history and temporarily hide the fault.

14. Power Fault Isolation by Symptom

Observed condition First areas to investigate
Controller completely dead Incoming supply, main protection, internal supply, distribution
Controller display works but all axes are unavailable Servo power, safety conditions, controller interlocks, common power system
Controller restarts intermittently Incoming supply stability, DC supplies, connections, internal diagnostics
One module is unpowered Local DC branch, module connector, branch protection, module fault

15. Example 1: Robot Controller Completely Dead

Reported condition: The robot controller does not power up after a plant electrical interruption.

Initial observations:

  • The operator interface is blank.
  • Normal startup indicators are absent.
  • The robot is unavailable.

Investigation:

  1. Confirm the state of the upstream electrical distribution.
  2. Verify the controller's specified incoming supply.
  3. Inspect the main disconnect and protective devices.
  4. Check the controller input and internal power-supply status.
  5. Investigate whether a fuse, breaker, or power-supply protection operated.
  6. Inspect for visible electrical damage before restoring power.

Possible root cause: An internal control power supply failed following an electrical disturbance.

Corrective action: Replace the confirmed defective supply using the approved service procedure, after checking that no downstream short circuit or other condition caused the failure.

Verification:

  • Confirm normal controller startup.
  • Check that the expected modules and interfaces are available.
  • Review active alarms.
  • Confirm servo-ready status when the required safety and enable conditions are satisfied.
  • Complete the approved operational checks.

16. Example 2: Controller Starts but Servo Power Is Unavailable

Reported condition: The robot controller starts normally, but the robot cannot enable its axes.

Investigation:

  1. Read the active alarms and servo-ready status.
  2. Confirm that the required safety conditions are satisfied.
  3. Check the servo power-system status.
  4. Review power-converter and DC-link diagnostics.
  5. Check the applicable switching and control circuits.
  6. Determine whether the fault affects all axes or only a subset.

Possible root cause: A required servo power-enable condition is not satisfied because of a fault in the applicable switching or interlock circuit.

Corrective action: Identify and repair the confirmed fault without bypassing safety or power-system interlocks.

Verification: Confirm the intended power sequence, valid servo-ready status, and successful controlled motion under the manufacturer's approved procedure.

17. Example 3: Robot Restarts Intermittently

Reported condition: The controller randomly restarts during production, and several communication devices briefly disappear.

Investigation:

  1. Preserve event and alarm history.
  2. Determine whether the reset affects the entire controller.
  3. Check for supply dips or interruptions.
  4. Inspect the incoming supply, switching components, and control power supply.
  5. Investigate loose or overheated connections under safe isolation.
  6. Capture transient supply conditions if the fault cannot be reproduced during ordinary measurements.

Possible root cause: An intermittent supply connection causes a temporary voltage drop.

Corrective action: Repair the confirmed connection defect and verify terminal integrity according to the applicable procedure.

Verification: Monitor controller operation and supply behavior under representative production conditions and confirm that the reset does not recur.

18. Practical Troubleshooting Workflow

Use this sequence for a robot power breakdown:

  1. Make the system safe. Follow emergency response, isolation, and stored-energy procedures.
  2. Record the fault. Capture alarm codes, event history, controller state, and the operating condition.
  3. Classify the symptom. Determine whether the controller is dead, unstable, or powered but unable to enable servo operation.
  4. Identify the affected power domain. Separate incoming mains, controller DC supply, servo power, and auxiliary supplies.
  5. Check the relevant supply path. Use the electrical drawings to trace the path from source to affected equipment.
  6. Verify the electrical condition. Use appropriately rated instruments and manufacturer limits.
  7. Investigate protection and switching. Check why a fuse, breaker, contactor, or electronic protection function operated.
  8. Check downstream loads. Investigate shorts, overloads, and failed modules before replacing the upstream supply.
  9. Correct the root cause. Use approved parts and procedures.
  10. Restore and validate. Confirm normal startup, safety status, servo readiness, and controlled machine operation.

20. Preventive Maintenance Checklist

  • Verify the incoming supply against the controller specification.
  • Inspect accessible power connections for heat damage or deterioration.
  • Check protective devices and investigate any repeated operation.
  • Inspect controller fans, filters, and ventilation paths.
  • Review recurring power and undervoltage alarms.
  • Check power-supply status indicators and diagnostics.
  • Verify protective bonding according to the maintenance procedure.
  • Inspect cable routing and cabinet condition.
  • Maintain current electrical drawings and power-distribution records.
  • Record power-supply module and protection-device specifications.
  • Review plant power-quality events where relevant.
  • Preserve the controller's approved configuration and recovery documentation.
  • Verify normal startup and operation after maintenance.

21. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What are the main power domains in an industrial robot?

The main domains are incoming mains, controller electronics supply, servo power, auxiliary power, and protective earth. Their exact arrangement depends on the controller design.

Q2. Why can a robot controller start while the robot cannot move?

The controller electronics may be powered while servo power is unavailable. Safety conditions, power-converter faults, interlocks, or servo-enable conditions may prevent movement.

Q3. What should be checked when a controller is completely dead?

Start with the specified incoming supply, main disconnect and protection, controller input, internal power supply, and distribution circuits. Follow the electrical drawings rather than replacing parts at random.

Q4. Why should a tripped breaker not be reset repeatedly?

Repeated operation may indicate a short circuit, overload, failed component, or other unresolved condition. Repeated resets can worsen damage and create safety risks.

Q5. What is the difference between a controller power supply and a servo power supply?

A controller supply powers electronics such as CPUs and I/O. The servo power system supplies the power electronics responsible for motor operation. Some controller designs integrate these functions physically while keeping their roles distinct.

Q6. Why can a DC-bus fault remain dangerous after isolation?

DC-link capacitors can retain hazardous electrical energy after incoming power is disconnected. The specified discharge procedure and verification of the safe state are essential.

Q7. How can a technician investigate an intermittent power fault?

Preserve event logs, identify the affected power domain, inspect connections safely, check for supply disturbances, and use suitable monitoring equipment to capture intermittent conditions.

Q8. Why should protective earth never be disconnected for troubleshooting?

Protective earth is required to reduce electric-shock risk and provide the intended fault-current path. Removing it can make exposed conductive parts dangerous.

Q9. What does a controller reset accompanied by several module communication losses suggest?

It suggests a possible common event affecting controller power or the controller itself. The supply, internal DC rails, reset history, and distribution must be checked before concluding that multiple communication devices failed independently.

Q10. What confirms that a robot power repair is complete?

The root cause has been corrected, the expected supplies and controller states are restored, alarms are resolved, safety conditions are satisfied, and the approved functional and controlled-motion checks pass.

22. Completion Checklist

  • Understand the robot controller's power architecture.
  • Distinguish incoming mains from controller and servo power.
  • Identify common supply abnormalities and their symptoms.
  • Diagnose a completely dead controller systematically.
  • Diagnose a powered controller with unavailable servo power.
  • Understand power-supply, fuse, breaker, and contactor faults.
  • Recognize phase-loss, thermal, and intermittent supply problems.
  • Apply DC-link and electrical-isolation safety requirements.
  • Preserve alarms and evidence before resetting the controller.
  • Document measured values, root cause, and corrective action.
  • Verify normal startup and controlled operation after repair.
