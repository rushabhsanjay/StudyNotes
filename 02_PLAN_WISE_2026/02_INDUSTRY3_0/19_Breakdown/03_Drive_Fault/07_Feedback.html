1. Definition and Purpose

A feedback signal provides information about the actual state or movement of a motor or machine. A drive uses this information to monitor operation, improve control accuracy, detect faults, or regulate speed and position.

In an industrial drive system, feedback may come from an encoder, resolver, motor-mounted sensor, or another supported measurement device.

An encoder can provide information about shaft position, direction, and speed. Depending on the encoder type and drive configuration, it may provide incremental pulses or an absolute position value.

Feedback faults can cause a drive to trip, lose speed or position accuracy, oscillate, move unexpectedly, or refuse to enable motion.

Core diagnostic principle: Distinguish a genuine motor or mechanical problem from a feedback signal problem by comparing the commanded movement, measured feedback, drive diagnostics, wiring condition, and mechanical behavior.

2. Feedback System Architecture

A typical closed-loop motor-control system operates as follows:

PLC / Motion Controller
          ↓
   Speed or Position Command
          ↓
       Servo Drive
          ↓
     Motor and Load
          ↓
 Encoder / Resolver Feedback
          ↓
       Servo Drive
          ↓
  Feedback Processing and Control
          ↓
  Motor Current and Motion Correction

The drive compares the requested motion with the measured response and adjusts its output according to its control algorithm.

In a closed-loop system, feedback is essential for accurate speed or position regulation. In an open-loop VFD application, an encoder may not be installed or required. Some drives can use optional feedback for monitoring or specialized control.

Do not assume that every VFD requires an encoder. Confirm the installed control architecture first.

PPR and CPR are not universally interchangeable. Check whether the drive expects pulses, edges, quadrature counts, or another convention.

4. Types of Feedback Devices

4.1 Incremental encoder

An incremental encoder produces pulses as its shaft rotates. The drive counts these pulses to determine movement and can estimate speed from their frequency.

A common quadrature encoder provides A and B signals offset by approximately one-quarter of a cycle. Their sequence allows direction detection.

A Z or index signal may provide a reference position once per revolution.

Common applications include:

  • Servo motor speed and position control.
  • Conveyor tracking.
  • Electronic gearing.
  • Rotary positioning.
  • Machine synchronization.

A basic incremental encoder generally does not independently retain absolute shaft position through a power cycle. The system may require homing or another reference procedure.

4.2 Absolute encoder

An absolute encoder reports a coded shaft position rather than only incremental movement.

Depending on the device, it may be single-turn or multi-turn.

  • Single-turn: Identifies position within one revolution.
  • Multi-turn: Also tracks position across multiple revolutions, subject to the device's design and retention mechanism.

Absolute encoders may communicate through serial protocols or dedicated interfaces. A wiring, protocol, configuration, or battery-related issue, where applicable, may cause position information to become unavailable or invalid.

4.3 Resolver

A resolver is an electromagnetic position sensor that typically provides sine and cosine signals associated with rotor position. The drive or interface converts these signals into usable position information.

Resolvers are often used in demanding industrial environments because of their robust construction, but they still depend on correct excitation, wiring, interface configuration, and signal integrity.

4.4 Other feedback devices

Depending on the application, feedback may also come from:

  • Tachometers.
  • Hall-effect sensors.
  • Linear encoders.
  • External position transducers.
  • Motor temperature sensors.
  • Process sensors used in speed or process regulation.

These devices serve different purposes. A motor temperature sensor is not an encoder, and a process sensor does not necessarily measure shaft speed. Diagnose each according to its function.

5. Common Symptoms of Feedback Faults

Symptom Possible cause

Servo drive refuses to enable Feedback initialization failure or configuration mismatch
Motor jerks at startup Incorrect feedback wiring, invalid position information, or control mismatch
Incorrect rotation direction Feedback phase relationship or configuration issue
Speed reading is zero while the shaft rotates Missing pulses, incorrect interface, or feedback-monitoring issue
Actual speed is unstable Noise, loose connections, intermittent signal, or unsuitable configuration
Positioning error increases Incorrect scaling, missed counts, mechanical slip, or feedback failure

Position changes after power-up Reference or absolute-position initialization issue

These symptoms are not unique to feedback failures. Mechanical binding, incorrect tuning, overload, and power-stage faults can produce similar behavior.

6. Encoder Wiring and Connections

Encoder wiring depends on the encoder type, supply voltage, output circuit, connector pinout, and drive interface.

Common incremental encoder arrangements include:

  • Single-ended outputs.
  • Differential outputs.
  • A, B, and Z signal channels.
  • Complementary differential pairs such as A+/A− and B+/B−.
  • Separate power and common conductors.

Some encoders include additional channels, temperature sensors, serial communication, or other manufacturer-specific connections.

6.1 Common wiring faults

  • Loose connector.
  • Bent or damaged pins.
  • Broken conductor inside the cable.
  • Incorrect pinout.
  • Missing encoder supply.
  • Incorrect supply voltage.
  • Reversed or incorrectly assigned signal wires.
  • Poor shield termination.
  • Damaged cable insulation.
  • Oil, coolant, moisture, or contamination in the connector.
  • Cable routed alongside high-interference motor wiring without suitable segregation.
  • Incorrect connector assembly after motor maintenance.

6.2 Inspection procedure

  1. Identify the exact encoder and drive interface.
  2. Obtain the manufacturer's pinout and wiring diagram.
  3. Follow the approved isolation procedure before disconnecting connectors.
  4. Inspect connector locking, pins, cable routing, and visible damage.
  5. Verify the encoder supply using the specified test procedure.
  6. Check continuity only with the circuit safely isolated and electronics protected.
  7. Confirm the signal type and differential-pair assignments.
  8. Inspect shield and grounding arrangements against the manufacturer's requirements.
  9. Reconnect and secure the connector correctly.
  10. Verify feedback status using the drive's diagnostic monitor.

Do not assume that two encoder connectors with the same physical shape have the same pinout.

7. Encoder Power Supply Faults

An encoder may require a dedicated supply from the drive, an external supply, or a specified feedback interface.

An incorrect supply can cause a missing signal, intermittent operation, encoder damage, or a drive feedback alarm.

7.1 Diagnostic checks

  • Confirm the specified supply voltage.
  • Measure the supply at the approved test point.
  • Check voltage at the encoder end where the procedure permits.
  • Inspect supply and common conductors.
  • Check for voltage drop under operating conditions.
  • Verify whether the supply is shared with other devices.
  • Check the manufacturer's current requirements and protection provisions.

A supply reading at the drive does not prove that the correct voltage reaches the encoder. A damaged conductor or poor connector can cause voltage drop under load.

Never apply an assumed supply voltage to an encoder. Use the device specification.

8. Incremental Encoder A/B Signals

Incremental encoders commonly use two channels, A and B, with a phase difference that enables direction detection.

If A leads B, the system interprets one direction. If B leads A, it interprets the opposite direction. The exact convention depends on the encoder and drive configuration.

8.1 Common A/B faults

  • One channel missing.
  • A and B channels swapped.
  • Incorrect differential-pair wiring.
  • Signal distortion.
  • Excessive electrical noise.
  • Incorrect encoder resolution setting.
  • Incorrect feedback direction configuration.
  • Signal frequency exceeding the interface's supported limit.

8.2 Symptoms

  • Direction is reported incorrectly.
  • Speed reading is approximately half or double the expected value.
  • Position accumulates incorrectly.
  • Feedback drops out at higher speed.
  • Drive reports encoder signal or tracking errors.

8.3 Diagnostic method

  1. Confirm encoder model and resolution.
  2. Verify A/B channel assignments against the pinout.
  3. Check the drive's feedback monitor while the shaft moves under approved conditions.
  4. Compare measured direction with commanded and physical direction.
  5. Inspect signal quality using the manufacturer's recommended diagnostic method.
  6. Verify scaling and counting conventions.
  7. Investigate interference or cable limitations if the fault occurs only at higher speed.

Do not swap encoder channels experimentally on an active servo system. An incorrect feedback relationship can cause unstable or unexpected motion.

9. Encoder Resolution and Scaling

The drive must interpret encoder signals using the correct resolution and counting convention.

A mismatch can produce incorrect speed or position calculations even when the encoder and wiring are healthy.

For an incremental encoder with \(P\) pulses per revolution, the pulse frequency at shaft speed \(n\) RPM is:

\[ f\_{\text{pulse}}=\frac{P \times n}{60} \]

For an encoder producing 1,024 pulses per revolution at 1,500 RPM:

\[ f\_{\text{pulse}}= \frac{1024 \times 1500}{60} =25{,}600\text{ Hz} \]

This is 25.6 kHz before considering any multiplication convention used by the drive's counter.

If quadrature decoding counts four edges per pulse cycle, the count rate may be four times the pulse-cycle rate. Manufacturer definitions determine the correct interpretation.

9.1 Scaling-related symptoms

  • Displayed speed differs from a trusted measurement.
  • Positioning distance is incorrect.
  • Actual speed appears doubled or halved.
  • Position error increases consistently over a movement.
  • Electronic gearing or synchronization is inaccurate.

9.2 Diagnostic checks

  • Confirm the encoder's specified PPR or resolution.
  • Confirm whether the drive parameter expects PPR, CPR, or another format.
  • Verify any scaling or gear-ratio parameters.
  • Confirm the mechanical transmission ratio.
  • Compare the drive's speed monitor with an independent, appropriate measurement.
  • Check the encoder's maximum supported frequency and the interface's limits.

Do not change resolution settings until the encoder specification and the drive's counting convention are confirmed.

10. Absolute Encoder Faults

Absolute encoder systems depend on valid position data and a compatible communication interface.

Potential causes include:

  • Encoder supply failure.
  • Incorrect protocol or interface selection.
  • Incorrect encoder configuration.
  • Communication interruption.
  • Connector or cable fault.
  • Encoder incompatibility.
  • Invalid or uninitialized position.
  • Battery or multi-turn retention issue, where applicable.
  • Incorrect scaling or position offset.

10.1 Troubleshooting

  1. Record the exact alarm and diagnostic status.
  2. Confirm the encoder model and drive compatibility.
  3. Verify the encoder supply and connector.
  4. Check the supported communication or feedback interface.
  5. Read the encoder's diagnostic information.
  6. Check whether the drive reports position as valid or initialized.
  7. Follow the manufacturer's procedure for position recovery, initialization, or homing.
  8. Verify the machine's reference position before enabling normal automatic operation.

Never assume that clearing the alarm restores a valid absolute position. Confirm position integrity before allowing the machine to move.

11. Resolver Feedback Faults

A resolver typically uses an excitation signal and returns sine and cosine signals representing shaft position.

The resolver interface processes these signals to estimate angular position and, where supported, speed.

11.1 Possible faults

  • Missing excitation.
  • Incorrect resolver wiring.
  • Open or shorted conductors.
  • Damaged resolver cable.
  • Incorrect resolver configuration.
  • Loose connector.
  • Damaged resolver.
  • Fault in the drive's resolver interface.

11.2 Diagnostic approach

  • Verify the exact resolver and interface specification.
  • Inspect connectors and cable condition.
  • Check excitation and returned signals using the approved procedure.
  • Compare diagnostic readings with the manufacturer's expected ranges.
  • Check whether the fault changes with movement or cable position.
  • Separate cable, sensor, and interface faults where the design permits.

Resolver testing may require specialized equipment. Do not apply generic voltage or resistance limits without the correct device specification.

12. Feedback Cable Routing, Shielding, and Noise

Encoder and resolver signals can be affected by electromagnetic interference from VFD output cables, contactors, brakes, welding equipment, and other high-current switching devices.

A signal may appear healthy at standstill but become unreliable during acceleration or at higher speed.

12.1 Good installation practices

  • Use the cable type specified by the drive and encoder manufacturers.
  • Keep feedback wiring appropriately segregated from motor power and other high-interference wiring.
  • Follow the specified shield termination method.
  • Use suitable connectors and gland arrangements.
  • Maintain correct bonding and grounding.
  • Avoid unnecessary cable extensions and unsupported connector adapters.
  • Observe maximum cable length and signal-frequency limits.
  • Inspect routing changes after maintenance.
  • Keep feedback and communication wiring away from avoidable interference sources.

Shielding is not a substitute for correct routing, grounding, connector quality, or compatible signal interfaces.

12.2 Intermittent feedback faults

If the feedback fault occurs only when the motor moves:

  1. Check whether the cable bends or flexes during machine travel.
  2. Inspect the cable at moving joints, drag chains, and connector exits.
  3. Check for broken conductors or intermittent contacts using an approved method.
  4. Compare the fault timing with acceleration, speed, vibration, and nearby switching events.
  5. Inspect the shield and connector assembly.
  6. Verify signal integrity using appropriate diagnostic equipment.

Do not repeatedly flex a damaged cable on an energized system to reproduce a fault.

13. Feedback Faults Versus Mechanical Faults

A drive may report following error because the actual motion does not match the command. The cause may be feedback-related or mechanical.

Observation Possible feedback cause Possible mechanical cause
Command changes but position feedback does not Missing encoder signal or invalid position Coupling failure or blocked mechanism
Position error increases under load Lost counts or scaling error Excessive load, binding, or slipping transmission
Motor jerks or oscillates Incorrect feedback configuration or noisy signal Mechanical resonance or unsuitable tuning
Speed is consistently incorrect Wrong resolution or scaling Incorrect speed measurement or transmission ratio
Fault appears only at certain machine positions Cable damage during travel Local mechanical binding
Drive reports motion but load does not move Encoder measures motor shaft but transmission slips Coupling, belt, gearbox, or key failure

A motor-mounted encoder normally reports motor-shaft movement, not necessarily the movement of the final machine mechanism. A failed coupling or slipping belt can allow the motor encoder to report correct motion while the load moves incorrectly.

14. Feedback-Related Following Error

Following error is the difference between commanded position and measured position in a position-control system.

\[ e\_{\text{pos}}=x\_{\text{cmd}}-x\_{\text{actual}} \]

Where:

  • \(x\_{\text{cmd}}\) is the commanded position.
  • \(x\_{\text{actual}}\) is the measured position.
  • \(e\_{\text{pos}}\) is the position error.

The drive may trip if the error exceeds its configured threshold or persists beyond the permitted time.

Possible causes include:

  • Missing or incorrect feedback.
  • Incorrect encoder scaling.
  • Mechanical binding.
  • Excessive load.
  • Incorrect motion profile.
  • Unsuitable tuning.
  • Insufficient torque or acceleration capability.
  • Feedback direction mismatch.
  • Lost position reference.

14.1 Troubleshooting

  1. Record the commanded and actual position values.
  2. Check whether feedback changes when movement is commanded.
  3. Inspect the alarm timing and magnitude.
  4. Check encoder resolution and scaling.
  5. Verify direction and reference initialization.
  6. Investigate mechanical load and transmission.
  7. Check motion limits and control parameters.
  8. Follow the manufacturer's procedure for tuning or recovery.

Increasing the following-error threshold without identifying the cause can hide a dangerous motion-control problem.

15. Servo Motor Feedback and Motor Replacement

Servo motors may include encoders, resolvers, temperature sensors, and brake connections in the same assembly or connector system.

Replacing a motor can introduce feedback compatibility problems even when the motor's physical mounting and power rating appear suitable.

Verify:

  • Exact motor model.
  • Drive compatibility.
  • Feedback device type.
  • Connector and pinout.
  • Encoder resolution or protocol.
  • Motor and feedback configuration.
  • Brake and temperature-sensor wiring.
  • Mechanical coupling and alignment.
  • Required initialization or tuning procedure.
  • Position reference and homing requirements.

Never assume that two motors from the same manufacturer use identical feedback devices or connector assignments.

16. Safety Precautions for Feedback Troubleshooting

  • Follow machine-specific isolation and lockout/tagout procedures before disconnecting feedback wiring.
  • Follow the drive manufacturer's waiting period and verify the electrical state before working inside the equipment.
  • Secure the machine against gravity-driven or stored-energy movement.
  • Consider brakes, counterbalances, pneumatic or hydraulic energy, and mechanical loads.
  • Do not disconnect or reconnect encoder connectors while energized unless the manufacturer explicitly permits the procedure.
  • Do not apply an insulation tester to connected encoder electronics or feedback interfaces.
  • Use only the specified supply voltage and approved test equipment.
  • Keep personnel clear during powered diagnostic tests.
  • Do not defeat safety interlocks to force motion.
  • Verify the reference position and machine state before re-enabling automatic operation.
  • Use the approved recovery and validation procedure after feedback replacement or configuration changes.

Servo axes can move unexpectedly if feedback is invalid, reversed, or incorrectly configured. A feedback fault is not an invitation to test the axis by trial and error.

17. Practical Breakdown Example: Encoder Fault at Startup

Machine condition: A servo drive reports an encoder fault during startup and does not enable the axis.

Initial observations:

  • The control supply is available.
  • The drive reports a feedback-related alarm.
  • The motor does not execute the commanded movement.

Investigation:

  1. Record the exact drive alarm.
  2. Confirm the motor and encoder model.
  3. Inspect the feedback connector and cable.
  4. Verify the encoder supply according to the approved procedure.
  5. Check the feedback interface and configuration.
  6. Inspect diagnostic information for missing or invalid feedback.
  7. Verify compatibility if the motor or drive was recently replaced.

Possible root cause: The feedback connector was not fully seated after maintenance.

Corrective action: Isolate the system as required, correct the connector installation, and inspect the pins and cable for damage.

Verification: Confirm valid feedback status, correct position or speed monitoring, and successful operation using the approved commissioning procedure.

18. Practical Breakdown Example: Feedback Fault Only During Motion

Machine condition: A robot axis or servo mechanism operates at low speed but reports a feedback fault during faster movement.

Investigation:

  1. Record the speed and motion conditions associated with the alarm.
  2. Inspect cable routing, drag chains, and moving joints.
  3. Verify the encoder's supported signal frequency and the interface limits.
  4. Check the feedback monitor for dropouts.
  5. Inspect shield termination and routing near motor power cables.
  6. Use appropriate equipment to evaluate signal quality.
  7. Replace or repair damaged components only after identifying the likely fault location.

Possible root cause: A damaged feedback cable intermittently loses a signal conductor when flexed.

Corrective action: Replace the cable with the specified type and correct the routing or strain relief.

Verification: Test the full approved movement range and speed profile while monitoring feedback status and drive diagnostics.

19. Practical Breakdown Example: Incorrect Position After Motor Replacement

Machine condition: A servo axis moves, but its measured position or travel distance is incorrect after motor replacement.

Investigation:

  1. Verify the exact replacement motor and feedback-device model.
  2. Check encoder resolution and communication compatibility.
  3. Compare drive parameters with the approved configuration.
  4. Verify the mechanical transmission ratio and scaling.
  5. Check position reference and homing requirements.
  6. Inspect the coupling and mechanical alignment.
  7. Perform the manufacturer's approved initialization and commissioning procedure.

Possible root cause: The replacement motor uses a different encoder configuration from the original motor.

Corrective action: Install a compatible feedback device or apply the manufacturer-approved configuration for the replacement motor.

Verification: Confirm correct direction, position feedback, travel scaling, homing behavior, and operation within the approved limits.

22. Preventive Maintenance Checklist

  • Inspect feedback cables and connectors during scheduled maintenance.
  • Check cable routing and strain relief at moving axes.
  • Verify connector locking and pin condition.
  • Inspect for oil, coolant, moisture, or contamination.
  • Check shield termination against the approved design.
  • Review recurring feedback and following-error alarms.
  • Preserve approved feedback parameters and configuration backups.
  • Verify compatibility before motor or drive replacement.
  • Record encoder model and resolution in the asset documentation.
  • Follow the manufacturer's procedure for feedback initialization and homing.
  • Confirm feedback status and scaling after maintenance.
  • Validate machine motion and position before releasing the equipment.

23. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is the purpose of an encoder?

An encoder provides position or movement information. Depending on its type and interface, the drive can use this information to determine speed, direction, or absolute position.

Q2. What is the difference between incremental and absolute encoders?

An incremental encoder reports movement through pulses and generally requires a reference procedure to establish absolute position. An absolute encoder reports a coded position value within its supported measurement range.

Q3. What are A and B channels?

They are two incremental encoder signals with a phase relationship that allows the system to determine movement direction and count transitions.

Q4. What is a Z channel?

It is an index or reference signal available on some incremental encoders, often used to identify a repeatable reference point once per revolution.

Q5. Why can an encoder fault occur only at higher speed?

Possible causes include cable damage, electrical interference, excessive signal frequency, interface limitations, or signal-quality problems that become apparent during faster movement.

Q6. What is following error?

It is the difference between commanded and measured position. Excessive following error can result from feedback faults, mechanical binding, excessive load, unsuitable tuning, or other motion-control problems.

Q7. Why should encoder resolution be verified after motor replacement?

The drive must interpret the feedback using the correct resolution and counting convention. Incorrect configuration can cause speed and position scaling errors.

Q8. Why should an encoder cable not be insulation-tested while connected to the drive?

An insulation tester can apply a voltage that damages encoder electronics or the drive's feedback interface. Follow the manufacturer's isolation and testing instructions.

Q9. How can a technician distinguish a feedback fault from a mechanical fault?

Compare command and feedback behavior, inspect signal integrity and diagnostics, verify scaling, and evaluate the motor-to-load transmission. Neither an alarm nor a single monitor value proves the root cause.

Q10. What should be verified after repairing a feedback fault?

Confirm valid feedback, correct direction and scaling, valid position reference, normal drive diagnostics, and safe machine movement across the approved operating range.

  • Understand resolution, scaling, and counting conventions.

  • Distinguish feedback faults from mechanical transmission faults.
  • Understand following error and its possible causes.
  • Apply safe diagnostic procedures for servo feedback systems.
  • Record feedback configuration and diagnostic findings.
  • Verify safe and correct motion after repair.
