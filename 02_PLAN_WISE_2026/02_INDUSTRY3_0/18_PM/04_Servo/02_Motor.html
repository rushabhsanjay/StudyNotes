1. Definition

A servo motor is an electromechanical actuator designed for controlled motion, typically providing precise control of position, speed, and torque when operated with a compatible servo drive and feedback system.

Servo motors are widely used in CNC machines, robotics, indexing tables, packaging equipment, pick-and-place systems, automated assembly lines, and precision material-handling systems.

Unlike a conventional motor that may operate primarily at a commanded speed, a servo motor is commonly used in a closed-loop motion-control system. The controller commands the desired motion, the drive supplies electrical power, and feedback helps the system regulate actual movement.

The objective of servo motor maintenance is to preserve reliable torque production, smooth motion, accurate positioning, thermal performance, and mechanical integrity.

The exact arrangement depends on the system. Some drives close the position loop internally, while others receive position feedback through a controller or motion network.

A servo motor should not be considered independently of its drive, feedback device, cabling, brake, coupling, and mechanical load. A fault in any of these components can produce similar symptoms.

3. Main Types of Servo Motors

3.1 Permanent-magnet synchronous servo motor

Many modern industrial servo motors use permanent magnets in the rotor and three-phase windings in the stator.

The drive generates controlled currents to produce the required electromagnetic torque.

Characteristics include:

  • High torque density.
  • Fast dynamic response.
  • Precise speed and position control.
  • Good performance over a wide operating range when correctly configured.
  • Dependence on a compatible drive and feedback arrangement in typical closed-loop applications.

These motors are common in industrial CNC axes, robotics, and precision automation.

3.2 AC induction servo motor

An induction motor can be used in a servo application when the drive and feedback/control system support the required dynamic performance.

The rotor develops torque through electromagnetic induction rather than permanent magnets.

Characteristics include:

  • Robust motor construction.
  • No permanent magnets in the rotor.
  • Compatibility with suitable vector-control systems.
  • Application-dependent torque density and dynamic response.

Not every induction motor is a servo motor. The complete drive, feedback, motor, and application combination determines whether the system can meet the required motion performance.

3.3 Brushless DC motor

The term brushless DC motor is used for several motor designs and control conventions. Many industrial brushless servo motors use permanent magnets and electronically commutated windings.

They have no mechanical commutator brushes, reducing brush-related wear. However, bearings, feedback devices, connectors, insulation, and other components can still fail.

The terminology used by manufacturers is not always consistent. Confirm the actual motor construction and compatible drive rather than relying on the name alone.

6. Torque, Speed, and Mechanical Power

Rotational mechanical power is related to torque and angular speed:

\[ P=T\omega \]

Where:

  • \(P\) is mechanical power in watts.
  • \(T\) is torque in newton-metres.
  • \(\omega\) is angular speed in radians per second.

For torque in newton-metres and speed in revolutions per minute:

\[ P(\text{kW})=\frac{T(\text{N}\cdot\text{m})\times n(\text{rpm})}{9550} \]

For example, a motor delivering \(10\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\) at \(3000\text{ rpm}\) produces approximately:

\[ P=\frac{10\times3000}{9550}\approx3.14\text{ kW} \]

This is mechanical output power under the stated conditions, not the electrical input power. Actual power demand also depends on motor and drive losses.

Continuous torque and peak torque

  • Continuous torque: Torque the motor can deliver under specified thermal and operating conditions.
  • Peak torque: Higher torque available for a limited period within the manufacturer's limits.
  • Holding torque: Torque available to resist rotation at or near zero speed under the specified control and thermal conditions.
  • Rated speed: The speed associated with the manufacturer's specified rating.
  • Maximum speed: The specified upper operating limit, subject to load, voltage, mechanical, and control constraints.

Do not assume a servo motor can provide peak torque indefinitely. Excessive torque demand can overheat the motor or cause the drive to trip.

7. Torque Production and Motor Current

In many permanent-magnet servo systems, torque is closely related to the torque-producing component of motor current over the applicable operating range.

A simplified relationship is:

\[ T \approx K_t I \]

Where:

  • \(T\) is electromagnetic torque.
  • \(K_t\) is the applicable torque constant.
  • \(I\) is the relevant torque-producing current component.

The actual relationship depends on motor construction, drive control method, current definitions, and operating conditions.

This relationship is useful for maintenance because a rise in torque-producing current may indicate increased mechanical resistance or load. However, current alone does not prove a mechanical fault. Acceleration, commanded torque, control tuning, and operating mode must also be considered.

8. Servo Motor Feedback

A servo motor often uses an encoder or another feedback device to report rotor position or motion.

Feedback enables the drive or controller to compare commanded motion with actual motion and correct deviations.

Feedback technologies include:

  • Incremental encoder.
  • Absolute encoder.
  • Resolver.
  • Other manufacturer-supported feedback devices.

The feedback device may be integrated into the motor or installed separately.

Common feedback-related problems include:

  • Damaged feedback cable.
  • Loose connector.
  • Incorrect encoder configuration.
  • Feedback signal interference.
  • Incorrect motor/drive pairing.
  • Feedback device failure.
  • Incorrect resolution or scaling.

The detailed operation and diagnosis of feedback devices will be covered in the Encoder topic.

9. Servo Motor Brake

Some servo motors include an electromagnetic holding brake, particularly when used on vertical axes or applications where the load must remain stationary when the motor is disabled.

A holding brake is generally intended to hold a stationary load, not to serve as the normal dynamic stopping mechanism unless specifically designed and rated for that duty.

Brake operating sequence

A typical arrangement may follow this sequence:

  1. The drive and control system establish the required conditions for motion.
  2. The brake release supply is energized according to the manufacturer's sequence.
  3. The system confirms or allows the required brake-release interval.
  4. The motor produces the torque needed to control the load.
  5. When stopping, the drive controls deceleration.
  6. The brake engages according to the approved sequence before torque is removed where the design requires it.

The exact sequence depends on the motor, drive, brake, axis orientation, and machine safety design.

Common brake faults

  • Brake does not release.
  • Brake releases slowly.
  • Brake does not hold the load.
  • Brake coil or rectifier fault.
  • Incorrect brake supply voltage.
  • Incorrect release timing.
  • Worn brake components.
  • Incorrect control logic.

A brake that remains engaged can create high motor current, overheating, following errors, or a drive overload fault.

Never assume that a disabled servo motor is safely holding a vertical load. Follow the machine's approved load-support and isolation procedures before working on the mechanism.

10. Servo Motor Cooling and Thermal Health

Servo motors generate heat from winding losses, magnetic and mechanical losses, and other operating effects.

Cooling may be provided by natural convection, a fan, forced ventilation, or a liquid-cooling arrangement, depending on the motor design.

Factors affecting motor temperature include:

  • RMS current and duty cycle.
  • Continuous and peak torque demand.
  • Operating speed.
  • Ambient temperature.
  • Cooling-system condition.
  • Installation orientation and mounting.
  • Contamination and restricted airflow.
  • Repeated acceleration and deceleration.
  • Brake drag or mechanical friction.
  • Motor suitability for low-speed continuous operation.

At low speed, a motor that relies on shaft-mounted fan cooling may have reduced cooling capability. The manufacturer's continuous torque and thermal limits must therefore be checked for the actual operating speed and duty cycle.

Thermal overload versus drive overcurrent

These are different conditions.

  • An overcurrent fault usually indicates that current exceeded a monitored limit or another drive-specific criterion.
  • A thermal overload condition indicates excessive estimated or measured thermal loading of the motor or drive.

A motor can overheat over time without a single extreme current spike. Conversely, a drive may trip on instantaneous overcurrent before the motor becomes thermally hot.

11. Mechanical Installation and Alignment

Correct installation is essential to servo motor reliability and positioning accuracy.

Inspect:

  • Motor mounting bolts and mounting surface.
  • Coupling alignment.
  • Shaft fit and coupling condition.
  • Gearbox, ballscrew, belt, and pulley condition.
  • Bearing condition.
  • Mechanical backlash.
  • Load balance and friction.
  • Cable strain relief and connector support.
  • Brake operation and mounting where fitted.

Misalignment can cause bearing wear, vibration, coupling damage, and increased torque demand.

A servo axis may continue to operate despite poor alignment, but the drive may show increased current, torque ripple, following error, or positioning variation.

Never compensate for a mechanical defect by changing servo tuning before the mechanical condition has been assessed.

12. Servo Motor Cables and Connections

Servo systems commonly have separate motor power and feedback connections. Some motor designs also have dedicated brake and temperature-sensor connections.

Verify the exact connector and pinout from the motor and drive documentation.

Important checks include:

  • Correct motor power cable and connector.
  • Correct feedback cable and connector.
  • Brake supply and wiring, where fitted.
  • Temperature sensor wiring, where fitted.
  • Protective earth and required shielding.
  • Connector locking and strain relief.
  • Cable routing and separation from noise-sensitive signals.
  • Cable compatibility with the required flexing and movement.
  • Signs of oil ingress, moisture, abrasion, or crushed insulation.

A cable that repeatedly flexes with an axis may fail internally even when the external jacket looks normal. Inspect moving cable sections and cable carriers where the application uses them.

Do not interchange connectors merely because they physically fit. Similar-looking connectors can have different pin assignments and electrical functions.

A symptom can have multiple causes. Diagnose using fault history, measured values, wiring information, and mechanical inspection rather than replacing the motor based on one alarm.

14. Motor Insulation and Electrical Testing

Electrical testing must be suitable for the motor type and must protect connected electronics.

Winding resistance

Winding resistance measurements can help identify open circuits, abnormal phase-to-phase differences, or certain winding defects.

Interpretation requires consideration of:

  • Motor construction and winding configuration.
  • Measurement lead resistance.
  • Instrument accuracy.
  • Winding temperature.
  • Manufacturer tolerances and test procedure.

Some servo motors have low winding resistance, so ordinary handheld multimeters may not provide sufficient accuracy to detect small differences. Use an appropriate instrument and method when necessary.

Insulation resistance

Insulation resistance testing can help assess winding insulation, but the motor must be disconnected from the servo drive and other sensitive electronic devices according to the manufacturer's procedure.

Before testing:

  1. Isolate and lock out the equipment.
  2. Verify absence of voltage and address stored energy.
  3. Disconnect the motor from the drive as required.
  4. Isolate sensors or other components that could be damaged by the test voltage.
  5. Use the specified test voltage and acceptance criteria.
  6. Discharge the tested circuit according to the approved procedure.
  7. Restore the correct wiring and protective bonding.

Never apply a megohmmeter test voltage through a connected servo drive or feedback electronics.

Do not apply generic insulation-resistance thresholds to every servo motor. Use the manufacturer's requirements and the applicable maintenance procedure.

15. Servo Motor Maintenance Procedure

Routine inspection

  1. Confirm the motor asset identification and operating conditions.
  2. Review drive alarms and motor-related diagnostic trends.
  3. Inspect the motor housing, mounting, connectors, and accessible cables.
  4. Check for abnormal temperature, noise, vibration, or smell.
  5. Inspect the cooling arrangement.
  6. Check mechanical alignment, coupling, and transmission where accessible.
  7. Verify brake-related symptoms on motors equipped with brakes.
  8. Record findings and any changes from the normal baseline.

Detailed inspection

Detailed electrical or mechanical work may require motor removal, specialist testing, or manufacturer support.

  • Isolate the equipment and apply the approved energy-control procedure.
  • Support the load and account for gravity, springs, stored energy, and other hazards.
  • Disconnect cables using the manufacturer's procedure.
  • Inspect bearings, shaft, coupling, and mounting condition.
  • Test winding and insulation condition where appropriate.
  • Check encoder and brake condition using approved procedures.
  • Verify replacement compatibility before installing another motor.
  • Reconnect and commission the system according to the approved procedure.

Do not open a sealed motor or remove an integrated feedback device without considering the manufacturer's repair requirements. Some motors require specialized tools, cleanliness controls, alignment procedures, or calibration.

16. Practical Example: Servo Motor Overload on a CNC Axis

Situation

A CNC axis begins reporting a high torque demand and intermittently trips during rapid movement. The motor previously operated normally.

Investigation

  1. Record the drive fault code and operating conditions at the time of the trip.
  2. Compare torque demand during the same axis movement with historical values.
  3. Check whether the problem occurs at a specific position or throughout the travel.
  4. Inspect the ballscrew, guideways, coupling, bearings, and lubrication condition according to the machine procedure.
  5. Check whether a brake is fitted and whether it releases correctly.
  6. Review encoder feedback and following-error diagnostics.
  7. Compare the motion profile and drive configuration with the approved baseline.
  8. Check motor temperature and the frequency of overload events.

Possible findings

  • Increased friction at a particular travel position.
  • Misalignment introduced during mechanical maintenance.
  • Brake drag.
  • A damaged bearing or coupling.
  • An incorrect motion parameter or increased process load.

Corrective action

Correct the verified cause, restore approved settings if required, and conduct controlled tests over the full required axis travel.

Record the final torque demand, positioning performance, fault status, and maintenance findings. If the drive and motor are healthy, avoid replacing them merely because the alarm appeared in the drive.

17. Servo Motor Replacement

Before selecting a replacement, verify more than rated power.

Check:

  • Manufacturer and exact model or approved successor.
  • Continuous and peak torque requirements.
  • Rated and maximum speed.
  • Voltage and current compatibility.
  • Mechanical mounting and shaft dimensions.
  • Encoder or feedback type and compatibility.
  • Connector type and pinout.
  • Brake rating and control requirements.
  • Temperature sensor type.
  • Motor cable and feedback cable compatibility.
  • Environmental protection and cooling arrangement.
  • Compatibility with the drive and motion controller.

A motor with similar power may have different inertia, feedback resolution, torque characteristics, dimensions, or brake behavior. These differences can affect control stability, positioning, stopping, and machine safety.

After replacement, follow the approved configuration and commissioning procedure. Verify the motor identity and data, feedback configuration, direction, brake operation, motion limits, positioning performance, and applicable safety functions.

18. Preventive Maintenance Checklist

Electrical and thermal

  • Record motor model, serial number, and nameplate data.
  • Check drive alarms and motor-related diagnostic history.
  • Review current or torque trends.
  • Inspect motor cables and connectors.
  • Check protective earth and required bonding.
  • Inspect the cooling arrangement.
  • Investigate abnormal temperature or insulation-related faults.

Mechanical

  • Inspect mounting and coupling condition.
  • Check alignment according to the approved method.
  • Investigate abnormal noise and vibration.
  • Inspect the gearbox, ballscrew, belt, or other transmission.
  • Check for unusual friction or load changes.
  • Verify brake symptoms and operation where fitted.

Feedback and control

  • Check feedback diagnostics and connectors.
  • Review positioning accuracy and following error.
  • Compare torque demand with the established baseline.
  • Check for recurring motion-related faults.
  • Verify that recent parameter changes were authorized and validated.

Documentation

  • Record inspection results.
  • Update the maintenance history.
  • Record motor or cable replacement details.
  • Preserve the approved drive configuration and motor identification.
  • Document corrective actions and verification results.

For predictive maintenance, compare similar machine cycles and loads. A torque increase is meaningful only when the commanded motion, load, speed, and operating conditions are understood.

20. Key Engineering Principles

  • A servo motor must be evaluated together with its drive, feedback device, cables, brake, and mechanical load.
  • Correct motor identification and drive compatibility are essential during replacement.
  • Torque demand and motor temperature are useful health indicators when interpreted against operating conditions.
  • Mechanical friction and misalignment can appear as electrical or servo-control faults.
  • A holding brake is not automatically a dynamic stopping brake or a maintenance isolation device.
  • Feedback and power cables must be compatible, correctly connected, and installed according to the manufacturer's requirements.
  • Electrical testing must protect the drive and integrated electronics.
  • Do not use parameter changes to hide mechanical defects.
  • Confirm motor direction, brake behavior, positioning, and protection after relevant maintenance.
  • Maintain accurate motor identification, diagnostic history, configuration records, and spare-part information.
