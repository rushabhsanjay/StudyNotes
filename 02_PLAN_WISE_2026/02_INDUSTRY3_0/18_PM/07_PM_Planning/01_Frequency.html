1. Definition and purpose

Preventive maintenance (PM) frequency defines how often a maintenance task must be performed on equipment to maintain reliability, safety, performance, and availability.

Typical frequencies include daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, half-yearly, and yearly tasks. The correct interval depends on the equipment design, operating hours, duty cycle, environmental conditions, failure history, manufacturer recommendations, safety requirements, and production criticality.

PM frequency is not simply a calendar chosen by habit. It is a planned interval intended to detect deterioration, prevent avoidable failures, maintain required performance, and reduce unplanned downtime.

For industrial automation equipment, PM frequency may apply to PLCs, robots, CNC machines, servo drives, VFDs, HMIs, sensors, electrical panels, cooling systems, networks, safety devices, and backup systems.

2. Why PM frequency matters

A well-designed PM schedule helps an organization:

  • Detect developing faults before equipment fails.
  • Maintain machine safety and process quality.
  • Reduce repeat breakdowns and emergency maintenance.
  • Allocate manpower, tools, spares, and shutdown windows.
  • Maintain reliable maintenance records.
  • Control maintenance cost and unnecessary intervention.
  • Improve equipment availability and maintainability.
  • Meet applicable legal, regulatory, manufacturer, and company requirements.
  • Establish consistent inspection standards across shifts and machines.

Two opposite errors must be avoided:

  • Under-maintenance: inspection or servicing is too infrequent to detect relevant deterioration.
  • Over-maintenance: tasks are performed too frequently without a meaningful reliability benefit, increasing cost and potentially introducing maintenance-induced faults.

The goal is the appropriate interval for each task, not the maximum number of maintenance activities.

4. Daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly maintenance

4.1 Daily or per-shift tasks

Daily tasks should focus on visible abnormalities, operating condition, and early indications of failure. Many checks can be performed by trained operators, with maintenance involvement where required.

Typical activities:

  • Check machine and robot controller status.
  • Review active alarms and abnormal warnings.
  • Observe unusual noise, vibration, odor, or heat.
  • Check for visible leaks, damaged cables, loose covers, or obstructions.
  • Check accessible sensors and fixtures for contamination or damage.
  • Verify that cooling fans and ventilation openings are not visibly obstructed.
  • Check relevant pressure, temperature, or utility indications.
  • Confirm that guards and safety devices appear intact without bypassing or defeating them.
  • Record abnormalities and report faults through the approved process.
  • Review unresolved issues from the previous shift.

Daily visual checks do not replace formal safety inspections, electrical testing, or manufacturer-prescribed maintenance.

4.2 Weekly tasks

Weekly tasks involve more deliberate inspection of selected components and trends.

Typical activities:

  • Inspect accessible cable routes, connectors, and cable carriers.
  • Review recurring alarms and breakdown patterns.
  • Inspect panel filters, ventilation condition, and visible contamination.
  • Check sensor mounting and accessible actuator condition.
  • Review robot and CNC abnormality records.
  • Verify that routine backup jobs or manual backup requirements have been completed.
  • Inspect pneumatic and hydraulic systems for visible deterioration.
  • Review open maintenance notifications and overdue tasks.
  • Check that housekeeping and access around equipment remain satisfactory.

Any electrical enclosure opening, measurement, or intervention must follow authorization, isolation, and electrical-safety requirements.

4.3 Monthly tasks

Monthly tasks generally provide a more structured review of condition, reliability, records, and configuration.

Typical activities:

  • Review alarm frequency and downtime by machine.
  • Inspect accessible electrical and control-panel condition using approved procedures.
  • Check cooling-system performance and maintenance indicators.
  • Review robot program and controller backup status.
  • Inspect selected connectors, cables, and network equipment according to the approved method.
  • Review PM completion and overdue work.
  • Analyze repeat failures and verify corrective actions.
  • Review critical spare availability and obsolescence risks.
  • Check calibration status of equipment requiring calibration.
  • Review machine-specific risks and update work instructions when approved.

Monthly does not automatically mean opening every panel or retightening every terminal. Tasks must be selected according to design, risk, manufacturer instructions, and site procedures.

4.4 Quarterly and half-yearly tasks

These intervals are useful for tasks that require more time, specialist tools, or a planned maintenance window.

Examples:

  • Detailed inspection of robot dress packs and cable carriers.
  • Review of axis, drive, motor, and mechanical condition indicators.
  • Condition assessment of cooling fans and filters.
  • Inspection of selected pneumatic and hydraulic components.
  • Review of PLC, CNC, HMI, and robot configuration changes.
  • Controlled verification of selected functions and interlocks.
  • Review of backup integrity and recovery readiness.
  • Assessment of repeat-failure trends and maintenance effectiveness.

Some tasks may be condition based or operating-hour based instead of calendar based. Use the manufacturer's specified method where one exists.

4.5 Yearly tasks

Yearly maintenance typically covers deeper inspections, specified servicing, and a formal review of the maintenance strategy.

Examples:

  • Manufacturer-prescribed major inspection or service.
  • Calibration and measurement-system verification where required.
  • Condition assessment of motors, drives, and mechanical assemblies.
  • Inspection or replacement of components according to specified service life.
  • Review of safety-system validation requirements and records.
  • Review of controller, firmware, software, and configuration support status.
  • Restore-readiness review for critical automation backups.
  • Review of critical spares, obsolete components, and migration requirements.
  • Analysis of annual downtime, MTBF, MTTR, and recurring failures.
  • Review of PM task effectiveness, frequency, and completion performance.

Yearly PM does not mean replacing every component annually. Replace parts according to manufacturer requirements, condition, reliability evidence, safety requirements, and approved engineering decisions.

5. Selecting the correct PM frequency

The interval should be selected separately for each maintenance task. A robot may need a daily alarm review, a periodic cable inspection, a manufacturer-defined battery replacement interval, and a condition-based mechanical inspection. Applying one frequency to the whole robot would be convenient, but not especially intelligent.

5.1 Factors affecting frequency

Factor Effect on PM planning
Manufacturer recommendations Establish specified inspection and service requirements
Equipment criticality Higher production or safety impact may justify more frequent monitoring
Failure history Repeated failures may justify an interval review or additional diagnostics
Operating hours High utilization may accelerate wear
Cycle count Repeated movement may increase wear in cycle-dependent components
Environment Dust, heat, moisture, vibration, and contamination can accelerate deterioration
Component technology Batteries, filters, fans, belts, and other parts have different degradation mechanisms
Failure detectability Hidden failures may require periodic functional testing or condition monitoring
Failure consequence Safety-critical or high-consequence failures require risk-based controls
Access requirements Tasks requiring shutdown or special access may need coordinated outage planning
Cost and manpower Resources must be available without compromising necessary maintenance
Historical effectiveness Actual findings should support retaining or changing an interval

5.2 Practical frequency-selection method

  1. Identify the equipment and component.
  2. Define the failure mode or condition the task is intended to detect or prevent.
  3. Review manufacturer instructions, safety requirements, and applicable standards.
  4. Assess failure probability, consequence, detectability, and operating conditions.
  5. Select a suitable inspection method and initial interval.
  6. Define acceptance criteria and required technician competency.
  7. Record the interval, task owner, duration, and required tools or spares.
  8. Review findings and failures after a suitable monitoring period.
  9. Change the interval only through approved maintenance or engineering change control.

A risk assessment may justify more frequent checks or additional condition monitoring. It does not justify extending a manufacturer-mandated or legally required inspection interval without the required engineering and regulatory approval.

6. Calendar-based versus operating-hour-based PM

Calendar time and equipment usage measure different things.

  • Calendar based: the task becomes due after a defined time interval.
  • Operating-hour based: the task becomes due after a defined number of running hours.
  • Cycle based: the task becomes due after a defined number of operations.
  • Condition based: the task becomes due when a measured indicator reaches a threshold.
  • Event based: the task becomes due after a specified incident or intervention.

Example: a machine runs for 24 hours per day, while another machine of the same type operates for only 6 hours per day. If a component's wear depends mainly on operating hours, a calendar interval alone may not reflect its actual usage.

Conversely, a battery, seal, or other component may age with calendar time even when equipment usage is low.

For an operating-hour interval:

\[ \text{Estimated calendar interval} = \frac{\text{Required operating hours}}{\text{Average operating hours per day}} \]

If an inspection is due every 500 operating hours and the machine averages 20 operating hours per day:

\[ \frac{500}{20}=25\text{ operating days} \]

This is an estimate. The actual due date depends on recorded running hours, downtime, and the maintenance system's scheduling rules.

8. PM frequency for robot components

Robot components require different maintenance intervals because their failure mechanisms differ.

Robot component Possible PM basis Example of maintenance activity
Controller Calendar and condition based Review faults, cooling condition, and backup status
Motors and servo drives Condition, operating hours, and manufacturer requirements Review drive diagnostics, load indicators, and temperature
Gearboxes and reducers Manufacturer schedule and condition Inspect for leakage, abnormal noise, and specified lubrication requirements
Cables and dress packs Condition and cycle exposure Inspect for wear, abrasion, damage, and routing problems
Teach pendant Condition and functional requirements Inspect cable, display, controls, and enclosure condition
Calibration and mastering data Event based and verification requirements Verify after relevant mechanical, encoder, or controller interventions
Batteries Manufacturer-defined service interval and condition Replace using the approved procedure and record the replacement
Programs Change based and periodic review Verify approved revision, dependencies, and integrity
Backups Change based and periodic verification Check approved revision, secondary copy, and recovery readiness
Alarm history Daily review and periodic trend analysis Identify recurring faults and confirm corrective-action effectiveness

The term condition based does not mean waiting for a breakdown. It means using measurable indicators, inspections, or diagnostics to decide when intervention is necessary.

9. PM frequency for electrical and automation systems

PLC and control panels

Potential tasks include:

  • Daily status and alarm review.
  • Periodic review of module diagnostics and communication errors.
  • Inspection of cooling, ventilation, and contamination.
  • Approved inspection of wiring and connections.
  • Periodic review of PLC program and hardware configuration backups.
  • Event-based checks after module replacement, power events, or configuration changes.

Do not routinely reseat PLC modules, remove wiring, or retighten terminals without an approved procedure. Unnecessary intervention can introduce faults.

VFDs and servo drives

Potential tasks include:

  • Review active warnings and fault history.
  • Observe abnormal noise, overheating, or cooling issues.
  • Inspect cooling paths and filters according to the manufacturer's requirements.
  • Review operating load and recurring fault patterns.
  • Perform specified electrical and mechanical inspections under appropriate isolation.
  • Review configuration and parameter backups after approved changes.

Drive capacitors and DC buses may retain hazardous voltage after isolation. Follow the manufacturer's discharge time and voltage-verification procedure before work.

Sensors and field wiring

Potential tasks include:

  • Routine checks for visible damage and abnormal indication.
  • Periodic inspection of mounting, alignment, contamination, and cable routing.
  • Review recurring signal dropouts and I/O diagnostic events.
  • Functional verification at the specified interval.
  • Calibration or replacement according to device requirements.

The required frequency depends on sensor type, environment, process tolerance, and the consequences of an undetected failure.

10. Frequency planning for safety-related equipment

Safety-related devices must be maintained according to their design, risk assessment, manufacturer instructions, and applicable requirements.

Examples include emergency stops, guard interlocks, safety scanners, enabling devices, safety PLCs, and robot safety functions.

The maintenance plan should distinguish between:

  • Routine visual checks.
  • Required functional checks.
  • Periodic proof tests or validation activities.
  • Post-repair and post-change validation.
  • Checks after incidents, impacts, or suspected safety-system faults.

A daily operator check does not replace a formal proof test or safety validation. Similarly, a safety function passing a routine check does not prove that all aspects of the safety system remain compliant.

Do not invent test intervals for safety devices. Use the approved safety documentation and applicable requirements.

11. Scheduling PM work without disrupting production

A technically correct task can still fail as a maintenance system if it is scheduled when the equipment cannot be accessed or the required resources are unavailable.

A good PM schedule accounts for:

  • Production calendar and planned shutdowns.
  • Equipment availability and isolation requirements.
  • Task duration and required manpower.
  • Specialist skills and permits.
  • Tools, test instruments, and spare parts.
  • Dependencies between tasks.
  • Required approvals and safety precautions.
  • Whether a task can be performed online or requires downtime.
  • The production impact of delaying the task.

Example

A monthly robot backup review may be completed while the cell remains available, provided the approved method allows it. A mechanical inspection requiring access inside the safeguarded workspace may require isolation and a coordinated shutdown.

These tasks should not be scheduled as if they have the same access requirements merely because both are monthly.

12. PM calendar and due-date calculation

A PM calendar converts frequency definitions into actionable due dates.

For a simple calendar-based interval:

\[ \text{Next due date} = \text{Last completion date} + \text{Defined interval} \]

However, the maintenance system must apply the organization's scheduling rules. Calendar-month intervals, fixed-date schedules, and intervals measured from actual completion can produce different dates.

For example, a monthly task may be scheduled on a fixed day each month, while a task due every 30 days is calculated from the previous date. These are not necessarily equivalent.

A PM plan should specify:

  • Scheduling basis.
  • Cycle or time interval.
  • Start date and scheduling anchor.
  • Tolerance or permitted scheduling window.
  • Rules for missed or overdue tasks.
  • Rules for shutdown-dependent work.
  • Whether completion resets the next due date.
  • Whether critical overdue tasks require escalation.

Avoid silently changing due dates simply to make a maintenance dashboard appear green.

13. PM compliance, overdue tasks, and schedule control

A PM plan must distinguish between work that is scheduled, released, completed, overdue, and verified.

Status Meaning Required management response
Planned Included in the future maintenance schedule Ensure resources and access are available
Due Scheduled interval has arrived Arrange execution within the approved window
Released Work order or task has been authorized for execution Confirm permits, isolation, tools, and personnel
In progress Work has started Track completion and any new findings
Completed Task execution has been recorded Verify required results and documentation
Overdue Due date or permitted window has passed Assess risk, prioritize, and escalate as required
Deferred Work has been postponed with a recorded reason Document authorization, risk assessment, and revised date
Cancelled Task has been formally cancelled Record the reason and ensure cancellation is authorized

A task marked complete should have evidence appropriate to the work performed. For an inspection, this may include recorded findings. For a test, it may include the test result and instrument details. For a replacement, it may include the component identity and relevant configuration or calibration checks.

13.1 PM compliance calculation

A commonly used measure is:

\[ \text{PM compliance (\\%)} = \frac{\text{PM tasks completed within the defined window}} {\text{PM tasks due in that window}} \times 100 \]

For example, if 95 out of 100 tasks due during a month were completed within the approved scheduling window:

\[ \frac{95}{100}\times100=95\\% \]

The organization must define how cancelled, deferred, duplicate, and shutdown-dependent tasks are counted. Otherwise, the percentage can be misleading.

PM compliance alone does not measure maintenance quality. A completed task may have been ineffective, incorrectly performed, or poorly documented.

14. Missed PM and overdue-task management

When a PM task becomes overdue, the response should depend on the task's criticality and the consequences of delaying it.

  1. Confirm the reason for the delay.
  2. Identify the equipment, task, and required interval.
  3. Assess the risk of postponement.
  4. Determine whether temporary controls or operating restrictions are required.
  5. Obtain the required approval for any deferral.
  6. Reschedule the task and arrange resources.
  7. Complete and document the work.
  8. Investigate repeated scheduling failures where appropriate.

Safety-critical, legally mandated, and manufacturer-required tasks must follow their applicable requirements. They must not be casually deferred because production is busy.

Repeated overdue tasks may indicate inadequate manpower, unrealistic task durations, poor scheduling, missing spares, access constraints, or weak ownership. These are planning problems, not merely technician performance problems.

15. CMMS and SAP PM implementation

A computerized maintenance management system (CMMS), including SAP Plant Maintenance (SAP PM), can manage recurring tasks and convert them into maintenance orders or notifications according to the configured process.

A well-defined PM plan should include:

  • Equipment number and functional location.
  • Task list or maintenance strategy.
  • Task description and inspection criteria.
  • Frequency and scheduling basis.
  • Planned duration and required manpower.
  • Work center and responsible group.
  • Required spare parts, tools, and test instruments.
  • Safety precautions, permits, and isolation requirements.
  • Acceptance criteria and measurement fields.
  • Task list version and revision status.
  • Scheduling parameters and due-date rules.
  • Completion evidence and follow-up actions.

Depending on the SAP configuration, maintenance plans may use time-based strategies, performance-based scheduling, or multiple maintenance packages. The exact setup depends on the organization's SAP implementation.

Example PM task structure

Field Example
Functional location Robotic cell, station 03
Equipment Industrial robot 07
Task Review alarm history and recurring faults
Frequency Daily or per shift, according to the approved plan
Responsible group Central maintenance or designated automation team
Work instruction Approved robot alarm-history inspection procedure
Acceptance criterion Critical alarms reviewed, abnormalities recorded, and required escalation initiated
Completion evidence Alarm codes, findings, and corrective-action references
Follow-up Maintenance notification for unresolved or recurring faults

The example shows a task definition, not a universal SAP transaction sequence or prescribed frequency.

16. Improving PM frequency using reliability data

PM intervals should be reviewed using actual equipment performance, inspection findings, and failure mechanisms.

Useful measures include:

  • PM compliance.
  • Overdue task count and age.
  • Breakdown frequency.
  • Repeat-failure rate.
  • MTBF and MTTR.
  • Planned versus unplanned maintenance hours.
  • Inspection defect rate.
  • Percentage of PM tasks that identify actionable deterioration.
  • Failure recurrence after corrective maintenance.
  • Cost of maintenance and downtime.

Frequency review method

  1. Identify the task and the failure mechanism it addresses.
  2. Review the last several maintenance cycles and relevant failures.
  3. Examine inspection findings and whether they predicted failures.
  4. Determine whether the interval was too long, too short, or suitable.
  5. Check whether the task method itself was effective.
  6. Propose a change with supporting evidence.
  7. Obtain the required technical and management approval.
  8. Monitor results after the change.

If inspections repeatedly find no deterioration, that may justify reviewing the task, but it does not automatically justify reducing its frequency. The component may have a low-probability, high-consequence failure mode that still requires inspection or testing.

Likewise, recurring failures do not always justify more frequent inspection. The correct improvement may be a design modification, improved cooling, better cable routing, a revised program, or elimination of the underlying failure mechanism.

17. Common PM frequency planning mistakes

  • Applying the same interval to every component on a machine.
  • Using calendar time when operating hours or cycle count better represent wear.
  • Ignoring manufacturer requirements and safety-related intervals.
  • Scheduling tasks without checking access, manpower, tools, and spares.
  • Treating every PM task as a shutdown activity.
  • Replacing components on a calendar basis without technical justification.
  • Extending an interval because previous inspections found no defects, without risk review.
  • Increasing task frequency without identifying the failure mechanism.
  • Marking tasks complete without evidence.
  • Closing overdue tasks administratively without performing the required work.
  • Using PM compliance as the only measure of maintenance effectiveness.
  • Failing to update task lists after equipment modifications.
  • Creating duplicate PM plans for the same equipment and task.
  • Failing to distinguish operator checks from qualified maintenance work.

For each machine, create a task-frequency matrix with at least the following fields:

Use the matrix to verify that every critical system has suitable maintenance coverage and that each task has a defined method, interval, owner, and response to abnormal findings.

19. Completion criteria

A PM frequency plan is ready for implementation when:

  • Each task has a justified interval and scheduling basis.
  • Manufacturer, legal, and safety requirements have been addressed.
  • Calendar, operating-hour, cycle, condition, and event-based tasks are distinguished.
  • Acceptance criteria and follow-up actions are defined.
  • Work duration, resources, access, and safety requirements are known.
  • The scheduling system generates appropriate due dates.
  • Overdue and deferred tasks have a controlled escalation process.
  • Completion evidence is defined.
  • PM effectiveness will be reviewed using actual results.
  • Changes to frequency are controlled and traceable.

The purpose of PM frequency planning is to make maintenance predictable and effective while reducing both preventable failures and unnecessary intervention.
