1. Definition and Purpose

A CNC servo system is the closed-loop motion-control system that moves a machine tool's axes accurately according to commands from the CNC controller.

It converts programmed machining instructions into controlled mechanical movement. The system regulates motor position, speed, and torque while monitoring feedback to ensure the axis follows the required motion within specified limits.

In a CNC machine, servo systems commonly control the X, Y, and Z linear axes and additional rotary axes such as A, B, and C. Depending on the machine architecture, similar control principles may also apply to tool changers, auxiliary positioning axes, and other controlled mechanisms.

The primary objectives are:

  • Accurate positioning of machine axes.
  • Controlled acceleration, deceleration, and velocity.
  • Coordinated multi-axis movement.
  • Stable operation under changing cutting loads.
  • Detection of excessive following error and other abnormal conditions.
  • Repeatable machining and dimensional accuracy.
  • Reliable integration with CNC, PLC, feedback, and machine safety systems.

A servo system is not just a servo motor. It is a coordinated combination of the controller, drive, motor, feedback device, mechanical transmission, and associated configuration.

2. CNC Servo System Architecture

CNC Controller
    ↓ Position / Velocity / Motion Commands
Servo Drive
    ↓ Controlled Motor Current and Voltage
Servo Motor
    ↓ Torque and Rotation
Mechanical Transmission
    ↓
Machine Axis / Table / Tool Movement
    ↑
Encoder or Position Feedback
    ↑
Servo Drive / Motion Control
    ↓
Actual Position Compared with Commanded Position

This is a simplified functional representation. Depending on the control architecture, position, velocity, and current loops may be distributed between the CNC controller and servo drive.

A typical system includes the following components:

Component Function Typical fault
CNC controller Generates motion commands and coordinates axes Axis-control or communication alarm
Servo drive Controls motor current and motion response Overcurrent, undervoltage, feedback fault
Servo motor Converts electrical energy into controlled torque and rotation Overheating, winding fault, abnormal noise
Encoder or feedback device Measures motor position and supports speed estimation Feedback loss or position error
Coupling and transmission Transfers motor movement to the machine axis Backlash, looseness, mechanical binding
Ball screw or linear drive Converts rotation into linear movement, where fitted Wear, friction, pitch error
Linear scale, if fitted Measures actual linear axis position Scale contamination or feedback error
Brake, if fitted Holds the axis under specified conditions Brake does not release or hold correctly
Power and feedback cables Carry electrical power and measurement signals Intermittent faults, insulation damage
Cooling system Removes heat from the drive or motor Overtemperature

3. Servo System Versus Open-Loop Stepper System

A CNC servo system generally uses feedback to regulate motion. An open-loop stepper system typically commands steps without continuously verifying actual position through a feedback loop.

Feature Closed-loop servo system Typical open-loop stepper system
Feedback Uses position or other feedback Does not necessarily use position feedback
Position correction Can correct motion based on feedback Cannot independently detect lost steps without feedback
High-speed performance Designed for controlled dynamic motion within rated limits Depends on stepper characteristics and load
Overload response May detect following error or drive faults May lose synchronism without direct position detection
Tuning Requires configuration of motion and control behavior Usually requires step and motion parameter configuration
Typical application Industrial CNC axes and demanding motion systems Smaller machines and less demanding positioning applications

Closed-loop feedback does not make a servo system immune to positioning errors. Mechanical backlash, incorrect compensation, feedback faults, poor tuning, and thermal effects can still affect accuracy.

4. Types of Servo Control Loops

Many industrial servo systems use nested control loops. The exact arrangement and implementation depend on the drive and CNC platform.

4.1 Current or Torque Loop

The current loop regulates motor current. In many AC servo motors, controlled current produces electromagnetic torque.

For a simplified motor model:

\[ T \approx K_t I \]

Where:

  • \(T\) is motor torque.
  • \(K_t\) is the motor torque constant.
  • \(I\) is the relevant torque-producing current component.

This relationship is simplified. Actual torque production depends on motor design, current-vector control, magnetic conditions, and drive implementation.

The current loop is generally the fastest loop in a conventional nested servo architecture.

4.2 Velocity Loop

The velocity loop regulates motor speed using a speed command and measured or estimated actual speed.

\[ e_v(t)=v\_{\text{commanded}}(t)-v\_{\text{actual}}(t) \]

The controller uses the velocity error to determine the torque or current demand required to accelerate, decelerate, or maintain speed.

4.3 Position Loop

The position loop compares commanded position with measured position and generates motion corrections.

\[ e_p(t)=x\_{\text{commanded}}(t)-x\_{\text{actual}}(t) \]

The position-control function determines the corrective motion demand based on the error and configured control behavior.

Typical nested-loop relationship

Position Command
      ↓
Position Control
      ↓ Velocity Demand
Velocity Control
      ↓ Torque / Current Demand
Current Control
      ↓
Motor Torque
      ↓
Machine Movement
      ↑
Position and Speed Feedback

Some CNC systems perform portions of the position loop in the CNC controller, while the drive performs velocity and current regulation. Other architectures distribute functions differently.

Maintenance implication: A position error does not automatically indicate a defective encoder. The fault may involve motion commands, control tuning, current limits, drive behavior, feedback, or mechanical resistance.

5. Servo Motor Types Used in CNC Machines

5.1 AC Synchronous Servo Motor

Permanent-magnet synchronous servo motors are common in industrial CNC axes.

Typical characteristics include:

  • High torque density.
  • Accurate electronic commutation.
  • Encoder or other rotor-feedback requirements, depending on the design.
  • Good dynamic response.
  • Integration with a compatible servo drive.

The drive must be configured for the correct motor type and feedback arrangement.

5.2 AC Induction Motor Used in Servo Applications

Some motion systems use induction motors with suitable vector-control drives and feedback arrangements.

The motor and drive combination determines achievable dynamic performance, accuracy, and control behavior. Do not assume that every CNC axis uses a permanent-magnet servo motor.

5.3 Linear Motor

A linear motor produces linear force directly rather than generating rotary motion that must be converted through a ball screw or similar transmission.

Potential advantages include:

  • Direct linear movement.
  • Reduced transmission-related backlash.
  • High dynamic response in suitable designs.

Maintenance considerations include cooling, magnetic contamination, cable management, feedback integrity, and the condition of the linear guide system.

5.4 Torque Motor

Torque motors are used in some direct-drive rotary axes, tables, and precision positioning systems.

They can eliminate certain transmission components, but their behavior depends on motor sizing, cooling, feedback, bearing design, and control configuration.

6. Servo Drive and CNC Communication

The CNC controller and servo drive exchange commands, status information, and diagnostic data through a supported interface.

Depending on the machine, the interface may use a manufacturer-specific motion bus or an industrial network.

Typical information exchanged includes:

  • Position or motion commands.
  • Velocity or torque references.
  • Drive enable requests.
  • Ready and fault status.
  • Actual position and velocity.
  • Following-error information.
  • Operating-mode and diagnostic data.

A communication fault may stop an axis even when the motor, encoder, and mechanical transmission are healthy.

During diagnosis, distinguish between:

  • Loss of communication between CNC and drive.
  • Loss of feedback between encoder and drive.
  • Failure of the drive's motor power stage.
  • Failure of the CNC controller to generate or authorize motion.

These conditions can produce similar symptoms but require different investigations.

7. Position Feedback Devices

The servo system needs feedback appropriate to its control architecture.

Common feedback technologies include:

  • Incremental rotary encoder.
  • Absolute rotary encoder.
  • Resolver.
  • Linear scale or linear encoder.
  • Other supported motor or machine-position feedback devices.

Motor encoder versus linear scale

A motor encoder measures motor rotation. The CNC system may infer linear axis movement from the motor position and transmission characteristics.

A linear scale measures linear position more directly at its installation location.

Feature Motor encoder Linear scale
Measurement location Motor shaft or motor feedback system Machine axis or linear measurement path
Measures transmission errors directly Generally no Can capture errors within the measured axis path
Mechanical transmission effects May not detect all backlash or pitch errors Can reflect many errors between the scale's measurement points
Installation Often integrated with the motor Requires a separate linear measurement installation
Maintenance concerns Encoder, feedback cable, connector Scale contamination, alignment, readhead, cable

A linear scale does not automatically eliminate all machine errors. Its location, mounting, calibration, control configuration, and mechanical relationship to the tool and workpiece remain important.

8. Commanded Position, Actual Position, and Following Error

Following error is the difference between the commanded and actual position values used by the control system.

\[ e(t)=x_c(t)-x_a(t) \]

Where:

  • \(x_c(t)\) is commanded position.
  • \(x_a(t)\) is actual position.
  • \(e(t)\) is following error.

The control system generally permits some dynamic error during motion. A fault may occur when the error exceeds a configured threshold or another diagnostic condition is met.

Possible causes include:

  • Mechanical binding.
  • Excessive cutting load.
  • Incorrect servo tuning.
  • Excessive acceleration.
  • Motor or drive torque limitation.
  • Feedback interruption.
  • Mechanical looseness.
  • Incorrect motion configuration.
  • Axis travel restriction.

Practical interpretation

If following error increases during acceleration, investigate the motion demand, torque capability, tuning, and load.

If it increases at a consistent mechanical position, investigate local binding, guideway condition, transmission damage, cable movement, and other position-dependent conditions.

If actual position changes unexpectedly while the axis should be stationary, investigate the feedback system, mechanical holding behavior, and control state using the approved diagnostic procedure.

9. Servo Tuning and Stability

Servo tuning determines how the control system responds to position and velocity errors.

Typical tuning considerations include:

  • Position-loop gain.
  • Velocity-loop gain.
  • Integral action, where applicable.
  • Feedforward settings.
  • Filtering and resonance suppression.
  • Acceleration and deceleration behavior.
  • Inertia estimation or load adaptation, where supported.

Incorrect tuning may result in:

  • Oscillation.
  • Excessive overshoot.
  • Long settling time.
  • Abnormal noise.
  • Vibration.
  • Increased following error.
  • Poor contouring accuracy.
  • Unnecessary mechanical stress.

Increasing gain is not a universal solution to following error. Excessive gain can make the system unstable or amplify mechanical resonance.

Tuning changes should be performed by authorized personnel using the manufacturer's procedure, suitable measurements, and controlled test conditions. Preserve the original settings before making changes.

10. Servo Torque and Load Conditions

The servo motor must generate sufficient torque to overcome the load and accelerate the moving components.

The required torque depends on several factors:

  • Mass and inertia.
  • Acceleration and deceleration.
  • Friction.
  • Cutting forces.
  • Transmission efficiency.
  • Vertical-axis gravity load.
  • Mechanical condition.
  • Operating speed and duty cycle.

A simplified rotary relationship is:

\[ T=J\alpha \]

Where:

  • \(T\) is the net torque associated with angular acceleration.
  • \(J\) is the equivalent rotary inertia.
  • \(\alpha\) is angular acceleration.

This simplified equation does not include friction, external process forces, gravity effects, or transmission losses. Those must be considered in a complete machine-load calculation.

Excessive torque demand can result in high motor current, following errors, overheating, or drive trips.

When investigating increased torque demand, inspect the mechanical system as well as the electrical and control components. A worn bearing, damaged coupling, tight guideway, or obstructed mechanism may create symptoms that resemble an electrical fault.

11. Servo Parameters Relevant to CNC Axes

Servo-related parameters influence the relationship between commanded motion, motor response, feedback, and machine position.

Depending on the platform, relevant parameter groups may include:

Parameter group Purpose Risk if incorrect
Motor identification Defines the compatible motor and its characteristics Incorrect commutation or motor behavior
Feedback configuration Defines the feedback type and scaling Invalid position measurement
Electronic gearing or scaling Relates command units to motor movement Incorrect travel distance
Velocity limits Restricts axis speed Unexpected speed limitation or unsuitable operation
Acceleration limits Defines permitted acceleration behavior Excessive load or motion faults
Torque/current limits Restricts motor effort Inadequate torque or excessive mechanical stress
Position-loop settings Controls position response Instability or poor accuracy
Velocity-loop settings Controls speed response Oscillation or sluggish response
Following-error limits Defines permitted position deviation Nuisance faults or insufficient protection
Reference-return settings Defines the reference procedure Incorrect machine coordinate establishment
Travel limits Restricts movement range Incorrect travel protection
Compensation settings Corrects supported mechanical or measurement errors Dimensional errors if configured incorrectly

Parameter numbering and meaning vary between manufacturers and machine models. These categories are conceptual, not a universal parameter list.

Always use the correct CNC, drive, and machine-builder documentation.

12. CNC Servo Faults and Diagnostic Approach

Symptom Possible causes Recommended diagnostic direction
Axis will not enable Interlock, drive fault, communication problem, safety state Check controller, PLC, drive, and safety status
Axis does not move Missing command, reference issue, brake, drive or feedback fault Review axis state and drive diagnostics
Following-error alarm Excessive load, tuning, feedback issue, mechanical binding Compare commanded and actual motion
Positioning error Scaling, compensation, backlash, feedback, mechanical wear Verify coordinate configuration and machine condition
Oscillation or vibration Tuning, resonance, coupling, mechanical looseness Check mechanical condition and approved tuning diagnostics
Overcurrent Short circuit, overload, mechanical resistance, configuration issue Review fault timing, motor circuit, and load
Encoder alarm Cable, connector, feedback supply, encoder, compatibility Inspect feedback diagnostics and cable condition
Overtemperature Excessive duty, high load, cooling issue Review load trend, ventilation, and temperature diagnostics
Intermittent axis fault Cable flexing, loose connector, intermittent supply or network issue Correlate fault with axis position and movement
Axis drifts when stationary Feedback, holding brake, mechanical load, control state Secure the axis and investigate according to the approved procedure

Do not change parameters simply because an alarm is related to servo performance. Establish the failure mechanism first.

13. Practical Example: Following Error During Machining

Situation

A CNC machining centre intermittently generates a following-error alarm on the X-axis during heavy cutting. Rapid positioning without cutting appears normal.

Investigation

  1. Capture the complete CNC and drive alarm history.
  2. Record the program block, cutting condition, and axis state.
  3. Compare the following error and torque demand during cutting and non-cutting motion.
  4. Check for unusual cutting forces or a process condition that has changed.
  5. Inspect the mechanical transmission and axis movement under the approved maintenance procedure.
  6. Review the motor, drive, feedback, and motion configuration if the evidence indicates an electrical or control issue.
  7. Compare the results with the machine's baseline behavior.

Possible findings

  • A mechanical component has developed increased friction.
  • The cutting process is demanding more torque than the system can provide under the current conditions.
  • A feedback cable is intermittently failing during movement.
  • The motion configuration is unsuitable for the actual load.

Corrective action

Correct the verified cause and validate axis movement and machining performance. Avoid simply increasing the following-error limit, as this may conceal the underlying problem.

14. Practical Example: Positioning Error After Mechanical Maintenance

Situation

After a ball-screw or coupling intervention, the machine reports incorrect positioning even though the servo drive shows no active fault.

Investigation

  • Verify the machine reference and coordinate system.
  • Check the coupling and mechanical assembly.
  • Confirm the relevant feedback and scaling configuration.
  • Review any approved backlash or pitch compensation data.
  • Compare commanded position with actual position using the available diagnostic tools.
  • Perform the manufacturer's prescribed positioning verification.

Possible causes

  • Incorrect coupling installation.
  • Mechanical backlash or looseness.
  • Incorrect reference establishment.
  • Feedback scaling or configuration mismatch.
  • Compensation data changed or not restored.

Corrective action

Restore the correct mechanical assembly and approved configuration, then perform the required calibration and positioning checks.

The absence of a servo alarm does not prove that the axis is dimensionally accurate.

15. Practical Example: Servo Axis Fault After Motor Replacement

Situation

A replacement motor has been installed. The drive powers up, but the CNC reports an axis or feedback fault during startup.

Investigation

  1. Verify the exact replacement motor and drive compatibility.
  2. Confirm the feedback type and connector pinout.
  3. Check the approved motor and drive configuration.
  4. Inspect power, feedback, and brake connections where fitted.
  5. Review the exact alarm code and startup sequence.
  6. Confirm whether the controller requires an approved initialization, reference, or commissioning procedure.

Possible causes

  • Incorrect motor or feedback combination.
  • Wrong cable or connector pinout.
  • Configuration not updated for the replacement.
  • Damaged connector.
  • Incorrect brake wiring or control sequence.

Corrective action

Use the correct approved motor and configuration, correct the identified connection fault, and complete the required commissioning and reference verification before releasing the machine.

16. Safe Maintenance and Testing

Servo axes can produce unexpected movement and hazardous torque. CNC machines may also contain suspended loads, rotating components, stored electrical energy, and automatic restart functions.

Before maintenance:

  1. Follow the machine's approved shutdown and lockout/tagout procedure.
  2. Identify all relevant electrical, mechanical, pneumatic, and hydraulic energy sources.
  3. Account for DC bus stored energy and verify the required discharge procedure.
  4. Secure vertical axes and other gravity-loaded mechanisms.
  5. Prevent unexpected motion from automatic sequences or remote commands.
  6. Verify the required safe state before touching or disconnecting components.
  7. Use approved test methods and suitable instruments.
  8. Restore guards and protective devices before functional testing.

Do not manually force a servo axis or release a holding brake on a loaded vertical axis without the approved mechanical support and safety procedure.

Insulation-resistance testing must not be performed through connected servo drives or encoder electronics. Follow the equipment manufacturer's instructions for isolating the motor and cables before testing.

17. Preventive Maintenance Checklist

Routine inspection

  • Review CNC and servo-drive alarm history.
  • Identify recurring following-error or feedback faults.
  • Inspect accessible motor and feedback cables.
  • Check for cable-carrier wear and movement-related damage.
  • Inspect connectors, strain relief, and protective covers.
  • Review abnormal torque, current, or temperature trends where available.
  • Check cabinet cooling and drive ventilation.
  • Record unusual vibration, noise, or positioning behavior.

Planned maintenance

  • Verify the approved CNC and drive parameter backups.
  • Review motor, encoder, and drive compatibility records.
  • Inspect relevant mechanical couplings and transmission components.
  • Check feedback integrity using approved procedures.
  • Verify axis reference and positioning according to the maintenance plan.
  • Review tuning or compensation changes made since the previous inspection.
  • Confirm availability of approved replacement cables, motors, and feedback components.
  • Record findings and update maintenance history.

Inspection frequency should reflect manufacturer requirements, axis criticality, machine duty, environment, and the history of recurring failures.

18. CNC Servo Spare Parts and Configuration Control

Servo-system replacement parts must be selected by compatibility, not appearance.

Maintain traceable information for:

  • CNC controller and axis designation.
  • Servo drive model and firmware.
  • Motor model and rated characteristics.
  • Encoder or feedback type.
  • Motor and feedback cable part numbers.
  • Coupling and transmission specifications.
  • Brake specifications where fitted.
  • Parameter and configuration backup revisions.
  • Approved replacement or successor part numbers.

A replacement motor may fit mechanically but still be incompatible with the drive, feedback interface, commutation configuration, or machine application.

After a replacement, update the maintenance records and retain the approved configuration and commissioning results.

19. Key Engineering Principles

  • A CNC servo system combines motion commands, drive control, feedback, motor torque, and mechanical transmission.
  • Position, velocity, and current loops have distinct functions.
  • Motor feedback and linear-axis measurement are not necessarily the same thing.
  • Following error is a diagnostic measurement, not a root-cause diagnosis.
  • Mechanical resistance can produce symptoms that resemble drive or motor faults.
  • Servo tuning and parameter changes require controlled procedures and verified backups.
  • A healthy drive status does not guarantee correct machine positioning.
  • Replacement motors, encoders, cables, and drives must be compatible with the complete system.
  • Error history and trend data should be used together where available.
  • Safe isolation, load securing, and controlled commissioning are essential to reliable maintenance.
