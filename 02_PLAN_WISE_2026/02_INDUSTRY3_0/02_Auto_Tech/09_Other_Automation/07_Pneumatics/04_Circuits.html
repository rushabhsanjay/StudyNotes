1. Purpose of Pneumatic Circuits

A pneumatic circuit connects air-preparation equipment, directional valves, flow-control devices, cylinders, sensors, and other components so that compressed air produces a required machine movement.

In industrial automation, circuit design must achieve more than simply extending and retracting a cylinder. It must provide the correct motion, speed, sequence, repeatability, and behavior during faults.

A pneumatic circuit determines:

  • Where compressed air flows.
  • Which actuator moves and in which direction.
  • How quickly the actuator extends and retracts.
  • How multiple actuators operate in the correct sequence.
  • How pressure and exhaust restrictions affect performance.
  • How sensors and PLC logic confirm that a movement has completed.
  • How the system behaves during loss of air, electrical power, or feedback.

Pneumatic circuits can be implemented using purely pneumatic logic, electrical solenoid valves, PLC-controlled valves, or a combination of these approaches.

2. Basic Pneumatic Circuit Architecture

A typical PLC-controlled pneumatic system has the following structure:

Compressed-air source
        ↓
Isolation and air preparation
        ↓
Pressure regulation and distribution
        ↓
Directional control valves
        ↓
Flow-control devices and tubing
        ↓
Pneumatic cylinders or other actuators
        ↓
Position and pressure feedback
        ↓
PLC sequence logic
        ↓
Commands to valve solenoids

The diagram represents functional relationships rather than a mandatory physical installation order. Actual systems may include additional filters, dryers, lubricators, pressure switches, safety valves, manifolds, and local regulators.

The key distinction is that the pneumatic circuit carries energy, while the control system determines when and how that energy is used.

3. Pneumatic Tubing and Pipework

Tubing transports compressed air between the supply, valves, and actuators. Its dimensions and installation quality directly affect flow, pressure drop, motion speed, and reliability.

3.1 Tubing Material

Common materials include:

  • Polyurethane (PU) tubing for flexible pneumatic connections.
  • Polyamide (PA or nylon) tubing for applications requiring suitable pressure and mechanical resistance.
  • Polyethylene (PE) tubing for compatible applications within its specified limits.
  • Metal tubing or pipework where required by pressure, temperature, environment, or installation design.

Material selection must consider working pressure, temperature, chemical compatibility, flexibility, abrasion, external damage, and the manufacturer's rating.

Do not select tubing by material name alone. Different grades and wall thicknesses have different operating limits.

3.2 Tube Outer Diameter and Inner Diameter

Pneumatic tubing is often specified by its outer diameter, such as 6 mm, 8 mm, or 10 mm. However, the inner diameter is particularly important for flow capacity.

For a tube with outer diameter \(D_o\) and wall thickness \(t\):

\[ D_i=D_o-2t \]

Where \(D_i\) is the inner diameter.

A tube can fit the same push-in fitting as another tube while having a different inner diameter and therefore different flow characteristics.

3.3 Tube Diameter and Length

A smaller tube or longer tube increases flow resistance and can create pressure drop during high-demand movement.

Typical consequences include:

  • Slower cylinder movement.
  • Delayed response to valve commands.
  • Pressure variation during simultaneous actuator movements.
  • Inconsistent cycle time under changing loads.
  • Poor performance despite apparently adequate static supply pressure.

A common diagnostic mistake is to measure pressure only when the machine is idle. Pressure should also be evaluated during the actual movement that causes the problem.

3.4 Push-In Fittings

Push-in fittings allow tubing to be inserted into a fitting body and retained by a gripping mechanism, with a seal preventing leakage.

Reliable installation requires:

  1. Use tubing with the correct outer diameter and compatible material.
  2. Cut the tube squarely using a suitable cutter.
  3. Remove burrs and avoid damaging the tube end.
  4. Insert the tube fully into the fitting.
  5. Check for secure retention and leakage.
  6. Follow the manufacturer's procedure when removing the tube.

An angled cut, scratched tube surface, damaged O-ring, or incomplete insertion can create leakage or intermittent faults.

3.5 Common Tubing Installation Faults

Fault Likely effect Corrective action
Undersized tubing Excessive pressure drop and slow movement Verify inner diameter and required flow
Excessively long tubing Delayed response and pressure loss Reduce length where practical or resize the circuit
Kinked tubing Restricted airflow Replace damaged tubing and improve routing
Loose fitting Leakage and unstable performance Reinstall or replace the fitting correctly
Damaged tube end Leakage at the seal Cut back to undamaged tubing or replace it
Wrong fitting size Poor retention or leakage Match tube and fitting specifications
Tubing connected to the wrong port Reversed or unexpected motion Trace the circuit against the valve diagram
Shared line with excessive demand Pressure drop during simultaneous movement Review supply capacity and circuit distribution

4. Directional Valves in Circuit Design

Directional control valves determine which cylinder chamber receives pressure and which chamber exhausts.

Common choices include:

  • 3/2 valve: Usually used for a single-acting cylinder or a pneumatic signal.
  • 5/2 valve: Commonly used for a double-acting cylinder.
  • 5/3 valve: Used when a double-acting actuator requires a defined center flow condition.

The valve's port configuration must match the intended circuit. Port A does not universally mean extension, and a de-energized solenoid does not universally mean retraction.

For every circuit, establish:

  • The normal valve state.
  • The commanded state for each motion.
  • The actual port connections.
  • The exhaust paths.
  • The behavior when a command disappears.
  • The behavior after air or electrical power returns.

5. Flow-Control Valves and Cylinder Speed

A directional valve determines the flow path, but a flow-control valve can restrict airflow to regulate cylinder speed.

Common devices include:

  • Adjustable needle valves.
  • One-way flow-control valves.
  • Fixed flow restrictors.
  • Pressure-compensated flow-control devices in suitable applications.

A typical one-way flow-control valve permits relatively free flow in one direction and restricts flow in the other direction.

5.1 Meter-In Control

Meter-in control restricts the air entering the cylinder chamber.

Directional valve
       ↓
Flow control restricts inlet air
       ↓
Cylinder chamber

This approach can work well for some loads, but it may produce unstable motion when an external force drives the actuator in the same direction as its intended movement.

For example, an overrunning or gravity-assisted load can cause the piston to move faster than the incoming air can control reliably. The chamber may not remain adequately pressurized throughout the motion.

5.2 Meter-Out Control

Meter-out control restricts air leaving the cylinder chamber.

Directional valve
       ↑
Flow control restricts exhaust air
       ↑
Cylinder chamber

The exhaust restriction creates backpressure that can help control the movement of many pneumatic cylinders. It is widely used for industrial cylinder speed adjustment.

However, meter-out is not automatically suitable for every load or application. Gravity-loaded mechanisms and safety-critical movements may need additional engineering measures.

5.3 Meter-In Versus Meter-Out

Feature Meter-in Meter-out
Restricted flow Air entering the driven chamber Air leaving the chamber
Main control effect Limits inlet flow Creates exhaust backpressure
Common application Suitable loads that remain controlled by inlet flow Many conventional pneumatic cylinder applications
Potential limitation Overrunning loads may move unpredictably Excessive backpressure can reduce available force or affect motion
Diagnostic focus Inlet restriction and chamber pressure Exhaust restriction and backpressure

5.4 One-Way Flow-Control Valve

A one-way flow-control valve combines an adjustable restriction with a check-valve bypass.

One direction allows relatively free flow through the check valve. The opposite direction forces flow through the adjustable restriction.

The valve must be installed in the intended orientation. Reversing it can cause a cylinder to move quickly in the direction expected to be controlled and slowly in the other direction.

When adjusting the valve, make small changes and observe both motion speed and end-of-stroke behavior.

6. Adjusting Cylinder Extension and Retraction Speed

A double-acting cylinder has two movement directions, and each direction may need a different speed setting.

A common arrangement uses one-way flow-control valves at the cylinder ports so that exhaust flow is restricted during the relevant stroke while incoming air can flow relatively freely.

The correct installation depends on the desired control method and the particular valve design.

6.1 Practical Adjustment Procedure

  1. Confirm that the cylinder, load, mounting, and tubing are suitable for the application.
  2. Check that the supply pressure remains adequate during movement.
  3. Verify that the flow-control valves are installed in the correct orientation.
  4. Begin with the manufacturer's recommended settings or a suitably conservative opening.
  5. Operate the cylinder under controlled conditions.
  6. Adjust extension speed in small increments.
  7. Adjust retraction speed separately.
  8. Check for stick-slip motion, unstable movement, excessive noise, and end-of-stroke impact.
  9. Test under representative production load and cycle conditions.
  10. Record the final settings and confirm repeatability.

Do not use a flow-control valve to compensate for a damaged cylinder, insufficient supply capacity, a sticking directional valve, or a mechanical obstruction.

6.2 Why Closing a Flow Control Too Far Causes Problems

Excessive restriction can cause:

  • Very slow movement.
  • Incomplete stroke.
  • Delayed sensor activation.
  • Increased pressure difference across the restriction.
  • Poor repeatability under changing load.
  • Longer machine cycle time.
  • Increased sensitivity to leakage and friction.

Excessive opening can produce rapid motion and hard impact at the end of travel. Speed adjustment must balance cycle time, load stability, and impact energy.

7. Pressure Control and Pressure Drop

Pressure control and flow control are related but distinct.

  • Pressure regulation establishes or limits the pressure supplied to a circuit.
  • Flow control restricts airflow and influences how quickly an actuator moves.
  • Directional control determines the permitted flow path.
  • Pressure monitoring detects whether a required pressure condition is present.

A regulator set to 6 bar does not guarantee that a cylinder receives 6 bar during a high-flow movement. Pressure losses through tubing, fittings, filters, valves, and shared supply lines can reduce the pressure available at the actuator.

7.1 Static Pressure Versus Dynamic Pressure

Static pressure is measured when airflow demand is low or absent. Dynamic pressure is measured while air is flowing under the relevant operating condition.

If a cylinder operates correctly at idle but slows when multiple actuators move simultaneously, check dynamic pressure at the relevant supply and actuator points.

Possible causes include:

  • Insufficient compressor or receiver capacity.
  • Restricted filter or regulator.
  • Undersized distribution pipework.
  • Excessively long tubing.
  • Simultaneous high-flow demand.
  • Restricted valve passages.
  • Leaking equipment elsewhere on the supply.

7.2 Pressure Drop

Pressure drop is the difference between two points in a flowing pneumatic circuit.

\[ \Delta P=P_1-P_2 \]

Where \(P_1\) is the upstream pressure and \(P_2\) is the downstream pressure, measured under comparable operating conditions.

A large pressure drop across a component may indicate excessive flow demand, restriction, contamination, or unsuitable sizing. Interpret the result using the component's flow characteristics and operating conditions.

7.3 Force and Pressure Are Not Interchangeable

Increasing pressure can increase cylinder force, but it may not solve a motion problem caused by restricted exhaust, mechanical binding, or an undersized valve.

Before changing a regulator setting, confirm:

  • The permitted operating pressure of every affected component.
  • The force requirement of the application.
  • Whether pressure is falling during motion.
  • Whether the problem is actually flow-related.
  • Whether higher pressure creates a new mechanical or safety risk.

8. Pneumatic Circuit Sequencing

Sequencing defines the order in which actuators operate. In automated manufacturing, a sequence may coordinate clamps, locating pins, transfer cylinders, grippers, stops, and ejectors.

A simple two-cylinder sequence might be:

  • Cylinder A extends.
  • Confirmation sensor A1 becomes active.
  • Cylinder B extends.
  • Confirmation sensor B1 becomes active.
  • Cylinder B retracts.
  • Confirmation sensor B0 becomes active.
  • Cylinder A retracts.
  • Confirmation sensor A0 becomes active.

This is often written as:

\[ A^+ \rightarrow B^+ \rightarrow B^- \rightarrow A^- \]

Here, \(A^+\) means cylinder A extends, \(A^-\) means it retracts, and the same notation applies to cylinder B.

The notation describes the required movement order, not the electrical output logic or the exact valve configuration.

8.1 Sequence Diagram in Plain Text

Start condition
      ↓
A extends
      ↓
A extended feedback confirmed
      ↓
B extends
      ↓
B extended feedback confirmed
      ↓
B retracts
      ↓
B retracted feedback confirmed
      ↓
A retracts
      ↓
A retracted feedback confirmed
      ↓
Cycle complete

Each transition should be triggered by a defined condition. In a real machine, these conditions may include workpiece presence, clamp confirmation, guarding status, pressure permissives, robot handshake, and timeout supervision.

8.2 Open-Loop Versus Feedback-Based Sequencing

Open-loop sequencing commands movements according to a timer or predetermined logic without confirming the actual actuator position.

Feedback-based sequencing waits for appropriate sensor signals before advancing to the next step.

Feedback-based sequencing is generally more robust because it detects movements that are delayed, blocked, or incomplete. Timers can still be useful for timeout detection, delays, and dwell periods, but a timer alone does not prove that a cylinder reached its target.

9. PLC-Based Pneumatic Sequence Control

A PLC-based system typically separates the machine sequence from the physical valve output mapping.

A simplified control architecture is:

Start request and permissives
             ↓
       PLC sequence state
             ↓
    Output command selection
             ↓
       Valve solenoids
             ↓
       Cylinder movement
             ↓
    Position sensor feedback
             ↓
   Sequence transition checks
             ↓
   Next state or fault handling

9.1 Example State Structure

A sequence can be implemented with named states:

State Command Transition condition
Idle No motion command or defined home-state command Start request and permissives satisfied
A_EXTEND Command A extension A1 sensor confirmed
B_EXTEND Command B extension B1 sensor confirmed
B_RETRACT Command B retraction B0 sensor confirmed
A_RETRACT Command A retraction A0 sensor confirmed
COMPLETE Complete the cycle or wait for the next request Next cycle request or reset condition
FAULT Stop sequence progression and apply defined fault response Fault recovery conditions satisfied

The exact output commands in each state depend on the selected valves and the required machine behavior.

9.2 Essential PLC Logic Features

  • State management: Defines the current sequence step.
  • Transition conditions: Determine when the sequence can advance.
  • Position feedback: Confirms the requested actuator movement.
  • Timeout supervision: Detects a movement that takes too long.
  • Interlocks: Prevent incompatible or premature movements.
  • Fault handling: Defines how the machine responds when the expected condition is not met.
  • Reset and recovery: Establishes how the sequence returns to a known, validated state.
  • Diagnostics: Identifies the state, missing feedback, and failed permissive.

Avoid creating multiple unrelated rungs that can command contradictory outputs to the same valve. Use a consistent command structure with clear ownership of each output.

9.3 Timeout Supervision

Suppose cylinder A is commanded to extend. If the A1 sensor does not activate within the permitted time, the PLC should identify a timeout rather than silently advancing the sequence.

Possible causes include:

  • Low air pressure.
  • A stuck valve.
  • A mechanical obstruction.
  • A failed or misaligned sensor.
  • A disconnected tube.
  • A cylinder that is undersized for the load.

The timeout should be based on validated cycle performance with an appropriate margin. A timeout alarm identifies a failure to meet the expected condition; it does not, by itself, identify the root cause.

10. Pneumatic Sequencing Methods

There are several ways to implement pneumatic sequences.

10.1 PLC-Based Sequencing

The PLC controls solenoid valves and monitors sensor feedback.

Advantages include flexible logic, clear diagnostics, integration with HMI alarms, and straightforward changes to machine sequences when properly engineered.

This approach is common in modern industrial automation.

10.2 Pneumatic Logic Sequencing

Pneumatic logic components, such as pilot-operated valves and pneumatic signal elements, can implement sequence control without a conventional PLC.

Advantages may include independence from electrical control in suitable applications. However, complex logic can become difficult to diagnose, modify, and document.

10.3 Timer-Based Sequencing

A timer advances the sequence after a predetermined interval.

This method is simple, but the actual movement can vary with load, pressure, friction, and temperature. A timer should not be treated as confirmation that the actuator reached its destination.

10.4 Mechanical Sequencing

Mechanical cams, stops, or limit valves trigger the next operation based on physical movement.

This may be suitable for simpler machines, but wear, adjustment changes, and mechanical misalignment can affect repeatability.

10.5 Comparison

Method Main advantage Main limitation
PLC with sensor feedback Flexible logic and strong diagnostics Requires correct programming, I/O, and recovery design
Pneumatic logic Can operate without conventional electrical control Complex sequences can be difficult to maintain
Timer-based Simple implementation Time elapsed does not prove movement completion
Mechanical sequencing Direct physical actuation Wear and mechanical adjustment can affect repeatability

The best method depends on complexity, required reliability, diagnostics, environmental conditions, and the machine's safety requirements.

11. Avoiding Conflicting Actuator Commands

A pneumatic circuit can behave unpredictably if multiple commands are active simultaneously or if the sequence advances before the required movement has completed.

For example, a double-solenoid bistable valve may retain its previous position when both coils are de-energized. A PLC restart can also leave the physical valve state different from the state assumed by the sequence program.

Good control design should address:

  • Mutual exclusion of incompatible commands.
  • Confirmation of actual actuator positions.
  • Detection of contradictory sensor signals.
  • Handling of sensors that remain ON or fail to activate.
  • Output behavior during PLC stop, restart, and communications loss.
  • Recovery from interrupted sequences.
  • Prevention of automatic restart when it could create a hazard.

A pneumatic circuit should be designed with its electrical control behavior in mind. The same valve can behave differently depending on whether it is monostable, bistable, spring-centered, or pilot-operated.

12. Common Pneumatic Circuit Problems

Symptom Possible causes Recommended checks
All cylinders slow down together Supply pressure drop, restricted main filter, excessive simultaneous demand Measure dynamic pressure and inspect shared supply components
One cylinder moves slowly Local flow restriction, incorrect tubing, worn cylinder, excessive load Check its valve, tubing, flow control, load, and cylinder condition
Extension is fast but retraction is slow Incorrect one-way flow-control orientation, rod-side restriction, different load conditions Trace the retraction exhaust path and inspect flow-control settings
Cylinder speed changes between cycles Variable load, pressure fluctuations, intermittent restriction, sticking components Trend pressure and cycle time and inspect mechanical conditions
Sequence stops between steps Missing feedback, failed interlock, timeout, incorrect state transition Identify the current state and the exact unmet transition condition
Two cylinders move together unexpectedly Overlapping output commands, incorrect valve wiring, circuit cross-connection Check PLC logic, output mapping, and pneumatic connections
Cylinder does not complete its stroke Insufficient force, low pressure, excessive restriction, obstruction Check pressure under load, exhaust flow, load, and stroke clearance
Hard impact at the end of travel Excessive speed, incorrect cushioning, excessive moving mass Check speed settings, cushion adjustment, and energy capacity
Several valves operate erratically Shared electrical supply issue, manifold pressure problem, common exhaust restriction Check common power, pressure, manifold, and exhaust paths
Cylinder moves after a restart Retained valve state, unexpected output restoration, trapped air Review startup logic, actual position, and recovery procedures

13. A Systematic Troubleshooting Workflow

Use a repeatable method rather than changing several settings at once.

  1. Define the symptom. Identify the affected actuator, direction, sequence state, and operating conditions.
  2. Check the control command. Verify the PLC state, output request, interlocks, and fault conditions.
  3. Check the electrical output. Confirm the correct output channel, voltage, wiring, and solenoid condition.
  4. Check the supply. Measure pressure at the relevant location during movement.
  5. Trace the airflow. Verify port connections, tube routing, flow-control orientation, and exhaust paths.
  6. Check the actuator. Inspect alignment, load, rod condition, seals, and mechanical obstructions.
  7. Check feedback. Confirm that sensors operate correctly and their PLC input states match reality.
  8. Verify the correction. Repeat the movement under representative conditions and confirm that the fault does not recur.
  9. Document the result. Record the root cause, corrective action, affected components, and verification evidence.

This separates electrical, pneumatic, mechanical, and control-logic failures. Without that separation, a technician can spend an hour adjusting a flow control when the real problem is a sensor that never changes state.

14. Pneumatic Safety in Circuit Design

Pneumatic circuits contain stored energy. Removing the electrical command does not necessarily stop an actuator or remove pressure from its chambers.

Important design and maintenance considerations include:

  • Appropriate isolation and controlled exhaust arrangements.
  • Verification of zero pressure before disconnecting tubing.
  • Mechanical support for gravity-loaded or suspended mechanisms.
  • Protection against unexpected movement when pressure is restored.
  • Assessment of trapped air and retained valve states.
  • Defined restart and recovery behavior.
  • Suitable pressure ratings for tubing, fittings, valves, and cylinders.
  • Risk-based selection and validation of safety-related pneumatic components.

A standard directional valve or flow-control valve should not be treated as a safety-rated device without appropriate evidence and design validation.

Where pneumatic movement presents a personnel hazard, the required safety architecture must be determined from the machine risk assessment. Ordinary PLC sequencing and position sensors do not automatically constitute a safety function.

15. Practical Circuit Design Checklist

Before commissioning or modifying a pneumatic circuit, verify the following.

  • The required movement sequence is documented.
  • Each cylinder's bore, stroke, load, and mounting are suitable.
  • Valve function and normal state match the intended operation.
  • Port numbering and tubing connections are verified.
  • Tubing material, diameter, length, and pressure rating are appropriate.
  • Flow-control valves are correctly oriented.
  • Exhaust paths and silencers are not unnecessarily restrictive.
  • Dynamic pressure is adequate during the most demanding movement.
  • Extension and retraction speeds are adjusted independently where needed.
  • Cushioning is suitable for the moving mass and speed.
  • Position sensors provide the required feedback.
  • PLC transitions depend on appropriate conditions.
  • Movement timeouts and fault responses are implemented.
  • Startup and recovery behavior account for retained valve states.
  • Safety and stored-energy requirements are satisfied.
  • Final settings and circuit changes are documented.

16. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is the difference between directional control and flow control?

Directional control determines the permitted airflow path. Flow control restricts airflow and influences actuator speed.

Q2. Why is meter-out control commonly used in pneumatic cylinders?

Restricting exhaust creates backpressure that helps control motion in many conventional pneumatic applications, particularly where inlet-only restriction may allow an overrunning load to move unstably.

Q3. Why does a cylinder move slowly even when the regulator pressure appears correct?

The pressure may drop during motion because of restricted tubing, fittings, valves, filters, or insufficient supply capacity. Exhaust restriction and mechanical loading can also slow the cylinder.

Q4. What is the difference between static and dynamic pressure?

Static pressure is measured when airflow demand is low or absent. Dynamic pressure is measured while the circuit is operating under flow demand.

Q5. Why is sensor-based sequencing preferable to timer-only sequencing?

Sensor feedback confirms the required position condition. A timer only confirms that an interval has elapsed.

Q6. What does \(A^+ \rightarrow B^+ \rightarrow B^- \rightarrow A^-\) mean?

Cylinder A extends, cylinder B extends, cylinder B retracts, and cylinder A retracts, in that order.

Q7. What happens if a one-way flow-control valve is installed in the wrong orientation?

The restricted direction may be reversed, causing the actuator to move faster in the intended controlled direction and slower in the other direction.

Q8. Why can simultaneous cylinder movements cause pressure problems?

The combined airflow demand can exceed the available capacity of the supply, regulator, distribution pipework, valves, or tubing, causing dynamic pressure to fall.

Q9. Why should a cylinder timeout be implemented?

It detects that the expected position feedback did not arrive within the permitted interval, helping prevent silent sequence progression and supporting fault diagnosis.

Q10. What should be checked before increasing the pressure to solve a slow-cylinder problem?

Check dynamic pressure, restrictions, exhaust flow, load, mechanical binding, and component ratings. Higher pressure is not the correct remedy for every speed problem.

17. Practical Exercises

  1. Trace a real production cylinder circuit from the air-preparation unit through the valve to the cylinder.
  2. Record the tube outer diameter, estimated or specified inner diameter, tube length, fitting type, and relevant component pressure ratings.
  3. Identify whether the cylinder speed is controlled by meter-in or meter-out flow control.
  4. Observe extension and retraction speeds and determine whether separate adjustments are provided.
  5. Measure pressure under load, where suitable test points and approved procedures are available.
  6. Document a two-cylinder sequence and define the sensor condition required for each transition.
  7. Identify the PLC output, solenoid coil, cylinder sensor, timeout, and alarm associated with one movement.
  8. Diagnose a simulated slow-cylinder fault by checking pressure, flow restriction, tubing, exhaust, and mechanical load.
  9. Review a sequence that advances on timers alone and identify where position feedback would improve fault detection.
  10. Create a circuit change record with the original configuration, modification, reason, test results, and approved final settings.

18. Final Mental Model

  • Tubing: Determines how effectively compressed air reaches and leaves the actuator.
  • Directional valve: Selects the airflow path.
  • Flow-control valve: Restricts airflow to regulate movement speed.
  • Pressure regulator: Sets the regulated supply pressure.
  • Dynamic pressure: Reveals supply performance during actual operation.
  • Sequencing: Defines the required order of actuator movements.
  • Position feedback: Confirms movement conditions.
  • Timeouts: Detect movements that fail to complete within the permitted interval.
  • Troubleshooting: Separates control, electrical, pneumatic, mechanical, and feedback problems.
  • Safety: Accounts for stored pressure, unexpected movement, isolation, and recovery.
