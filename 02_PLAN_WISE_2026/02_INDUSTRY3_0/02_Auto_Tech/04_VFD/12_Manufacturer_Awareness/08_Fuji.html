08_Fuji - Fuji

1. Purpose and scope

Fuji Electric is a manufacturer of industrial electrical and automation equipment, including variable-frequency drives, power electronics, and motor-control products.

Its AC drives are commonly associated with the FRENIC product family. These drives are used in applications such as:

  • Industrial conveyors and material-handling systems.
  • Pumps, fans, and blowers.
  • Machine tools and production machinery.
  • Packaging and process equipment.
  • General-purpose motor-speed control.
  • Industrial machinery requiring communication with PLCs.

In automotive manufacturing, Fuji drives may operate conveyors, transfer systems, cooling fans, pumps, and auxiliary production equipment.

The objective of this topic is to recognize Fuji drive families, understand their configuration, connect them to PLC-based control systems, and troubleshoot common operating faults.

The basic VFD principles remain the same across manufacturers. What changes are the model families, parameter codes, software, fault definitions, and supported communication options.

2. Fuji FRENIC drive family awareness

Fuji's FRENIC portfolio contains several families designed for different application requirements. Availability and product lifecycle depend on the specific model and market.

FRENIC-Ace General-purpose industrial applications Machinery, conveyors, pumps, and fans
FRENIC-MEGA Higher-performance industrial applications Demanding machinery and broader control requirements
FRENIC-HVAC HVAC-focused applications Fans, pumps, and building-service equipment
FRENIC-Lift Elevator applications Specialized motion and lift-control requirements

These are broad product-recognition categories. Confirm the exact model code before choosing software, replacement hardware, communication modules, or parameter settings.

2.1 FRENIC-Mini

FRENIC-Mini is a compact drive family intended for smaller general-purpose applications.

Typical uses include:

  • Small conveyors.
  • Compact machinery.
  • Auxiliary pumps and fans.
  • Simple variable-speed applications.

Maintenance priorities include verifying motor data, run-command source, frequency reference, acceleration and deceleration times, and basic protection settings.

2.2 FRENIC-Ace

FRENIC-Ace is a general-purpose industrial drive family.

Depending on the model and configuration, it may support applications involving:

  • Conveyors and material handling.
  • Pumps and fans.
  • Industrial machinery.
  • Multiple command and reference sources.
  • Communication with automation systems.

For maintenance, focus on configuration backup, control-mode selection, I/O assignment, fault history, and PLC integration.

2.3 FRENIC-MEGA

FRENIC-MEGA is associated with more demanding industrial applications and advanced drive-control requirements.

Potential application considerations include:

  • Dynamic load changes.
  • Higher-performance motor control.
  • Current and torque limits.
  • Motor identification and tuning.
  • Communication and feedback requirements, where supported.

Do not assume that every FRENIC-MEGA model supports identical feedback options, communication networks, or safety features. Verify the exact product documentation.

2.4 FRENIC-HVAC

This family focuses on HVAC-related applications.

Typical uses include:

  • Ventilation fans.
  • Air-handling units.
  • Chilled-water pumps.
  • Cooling-water systems.

Maintenance considerations include process feedback, minimum and maximum speed, energy-efficient operation, and integration with building-management or supervisory control systems.

2.5 FRENIC-Lift

FRENIC-Lift is designed for elevator-related applications, which have requirements beyond ordinary conveyor speed control.

These can include specialized motion behavior, stopping performance, and coordination with lift-control equipment.

For maintenance, use the model-specific instructions and approved lift-system procedures. Do not treat a lift drive as an interchangeable general-purpose conveyor drive.

Fuji drives use the same general AC-to-DC-to-AC conversion principle as other industrial VFDs.

The control section receives commands, calculates the required motor output, monitors electrical conditions, and manages protective functions.

Important components include:

  • Rectifier: Converts the incoming AC supply to DC.
  • DC link: Stores and distributes DC energy.
  • Inverter: Produces variable-frequency and variable-voltage output.
  • Control board: Executes the motor-control algorithm and protection logic.
  • Keypad or operator panel: Displays status, faults, and parameter settings.
  • Cooling system: Removes heat from power electronics.
  • Communication interface: Exchanges commands and status with external systems where supported.
  • Optional hardware: May provide fieldbus connectivity, feedback, or braking-related functions.

A drive can be healthy electrically yet fail to run because its command source, enable conditions, or external interlocks are incorrect. Diagnose the complete system, not just the power electronics.

4. Configuration software and operator interface

4.1 Fuji configuration tools

Fuji has provided configuration and commissioning tools such as FRENIC Loader for supported drive families and generations.

The correct software depends on the exact model, firmware, communication interface, and supported tool version.

Depending on the product, configuration tools may provide:

  • Parameter reading and editing.
  • Drive monitoring.
  • Fault and alarm inspection.
  • Parameter backup and restoration.
  • Commissioning support.
  • Configuration management.

Do not assume that one FRENIC Loader version supports every Fuji drive family.

4.2 Local keypad

The local operator interface may allow maintenance personnel to:

  • View output frequency.
  • Monitor output current and other operating values.
  • Inspect fault indications.
  • Read and modify parameters.
  • Perform local operation if enabled.

The exact display functions and navigation differ by model.

4.3 Correct configuration workflow

  1. Record the complete drive model and type code.
  2. Confirm voltage, current, and power ratings.
  3. Identify installed communication or feedback options.
  4. Confirm the compatible software and connection interface.
  5. Read and save the existing parameters.
  6. Record the motor nameplate data.
  7. Verify command and speed-reference sources.
  8. Record fault history before resetting or changing settings.
  9. Document every significant configuration change.

Parameter backups should be clearly identified by machine, drive model, date, and configuration revision.

Important motor data can include:

  • Rated voltage.
  • Rated current.
  • Rated power.
  • Rated frequency.
  • Rated rotational speed.
  • Power factor and other values where required by the selected control mode.

Incorrect motor settings can lead to poor motor control, inaccurate monitoring, unsuitable protection behavior, or unsuccessful tuning.

Use the actual motor nameplate and follow the drive's instructions for the motor connection and voltage class.

6. Motor-control methods

The supported control modes depend on the exact FRENIC model, motor type, and configuration.

6.1 V/f control

V/f control maintains an appropriate relationship between output voltage and frequency over the intended operating range.

\[ \frac{V}{f}\approx\text{constant} \]

This is a simplified model. Actual operation can include low-speed voltage compensation, voltage limits, and other control adjustments.

Typical applications include fans, pumps, and suitable general-purpose conveyors.

Advantages:

  • Relatively simple commissioning.
  • Suitable for many ordinary speed-control applications.
  • Typically does not require encoder feedback.

Limitations:

  • Torque and speed performance may be less precise than suitable vector-control methods.
  • Low-speed behavior depends on the motor and drive configuration.

6.2 Vector control

Supported Fuji drives may provide vector-control modes that improve motor torque and speed behavior compared with basic V/f control in suitable applications.

Potential benefits include:

  • Improved speed regulation under changing load.
  • Better torque response.
  • Improved dynamic behavior.

Accurate motor data is important. Perform motor tuning or identification only where supported and according to the applicable manual.

6.3 Feedback-based control

Some configurations support feedback-based control using compatible hardware or process sensors.

Examples include:

  • Encoder feedback for motor-speed control where supported.
  • Pressure feedback for a pump application.
  • Process feedback through a PLC or an internal controller.

Do not confuse process feedback with motor encoder feedback. They measure different variables and serve different control objectives.

Feature V/f control Vector control Feedback-based control
Main objective General speed control Improved torque and speed behavior Regulation using a measured variable
Feedback hardware Often not required Depends on the selected mode Depends on feedback type
Commissioning effort Usually lower Moderate to higher Application-dependent
Example Fan Conveyor with changing load Pump maintaining pressure

7. PLC integration and communication

Fuji drives can be controlled through hardwired terminals or supported industrial communication interfaces.

Protocol availability depends on the exact drive and installed options. Interfaces such as Modbus RTU, PROFIBUS, PROFINET, or EtherNet/IP must be verified for the specific model and hardware configuration rather than assumed to be universally available.

7.1 Hardwired control

A common architecture is:

PLC digital output ----------> Drive run input
PLC analog output -----------> Drive speed-reference input
Drive ready output ----------> PLC digital input
Drive fault output ----------> PLC digital input

Check terminal assignments, signal compatibility, common wiring, reference scaling, command-source settings, and required external enable signals.

7.2 Network control

A supported communication interface may exchange the following information:

PLC to drive Drive to PLC
Run/stop command Ready status
Direction command Running status
Frequency reference Actual output frequency
Reset request, where permitted Fault status
Application-specific commands Output current or other monitored values

The exact data structure depends on the communication interface and drive configuration.

7.3 Control word and status word

A control word carries commands from the PLC to the drive. A status word reports drive conditions back to the PLC.

For example, the controller may request operation while the drive reports that it is not ready or is faulted.

Always confirm the bit definitions, command sequence, and scaling from the relevant communication documentation.

7.4 Common communication issues

  • Incorrect command source.
  • Incorrect frequency-reference source.
  • Unsupported or missing communication option.
  • Wrong network address or communication settings.
  • Incorrect PLC data mapping.
  • Incorrect frequency-reference scaling.
  • Local mode preventing remote operation.
  • A fault or interlock preventing the run command from being accepted.
  • Network timeout or lost communication.

Diagnostic sequence: Check physical connection, network health, PLC data, command acceptance, drive status, and motor operation as separate stages.

8. Fuji drive faults and diagnostics

Fault names and codes vary across FRENIC families. Always use the fault table for the exact model.

Fault category Possible causes First checks

Overvoltage Regenerative energy, rapid deceleration, supply conditions Check stopping ramp, inertia, and braking arrangements
Undervoltage Supply dip, loss of phase, loose connection Check incoming supply and connections

8.1 Diagnose according to fault timing

  • At power-up: Check the displayed fault, supply, wiring, and configuration.
  • At starting: Check the command source, enable conditions, interlocks, motor circuit, and mechanical load.
  • During acceleration: Check ramp time, current demand, motor data, and mechanical resistance.
  • During steady operation: Check load, cooling, supply quality, and motor condition.
  • During deceleration: Check regenerative energy, stopping settings, and braking capability.
  • After a PLC change: Check network configuration, data mapping, command source, and reference source.

8.2 Correct troubleshooting sequence

  1. Record the exact fault code and display text.
  2. Record what the machine was doing when the fault occurred.
  3. Inspect available fault history and operating values.
  4. Identify the likely electrical, mechanical, configuration, or communication cause.
  5. Correct the underlying problem.
  6. Reset only when safe and appropriate.
  7. Test operation and monitor for recurrence.

A fault code narrows the investigation. It does not automatically identify the failed component.

9. Braking and regenerative energy

During deceleration, a motor and high-inertia load may return energy to the drive's DC bus. If the drive cannot dissipate or return that energy sufficiently, DC-bus voltage may rise and cause an overvoltage fault.

Potential corrective measures include:

  • Increasing deceleration time.
  • Reviewing machine inertia and stopping requirements.
  • Checking whether the drive supports the required braking hardware.
  • Verifying braking resistor ratings and configuration where applicable.
  • Considering an appropriate regenerative solution for demanding applications.
  • Checking supply conditions and the timing of the fault.

Do not assume every Fuji drive includes an internal braking chopper or supports the same external braking equipment.

Practical example

A transfer conveyor operates normally but trips when commanded to stop rapidly.

Investigate the fault code, deceleration time, load inertia, braking arrangement, and supply conditions. If a controlled change to the stopping ramp is appropriate, verify that it does not compromise the machine's stopping time or safety requirements.

10. Parameter backup and restoration

A valid backup reduces downtime during drive replacement and supports configuration management.

10.1 Before replacement

Record:

  • Full drive model and type code.
  • Input voltage, output current, and power rating.
  • Motor nameplate data.
  • Control mode and tuning settings.
  • Run-command source.
  • Frequency-reference source.
  • Acceleration and deceleration times.
  • Frequency and current limits.
  • Digital and analog I/O assignments.
  • Communication protocol and network settings.
  • Installed options.
  • Fault history.
  • Parameter backup and its location.

10.2 Replacement procedure

  1. Verify the replacement drive's electrical and application suitability.
  2. Confirm compatibility of model, firmware, options, and software.
  3. Restore only parameters valid for the target drive.
  4. Compare critical settings with the original record.
  5. Verify motor data, control sources, I/O, and communication.
  6. Validate interlocks and required safety functions.
  7. Conduct a controlled functional test.
  8. Save the final configuration and update the maintenance record.

Do not assume that a backup from FRENIC-Mini can be loaded unchanged into FRENIC-MEGA or FRENIC-Ace. Compatibility depends on the specific products and software.

11. Safety precautions

Before working on a Fuji drive:

  • Apply the site's lockout/tagout procedure.
  • Isolate all relevant power sources.
  • Wait for the discharge time specified in the exact product manual.
  • Verify the absence of hazardous voltage using an appropriate instrument.
  • Treat the DC bus as hazardous until verified safe.
  • Isolate the drive electronics before insulation-resistance testing, following the manufacturer's procedure.
  • Do not bypass interlocks or protective functions to force operation.
  • Do not treat Safe Torque Off (STO), where provided, as electrical isolation.

STO prevents torque production according to its specified safety function. It does not necessarily remove hazardous voltage from the drive or motor terminals.

Safety capabilities and ratings must be confirmed for the installed drive and complete machine.

12. Practical automotive maintenance cases

Case 1: Conveyor fails to start

Symptoms: The PLC program indicates RUN, but the motor remains stopped.

Check:

  1. Active drive fault.
  2. Drive ready and enabled status.
  3. Configured run-command source.
  4. Actual command reaching the drive.
  5. External enable and interlock conditions.
  6. Local/remote selection.
  7. Network communication, if used.
  8. Motor circuit and mechanical load.

Case 2: Motor speed differs from the PLC request

Symptoms: The PLC requests 40 Hz, but the drive output frequency is lower.

Check:

  • Selected frequency-reference source.
  • PLC output value.
  • Analog input scaling or network data mapping.
  • Minimum and maximum frequency limits.
  • Process-control functions that may influence the reference.
  • Drive operating status.

Separate the requested frequency, actual output frequency, and actual motor RPM. These values are related but not identical.

Case 3: Overcurrent during acceleration

Symptoms: A conveyor starts to accelerate and then trips.

Possible causes include:

  • Excessively short acceleration time.
  • Jammed conveyor or excessive mechanical load.
  • Incorrect motor data.
  • Motor or cable problems.
  • Unsuitable control-mode settings.
  • Current limits or other operating constraints.

First determine whether the fault is caused by electrical conditions, mechanical resistance, or configuration. Do not increase limits simply to suppress the fault.

Case 4: Communication fault after drive replacement

Symptoms: The replacement drive powers up, but the PLC cannot control it.

Check the installed communication option, supported protocol, network configuration, PLC hardware configuration, data mapping, and command/reference sources.

A drive that powers up successfully is not necessarily ready for remote operation.

Case 5: Fan overheats or airflow is low

Symptoms: A fan runs, but the process does not achieve the required airflow or the drive reports an overtemperature condition.

Investigate separately:

  • Drive cooling and ambient temperature.
  • Motor and fan mechanical condition.
  • Speed reference and frequency limits.
  • Airflow restrictions.
  • Control feedback, if used.
  • Actual motor current and operating load.

Low airflow and drive overtemperature can coexist, but one does not prove the other caused it.

14. Interview questions and answers

Q1. What is Fuji Electric's main VFD product family?

The FRENIC family, which includes products designed for compact machinery, general-purpose industrial applications, HVAC, and specialized applications.

Q2. What is the difference between FRENIC-Mini and FRENIC-MEGA?

They target different application and performance requirements. FRENIC-Mini is associated with compact general-purpose applications, while FRENIC-MEGA is associated with more demanding industrial applications. Verify exact capabilities against the specific models.

Q3. Which software is used for Fuji drives?

FRENIC Loader is a configuration tool for supported Fuji drive families and generations. The correct version depends on the model and firmware.

Q4. Why might a drive show ready status but fail to start?

The command source may be incorrect, the run command may not be reaching the drive, an interlock may be unsatisfied, or local/remote selection may prevent remote operation.

Q5. Why can overcurrent occur during acceleration?

Possible causes include excessive acceleration demand, a jammed or overloaded machine, motor or cable problems, incorrect motor data, or unsuitable control settings.

Q6. Why can overvoltage occur during deceleration?

The motor/load can regenerate energy into the DC bus. If the drive cannot dissipate or return that energy adequately, DC-bus voltage can rise.

Q7. What is the purpose of vector control?

Vector control uses a more sophisticated motor-control method to improve torque and speed behavior compared with basic V/f control in suitable applications.

Q8. What is the difference between command source and frequency-reference source?

The command source determines where start/stop instructions originate. The frequency-reference source determines where the requested motor speed comes from.

Q9. What should be recorded before replacing a Fuji drive?

Model and rating, motor data, control mode, command/reference sources, ramps, limits, I/O assignments, communication settings, options, fault history, and parameter backup.

Q10. Why should fault history be captured before resetting?

It preserves evidence about the failure and its timing, helping distinguish electrical, mechanical, configuration, and communication problems.

Q11. Can parameters be transferred between FRENIC families without verification?

No. Parameter structures, functions, and supported options may differ. Compatibility must be checked for the exact source and target products.

Q12. Is STO equivalent to isolating the drive's power supply?

No. STO provides a specified torque-prevention function but does not replace electrical isolation and lockout/tagout.

Prepare a diagnostic sequence that verifies the command source, actual run signal, interlocks, local/remote mode, and drive status.

The PLC requests 40 Hz, but the drive produces 25 Hz.

List the checks for reference selection, signal scaling, network data mapping, frequency limits, and any active process-control function.

Exercise 3: Overcurrent during acceleration

A conveyor trips when accelerating from standstill.

Separate the possible causes into electrical, mechanical, configuration, and motor-data categories. Identify the evidence required before changing parameters.

A drive begins tripping after the motor cable is replaced.

Prepare a safe diagnostic plan covering wiring, insulation, grounding, motor condition, and the exact fault timing.

16. Final mental model

When troubleshooting a Fuji FRENIC drive:

  1. Identify the complete model, rating, firmware, and installed options.
  2. Consult the correct manual and compatible configuration software.
  3. Verify motor data, control mode, command source, reference source, ramps, and limits.
  4. Separate supply, drive, motor/cable, mechanical, configuration, and communication problems.
  5. Record the fault code and operating conditions before resetting.
  6. Correct the root cause rather than repeatedly clearing the fault.
  7. Validate machine operation, interlocks, stopping behavior, and required safety functions.
  8. Document the final configuration and preserve the backup.

The goal is not to memorize every Fuji parameter number. It is to recognize the product family, locate the correct documentation, understand the control architecture, and diagnose faults methodically.
