1. Definition

Speed control is a motor-control method in which the drive regulates motor speed to follow a specified speed reference.

Torque control is a motor-control method in which the drive regulates the motor's electromagnetic torque to follow a specified torque reference, within the limits of the motor, drive, and control system.

Both modes are available on many industrial VFDs, particularly drives with vector-control capabilities. Availability and implementation depend on the drive model and selected control mode.

The main difference is what the drive is primarily commanded to regulate:

  • Speed control: “Maintain the requested speed.”
  • Torque control: “Produce the requested torque.”

These are not merely two names for the same function. They describe different control objectives.

2. Speed control

In speed-control mode, the drive receives a speed reference and adjusts motor torque as needed to maintain the requested speed.

For example, an automotive assembly-line conveyor may be commanded to run at 30 Hz equivalent motor frequency or at a specified speed in RPM. If the load increases, the drive increases motor torque, within its capabilities, to maintain the target speed.

Example

Suppose a conveyor is commanded to run at 1,000 RPM.

Operating condition Requested speed Actual speed Drive response
Conveyor empty 1,000 RPM 1,000 RPM Supplies torque needed for light load

Conveyor mechanically jams 1,000 RPM Falls significantly May reach current/torque limits or trip

The drive does not create unlimited torque. If the required torque exceeds its available capability, speed regulation can fail or a protection function can trip.

3. Torque control

In torque-control mode, the drive primarily regulates the requested motor torque rather than forcing the motor to maintain a particular speed.

The actual speed is determined by the balance between motor torque, load torque, inertia, and friction.

For example, a winding machine may need controlled tension. The drive adjusts torque to maintain the required winding force or tension, while the resulting speed changes as the roll diameter changes.

Torque control is not a speed-holding mode. If the application requires both precise speed and precise tension, the system may use a coordinated control architecture rather than a single fixed torque command.

4. Speed control versus torque control

Feature Speed control Torque control
Primary reference Speed Torque
Main objective Maintain target speed Maintain target torque
Response to increased load Increases torque to preserve speed Maintains commanded torque, so speed may change
Speed behavior Regulated around the reference within capability Determined by the torque balance and load
Typical application Conveyors, fans, pumps, many machine axes Winders, tension control, load-sharing drives
Important limit Available torque/current Available torque/current and permissible speed
Feedback Speed measured or estimated, depending on mode Torque commonly controlled through current and the motor model; speed feedback may also be used
Main risk if poorly configured Speed loss, excessive torque/current, or trip Unexpected acceleration, overspeed, or excessive force

Important: Both modes can have current limits, torque limits, speed limits, ramps, and protective functions. Selecting torque mode does not disable these protections automatically.

5. Relationship between speed, torque, and power

Mechanical power is related to torque and angular speed:

\[ P = T\omega \]

Where:

  • \(P\) = mechanical power in watts.
  • \(T\) = torque in newton-metres (N·m).
  • \(\omega\) = angular speed in radians per second (rad/s).

When torque is in N·m and speed is in RPM, a convenient formula is:

\[ P(\text{kW}) = \frac{T(\text{N·m}) \times n(\text{RPM})}{9550} \]

Example

A motor produces 20 N·m at 1,000 RPM.

\[ P = \frac{20 \times 1000}{9550} \approx 2.09\text{ kW} \]

This is the mechanical power at that operating point, ignoring losses between electrical input and shaft output.

The equation also explains why a motor's available torque and power vary with speed. The drive, motor, cooling arrangement, and operating region determine the permissible torque-speed envelope.

6. How torque is controlled in a VFD

In many vector-controlled drives, torque is controlled primarily by regulating the torque-producing current component.

For a field-oriented induction motor control system, torque is related approximately to rotor flux and the q-axis current:

\[ T_e \propto \psi_r i_q \]

This is why accurate motor parameters and appropriate identification can matter for torque control.

Is torque directly measured?

Usually, the drive does not measure shaft torque directly with a torque transducer. It commonly estimates or controls torque using motor current and its motor model.

Therefore, displayed torque may be an estimate or a normalized value, not a direct mechanical torque measurement. A calibrated torque transducer is needed when direct measurement is required.

7. Open-loop and closed-loop speed control

The terms open-loop and closed-loop describe whether the control system uses feedback to correct the controlled variable. They are related to, but not identical to, the distinction between V/f and vector control.

Open-loop speed operation

The drive receives a speed-related command but does not use direct shaft-speed feedback to correct the actual speed.

For example, a V/f drive may set output frequency according to a reference. Actual motor speed differs from synchronous speed because of slip and load.

Closed-loop speed operation

The controller compares the speed reference with measured or estimated actual speed and corrects the output to reduce the error.

Encoder-based control provides physical shaft-speed feedback. Some sensorless vector drives estimate speed and use the estimate in their control loop.

Term Meaning
Open-loop No feedback of the controlled output is used for correction
Closed-loop Feedback is used to correct the controlled output
Sensorless Speed or flux is estimated without a physical shaft sensor
Encoder-based Shaft motion is measured using an encoder or supported feedback device

Avoid treating these terms as interchangeable. A sensorless vector drive may still use closed-loop control based on estimated speed.

8. Common torque and speed limits

Drive limits define the operating boundaries. Their exact implementation varies by manufacturer.

Speed limits

  • Minimum speed.
  • Maximum speed.
  • Forward and reverse speed limits.
  • Overspeed limits, where supported.

Torque and current limits

  • Maximum motoring torque.
  • Maximum regenerative or braking torque, where supported.
  • Current limit.
  • Torque-reference limit.
  • Speed-dependent torque restrictions.

Ramp settings

  • Acceleration time.
  • Deceleration time.
  • Torque-reference ramp, where available.
  • Speed-reference ramp.

A speed ramp controls how quickly the speed command changes. A torque ramp controls how quickly the torque reference changes. These can have very different effects on the machine.

Example: A conveyor that accelerates too quickly may produce mechanical shock even if its final speed is correct. Reducing the speed ramp may solve the issue without changing the control mode.

Application Common control choice Reason

Cooling fan Speed control Regulate airflow
Centrifugal pump Speed control Regulate flow or pressure through a suitable control system
Winding/unwinding machine Torque control or coordinated speed-torque control Maintain web tension as roll diameter changes
Two motors driving a shared load Coordinated speed or load-sharing control Prevent one motor from carrying disproportionate load
Mixer Speed control, possibly with torque monitoring Maintain speed while monitoring process load
Hoist or vertical axis Engineered speed/torque control with brake and safety design Control motion while safely managing the suspended load

A machine may combine modes. For example, one drive may operate as the speed master while another uses a torque reference to share the load.

10. Torque control in winding and tension applications

Winding systems illustrate why torque control is useful.

The relationship between winding torque, web tension, and roll radius is approximately:

\[ T = F r \]

Where:

  • \(T\) = required shaft torque.
  • \(F\) = tangential web tension.
  • \(r\) = effective roll radius.

If tension must remain constant while the roll radius changes, the required torque changes.

For example, with a web tension of 100 N:

Roll radius Required ideal torque
0.10 m 10 N·m
0.20 m 20 N·m
0.30 m 30 N·m

This is an idealized calculation that excludes friction, inertia, transmission losses, and dynamic effects.

A practical winding system may therefore use a tension sensor, roll-diameter estimation, torque compensation, and coordinated speed control. Simply setting a fixed torque reference may not maintain constant tension as the roll diameter changes.

11. Practical setup and commissioning

Before choosing speed or torque mode:

  1. Identify the application and determine whether speed, torque, tension, or load sharing is the actual control objective.
  2. Verify motor and drive ratings, wiring, motor parameters, and the selected control mode.
  3. Confirm the source and scaling of the reference, including whether the reference is expressed in RPM, frequency, percentage, or engineering units.
  4. Configure current, torque, and speed limits according to the equipment design and manufacturer instructions.
  5. Set appropriate acceleration, deceleration, and reference ramps.
  6. Verify the required feedback or estimation method.
  7. Test the machine under controlled conditions, starting with approved low-risk operating conditions.
  8. Monitor commanded speed, actual or estimated speed, current, torque indication, active limits, and fault history.
  9. Verify the response to representative load changes.
  10. Save the approved parameter set and document the final control mode and test results.

For torque-control applications, specifically verify the behavior at startup, at low speed, when the load is removed, and when the reference or feedback signal is lost. The response must be appropriate for the machine's hazards.

Symptom Possible cause Checks
Speed drops under load Torque/current limit, overload, poor tuning, incorrect motor data Check load, actual speed, current, torque limit, and drive status
Speed oscillates Poor tuning, unstable reference, feedback noise, interacting controllers Trend reference and actual speed; inspect feedback and control settings
Motor accelerates unexpectedly in torque mode Load torque is too low for the commanded torque, reference error, inadequate speed limiting Check torque-reference polarity and scaling, speed limits, and machine response
Motor cannot produce commanded torque Current limit, undersized motor/drive, incorrect parameters, inadequate supply or operating conditions Review current, torque estimate, ratings, and limits
Torque display does not match measured shaft torque Display is an estimate, incorrect motor model, scaling differences, mechanical losses Confirm the manufacturer's definition and compare with calibrated measurement
Drive trips during acceleration Short ramp, excessive inertia, jam, current limit, drive sizing Review fault history, mechanics, ramp, and current trend
Two drives sharing a load fight each other Incorrect master/follower arrangement, reference mismatch, incompatible control strategy Check the control architecture and load-sharing settings
Winder tension changes with roll diameter Fixed torque reference, missing diameter compensation, sensor scaling issue Verify tension feedback, radius compensation, and torque reference

Example: conveyor speed drops when the fixture arrives

A drive in speed-control mode should increase torque to recover the requested speed. If the speed remains low:

  • Check whether the drive is at its current or torque limit.
  • Verify motor nameplate data and vector identification.
  • Inspect the conveyor for binding or excessive load.
  • Confirm the speed reference and actual-speed feedback.
  • Verify the motor and drive ratings.

If the drive is in torque-control mode, the same speed drop may be an expected result of the torque reference and load balance rather than a speed-control fault. First establish which mode is active. Otherwise, troubleshooting becomes a highly elaborate way to inspect the wrong problem.

13. Safety considerations

  • Torque control can produce force even when the speed command is low.
  • A positive torque command can accelerate a lightly loaded machine.
  • Speed and torque limits must be configured and validated for the application.
  • Loss of a reference, feedback signal, or communication link must have a defined and safe response.
  • Torque control alone does not safely hold a suspended load.
  • STO is not a mechanical brake.
  • Do not disable interlocks or safety functions to test control behavior.

The required safe response depends on the machinery risk assessment, drive capabilities, and applicable safety architecture.

14. Interview questions and answers

Q1. What is the difference between speed control and torque control?

Speed control regulates motor speed around a reference by adjusting torque. Torque control regulates the requested torque, allowing speed to vary with the load.

Q2. Does torque control maintain constant speed?

Not by itself. Speed depends on the balance between motor torque and load torque. A separate speed-control loop or coordinated control strategy may be required.

Q3. How does a VFD control motor torque?

In vector-control systems, the drive commonly regulates torque-producing current using a motor model and current feedback.

Q4. Is torque directly measured by a standard VFD?

Usually not. The drive commonly estimates torque from electrical quantities and the motor model. Direct measurement requires a suitable torque sensor.

Q5. Why is speed feedback useful?

It allows the controller to compare the speed reference with actual or estimated speed and correct the error.

Q6. Why is torque control useful in a winding machine?

It allows the drive to regulate winding torque, which can help control web tension when combined with suitable radius compensation and other control functions.

Q7. What happens if the load becomes too heavy in speed-control mode?

The drive increases torque within its limits. If the load exceeds available capability, speed may fall and the drive may reach a limit or trip.

Q8. What happens if the load is suddenly removed in torque-control mode?

The motor may accelerate because the commanded torque is no longer balanced by the load. Appropriate speed limits, supervisory logic, and safe machine design are essential.

Q9. Can two motors share a load using torque control?

Yes, with a properly designed load-sharing strategy. The drives must be coordinated to prevent unequal loading or conflicting control actions.

Q10. Is speed control always better for conveyors?

Not universally, but speed control is common because conveyors generally need to maintain a target line speed. The choice depends on the mechanical arrangement and process requirements.

16. Final mental model

  • Speed control: The drive tries to maintain a requested speed and adjusts torque accordingly.
  • Torque control: The drive tries to maintain a requested torque, while speed depends on the load and system dynamics.
  • Vector control: Provides a common foundation for precise torque regulation in many AC drive applications.
  • Feedback: Helps the system regulate speed or other measured variables, depending on the control architecture.
  • Limits and safety: Define what the motor and machine are allowed to do.

The key commissioning question is: What must the machine control directly, and what is allowed to change as the load changes? That determines whether speed control, torque control, or a coordinated combination is appropriate.
