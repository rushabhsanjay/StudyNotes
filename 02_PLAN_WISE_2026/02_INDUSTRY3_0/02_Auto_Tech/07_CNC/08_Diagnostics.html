08_Diagnostics - Diagnostics

1. Definition of CNC diagnostics

CNC diagnostics is the systematic process of identifying, isolating and verifying faults in a CNC machine by examining alarms, status signals, feedback data, control logic and physical equipment.

A CNC machine combines multiple interacting systems:

  • CNC controller and program execution.
  • PLC logic and machine sequencing.
  • Servo drives and motors.
  • Spindle drive and spindle motor.
  • Position and speed feedback devices.
  • Sensors, switches and actuators.
  • Electrical power and control circuits.
  • Mechanical transmission and machine structure.
  • Pneumatic and hydraulic systems.
  • Tool management, work offsets and machine parameters.
  • Communication networks and connected equipment.

A fault in one system can produce an alarm in another. For example, a CNC may report an axis positioning error because the servo drive detected a following error caused by mechanical resistance.

The alarm identifies a detected condition. It does not necessarily identify the root cause.

Objectives of CNC diagnostics

  1. Identify the affected subsystem.
  2. Determine the exact operating condition when the fault occurred.
  3. Separate electrical, control, feedback, drive, mechanical and process causes.
  4. Locate the failed component or incorrect condition.
  5. Restore operation through an approved corrective action.
  6. Verify normal operation and document the root cause.

The objective is not merely to clear the alarm. It is to understand why it occurred and prevent an avoidable repeat.

Diagnostic principle

Trace a failed operation through the complete chain:

Command → control decision → output → physical response → feedback → completion status.

For an axis that does not move, for example, the fault could occur at any point:

  • The CNC never generated the movement command.
  • The PLC prevented motion.
  • The drive was not enabled.
  • The motor did not produce torque.
  • A mechanical obstruction prevented movement.
  • The encoder feedback was invalid.
  • The axis moved but the controller did not recognize the expected feedback.

Each possibility requires different evidence.

The tool must match the fault and the machine's electrical and safety requirements.

For example, a multimeter can verify certain electrical conditions, but it cannot independently establish that a servo loop is stable or that an encoder's complete signal path is correct.

4. Understanding CNC alarms

An alarm is a message generated when the controller or another subsystem detects a condition requiring attention.

Common alarm sources include:

  • CNC program or syntax errors.
  • Axis positioning and servo faults.
  • Spindle faults.
  • Reference-return errors.
  • Overtravel conditions.
  • PLC interlock failures.
  • Tool-change timeouts.
  • Communication faults.
  • Feedback or encoder faults.
  • Lubrication, pressure or temperature monitoring.
  • Safety-system status.

Alarm versus root cause

Suppose the CNC reports an axis following error.

Possible causes include:

  • Excessive mechanical load.
  • Servo drive fault.
  • Motor or feedback problem.
  • Incorrect drive configuration.
  • Mechanical binding.
  • Damaged coupling or transmission.
  • An abrupt command or inappropriate motion setting.

Replacing the motor immediately would be premature. The alarm describes a discrepancy between commanded and actual motion, not a verdict on which component failed.

Alarm investigation procedure

  1. Record the exact alarm number and text.
  2. Identify the affected axis, spindle or machine function.
  3. Record the machine mode and operation in progress.
  4. Check preceding alarms and relevant event history.
  5. Review drive and PLC diagnostics for related faults.
  6. Determine the first failed condition in the operating sequence.
  7. Confirm the cause before changing parameters or replacing parts.

The first alarm is not always the root cause, and the last alarm is not necessarily the most important. Alarm chronology matters.

5. CNC diagnostic screens and status information

CNC controls commonly provide screens for observing internal states and subsystem information.

Depending on the controller, these may include:

  • Axis command and actual position.
  • Following error.
  • Servo readiness and alarm status.
  • Spindle command and actual speed.
  • Reference-return status.
  • Active G-code modes.
  • Work coordinate system.
  • Active tool and compensation registers.
  • PLC signals and sequence states.
  • Machine interlocks.
  • Communication status.

Example: Axis commanded position versus actual position

Suppose an axis is commanded to position X = 100.000 mm.

The controller may show:

  • Commanded position: 100.000 mm.
  • Actual position: 98.500 mm.
  • Following error: 1.500 mm, subject to the controller's sign convention and calculation.

This indicates a discrepancy between commanded and measured position. It does not independently identify the reason.

Investigate drive status, movement conditions, load, feedback integrity and the controller's configured error monitoring.

Important distinction

Displayed position may represent a machine coordinate, work coordinate, interpolated position, encoder-derived position or another controller-specific quantity.

Confirm what the screen displays before comparing it with an external measurement.

6. Servo and axis diagnostics

Axis-related faults are common in CNC troubleshooting because position control depends on several interacting systems.

Typical axis faults

  • Servo drive alarm.
  • Motor overload.
  • Following error.
  • Encoder or feedback fault.
  • Reference-return failure.
  • Software overtravel.
  • Physical limit-switch activation.
  • Excessive vibration.
  • Positioning inaccuracy.
  • Axis not ready.
  • Communication failure between controller and drive.

Basic diagnostic sequence

Axis fails to move correctly

Example: Axis will not move

Check the following categories:

  1. Command: Was the motion command accepted?
  2. Mode: Is the machine in a mode that permits the requested operation?
  3. Interlocks: Is a PLC or safety condition preventing movement?
  4. Drive: Is the drive ready and enabled?
  5. Feedback: Is the position signal valid?
  6. Mechanics: Is there a binding condition, obstruction or damaged transmission?
  7. Configuration: Is the axis configured correctly, and does the evidence support a configuration problem?

Do not repeatedly command an axis that is stalled or making abnormal noise. Further movement can damage the drive, motor, coupling or machine mechanics.

7. Spindle diagnostics

The spindle system includes the CNC speed or orientation request, drive control, motor, transmission and feedback devices.

Common symptoms

  • Spindle does not start.
  • Spindle does not reach commanded speed.
  • Spindle rotates in the wrong direction.
  • Spindle stops unexpectedly.
  • Spindle orientation fails.
  • Excessive vibration or noise.
  • Spindle-drive alarm.
  • Speed feedback is missing or inconsistent.

Diagnostic layers

Layer What to verify
CNC program Correct spindle command and programmed speed
Machine logic Required permissives and interlocks
Drive Ready state, enable, fault and speed reference
Motor and transmission Physical condition, load and mechanical connection
Feedback Speed or position feedback where applicable
Monitoring Spindle-at-speed or orientation-complete status

Example: M03 is commanded but the spindle does not rotate

A useful diagnostic sequence is:

  1. Verify the programmed spindle command and speed.
  2. Check whether the CNC accepted the command.
  3. Inspect relevant machine interlocks.
  4. Check the spindle drive's ready and fault status.
  5. Check the drive enable and speed reference.
  6. Verify the physical motor and transmission condition through the authorized procedure.
  7. Examine feedback and alarms.

Do not increase the speed command or alter drive limits to force operation without establishing the cause.

8. CNC-to-PLC and machine-function diagnostics

Many machine functions are executed through PLC logic, including:

  • Automatic tool change.
  • Coolant control.
  • Chuck clamping and unclamping.
  • Pallet changing.
  • Lubrication.
  • Chip conveyor operation.
  • Guard and door status.
  • Machine-specific auxiliary functions.

An M-code may request a function while the PLC waits for a permissive or sensor signal.

Typical causes

  • Spindle not stopped.
  • Spindle orientation not confirmed.
  • Axis not at the required position.
  • Pneumatic pressure insufficient.
  • Tool clamp state incorrect.
  • Magazine position not confirmed.
  • Sensor failed or is misaligned.
  • PLC sequence fault.
  • Completion handshake not received.

Monitor the actual sequence state and signals rather than assuming that the M-code itself is defective.

9. Feedback and sensor diagnostics

Feedback allows the control system to determine whether an expected physical condition exists.

Common devices include:

  • Incremental encoders.
  • Absolute encoders.
  • Proximity switches.
  • Limit switches.
  • Photoelectric sensors.
  • Pressure switches.
  • Flow switches.
  • Temperature sensors.
  • Clamp-position sensors.
  • Tool-presence sensors.

Commanded state versus actual state

Suppose a PLC commands a pneumatic clamp to close.

The output may energize the solenoid valve, but the clamp may fail to close because of low air pressure, a blocked valve or mechanical obstruction.

If the clamp-position sensor remains OFF, the PLC may time out and prevent the next operation.

The diagnostic sequence should compare:

  1. PLC output command.
  2. Actual electrical output.
  3. Valve or actuator response.
  4. Physical movement.
  5. Sensor state.
  6. PLC sequence status.
  7. Timeout or alarm condition.

Sensor troubleshooting

Investigate:

  • Sensor supply.
  • Wiring and connector condition.
  • Input-channel status.
  • Sensor alignment and operating distance.
  • Target condition.
  • Electrical noise or intermittent connection.
  • Correct sensor type and switching behaviour.
  • PLC logic and signal interpretation.

A sensor LED being ON is useful evidence, but it does not prove that the corresponding PLC input is correctly received or interpreted.

10. CNC diagnostics for program and coordinate problems

Not every CNC fault is a hardware fault. Program logic, coordinate selection and compensation can create incorrect machining results.

Common problems include:

  • Incorrect G-code sequence.
  • Wrong work coordinate system.
  • Wrong tool or offset register.
  • Incorrect tool length compensation.
  • Incorrect cutter radius compensation.
  • Wrong units.
  • Incorrect absolute/incremental mode.
  • Incorrect plane selection.
  • Unsupported command or option.
  • Invalid compensation approach geometry.

Example: Machined part is shifted

Investigate:

  • Active work coordinate system.
  • Work offset values.
  • Physical fixture position.
  • Program coordinates.
  • Probing or datum measurements.

Do not change servo parameters to compensate for a work-offset error.

Example: Hole depth is incorrect

Investigate:

  • Programmed Z coordinate.
  • Active work offset.
  • Tool length offset.
  • Physical tool measurement.
  • Tool selection and compensation activation.
  • Workpiece datum.

Separate coordinate errors from physical motion errors before changing machine configuration.

11. Electrical and power diagnostics

CNC machines may experience faults originating in power distribution or control circuits.

Examples include:

  • Missing or unstable supply.
  • Blown fuse.
  • Tripped circuit breaker.
  • Contactor failure.
  • Loose connection.
  • Control-power loss.
  • Overtemperature.
  • Cooling fan failure.
  • Electrical noise.
  • Damaged cable.
  • Poor protective bonding or grounding.
  • Drive power-stage fault.

Diagnostic principles

  • Review the alarm and electrical drawings.
  • Check the relevant supply and protective devices.
  • Inspect connectors and wiring for damage or looseness.
  • Verify control power and device status.
  • Use correctly rated test equipment and approved measurement procedures.
  • Compare the measured condition against the manufacturer's specification.

Electrical measurements inside CNC cabinets and drive systems can expose personnel to hazardous voltage and stored energy. Only appropriately qualified personnel should perform them using the required protective measures.

Do not assume that a dark display or an unresponsive drive proves the machine is electrically isolated.

12. Pneumatic and hydraulic diagnostics

Many CNC machines depend on compressed air or hydraulic pressure for workholding, tool clamping, tool changing and other functions.

Common symptoms

  • Tool will not release.
  • Chuck does not clamp.
  • Tailstock fails to move.
  • ATC sequence times out.
  • Clamp pressure is insufficient.
  • Pressure switch remains inactive.
  • Actuator moves slowly or inconsistently.

Diagnostic chain

Supply pressure
      |
      v
Regulator / filter / valve
      |
      v
Solenoid command
      |
      v
Actuator movement
      |
      v
Position or pressure feedback
      |
      v
PLC confirms required state

Possible causes include low supply pressure, leaks, clogged filters, a faulty valve, damaged seals, a mechanical obstruction or incorrect feedback.

Verify pressure under the specified operating conditions. A pressure gauge showing acceptable static pressure does not always prove adequate pressure during actuator movement.

Before intervention, isolate the relevant energy source and safely release stored pneumatic or hydraulic energy according to the approved procedure.

13. Communication and network diagnostics

Modern CNC machines may use communication links between:

  • CNC and servo drives.
  • CNC and PLC.
  • Distributed I/O modules.
  • Remote operator panels.
  • Machine-tool measurement systems.
  • Industrial Ethernet devices.
  • Supervisory or production-monitoring systems.

Typical symptoms

  • Drive communication alarm.
  • Missing I/O data.
  • Intermittent axis readiness.
  • HMI data not updating.
  • PLC status inconsistent with physical equipment.
  • Network node unavailable.
  • Communication recovery after restart.

Diagnostic approach

  1. Identify which two devices are expected to communicate.
  2. Record the exact alarm and affected connection.
  3. Check device power and status indicators.
  4. Verify cable and connector condition.
  5. Review network topology and device diagnostics.
  6. Check configuration, addressing and supported communication settings.
  7. Determine whether the fault is local, intermittent or network-wide.
  8. Follow the manufacturer's recovery procedure.

Avoid changing network addresses or replacing communication hardware without evidence. An intermittent power supply or damaged cable can mimic a configuration problem.

The strongest diagnosis usually combines several methods. A single alarm, LED or measurement rarely tells the entire story.

15. A structured CNC troubleshooting workflow

Use this sequence as a general framework.

Step 1: Make the machine safe

Assess the current hazard and stop the operation using the approved procedure when required. Do not begin physical intervention while hazardous motion or energy remains uncontrolled.

Step 2: Record the symptom

Capture the alarm number and text, affected subsystem, machine mode, operation in progress and relevant preceding events.

Step 3: Define the expected behaviour

State exactly what should have happened.

Example: "After the M06 request, the spindle should orient and the tool changer should execute its transfer sequence."

Step 4: Observe the actual state

Use CNC diagnostics, PLC monitoring, drive diagnostics and physical observations to determine where expected behaviour diverges from actual behaviour.

Step 5: Locate the first failed transition

Find the earliest point where the command, condition, output, physical action or feedback becomes incorrect.

This is often more useful than focusing on the last alarm displayed.

Step 6: Test the leading hypothesis

Use an appropriate measurement or diagnostic check to confirm or reject the suspected cause.

Step 7: Correct the confirmed cause

Repair, replace, adjust or reconfigure only as justified by the evidence and approved procedure.

Step 8: Validate recovery

Execute the approved verification sequence and confirm that the alarm does not recur and the intended operation completes correctly.

Step 9: Document the result

Record the fault, root cause, corrective action, verification result and relevant configuration or component changes.

16. Common diagnostic mistakes

Clearing alarms without recording them: This can destroy useful fault context and event chronology.

Replacing parts based only on alarm text: Alarms identify detected conditions, not always failed components.

Changing parameters to eliminate symptoms: This may conceal a genuine mechanical, electrical or feedback problem.

Forcing PLC outputs to bypass sequence logic: This can cause unexpected motion or equipment damage and defeat intended protection.

Ignoring the physical mechanism: A correct electrical command does not prove that an actuator, valve or transmission is functioning.

Assuming a sensor is good because its LED is lit: The PLC input, wiring and logic still need verification.

Repeatedly resetting a recurring fault: A reset may clear the displayed condition without correcting its cause.

Changing multiple variables at once: This makes cause-and-effect analysis much harder and can introduce new faults.

Skipping post-repair verification: Restoring power or clearing the alarm does not prove that the machine is ready for production.

17. Safety requirements during diagnostics

CNC machines combine high-energy electrical systems, rotating spindles, heavy axes, sharp tools, hydraulic pressure and automated mechanisms.

Diagnostic work must account for all of them.

  • Keep guards and safety interlocks operational.
  • Use the approved stop and recovery procedure.
  • Apply lockout/tagout and verify isolation before work requiring hazardous-energy isolation.
  • Account for stored electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic and mechanical energy.
  • Never reach into a tool changer, chuck or moving axis to inspect a fault.
  • Do not bypass safety-related logic to complete a diagnostic sequence.
  • Do not force production outputs without an authorized procedure and suitable risk controls.
  • Use appropriate electrical test equipment and protective measures.
  • Confirm that the work area is clear before approved functional testing.
  • Follow the machine manufacturer's maintenance and commissioning instructions.

An alarm reset is not an energy-isolation procedure, and a program stop is not proof that all hazardous motion has ceased.

18. Interview questions and answers

Q1. What is CNC diagnostics?

The systematic investigation of CNC alarms, signals, feedback, configuration and physical equipment to identify the cause of a fault.

Q2. What is the difference between an alarm and a root cause?

An alarm reports a detected condition. The root cause is the underlying fault or condition that produced it.

Q3. What is a following error?

A difference between commanded and measured axis position, interpreted according to the controller's feedback and monitoring conventions.

Q4. Why should alarm history be recorded before resetting a machine?

It preserves fault context and event chronology that may help identify the first failed condition.

Q5. How would you diagnose an axis that does not move?

Check the command, operating mode, interlocks, drive readiness, feedback, electrical condition and mechanical load in a controlled sequence.

Q6. How would you diagnose an M06 timeout?

Trace the tool-change request, PLC sequence, permissives, outputs, actuator response, sensor feedback and completion handshake.

Q7. What is the difference between commanded position and actual position?

Commanded position is the intended position. Actual position is the controller's reported position based on its feedback and calculation methods.

Q8. Why might a spindle fail to start after M03?

Possible causes include an interlock, drive fault, missing enable, incorrect command, communication issue or spindle-system problem.

Q9. Why can a sensor appear active while the PLC sequence remains incomplete?

The input may not be received or interpreted correctly, another required condition may be missing, or the sequence may be waiting for a different signal.

Q10. What is signal tracing?

Following a command from its source through control logic, outputs, physical action and feedback to determine where the expected sequence fails.

Q11. How can a work-offset problem be distinguished from a servo problem?

A work-offset error changes the interpreted part coordinate origin. A servo fault involves the motion-control system or its response. Compare commanded coordinates, active offsets, actual motion and drive diagnostics.

Q12. Why should parameters not be changed before checking hardware?

Hardware and mechanical faults can mimic configuration problems. Unjustified changes may worsen the fault or introduce additional risks.

Q13. What is the purpose of PLC online monitoring?

It allows authorized personnel to inspect sequence states, interlocks and input/output conditions during diagnosis.

Q14. How do pneumatic faults affect CNC operation?

Insufficient pressure or a failed pneumatic component can prevent clamping, tool release or other machine functions and cause sequence timeouts.

Q15. What is the difference between a drive-ready signal and motion completion?

Drive-ready indicates that the drive meets its configured readiness conditions. Motion completion requires the relevant movement and control conditions to be satisfied.

Q16. Why can the first alarm be more useful than the last alarm?

Later alarms may be consequences of an earlier failure. Chronology helps distinguish initiating events from secondary effects.

Q17. What should be recorded in a CNC fault report?

The symptom, alarm history, machine state, evidence, root cause, corrective action and validation result.

Q18. Why is post-repair verification necessary?

It confirms that the corrective action restored the required function and that the original fault does not immediately recur.

Q19. Why is forcing a PLC output dangerous?

It may activate a mechanism without the normal sequence conditions or interlocks being satisfied.

Q20. What is the most useful general diagnostic principle?

Trace the complete command-to-feedback path and identify the first point where actual behaviour diverges from expected behaviour.

19. Practical exercises

Exercise 1: Axis following error

A machining centre reports a following error during axis movement.

Develop a diagnostic tree covering:

  • Alarm history.
  • Commanded versus actual position.
  • Drive readiness and alarms.
  • Feedback integrity.
  • Mechanical load and binding.
  • Coupling or transmission condition.
  • Approved configuration comparison.

Identify the evidence required before concluding that the servo motor is defective.

Exercise 2: M06 timeout

A tool change starts but does not complete.

Prepare a signal table containing:

Signal or state Expected evidence
CNC M06 request Request recognized
PLC sequence Correct state progression
Spindle status Required stop/orientation conditions met
Pneumatic pressure Within specified range
Tool changer output Commanded when permitted
Position feedback Required mechanism position confirmed
Clamp feedback Required clamp state confirmed
Completion handshake Correct completion acknowledgement
Alarm Identifies failed condition or timeout

Use the actual machine's documented sequence when performing real diagnostics.

Exercise 3: Spindle does not reach speed

The CNC requests 2,000 RPM, but the spindle remains below the target.

Separate the investigation into:

  • Program command.
  • CNC speed reference.
  • PLC permissives.
  • Drive status.
  • Gear range.
  • Motor and mechanical load.
  • Speed feedback.
  • Relevant alarms and monitoring conditions.

Exercise 4: Coolant failure

M08 is requested and the PLC output turns ON, but no coolant reaches the tool.

Determine how to distinguish among a failed starter, pump fault, valve problem, blocked circuit, low coolant level and missing flow feedback.

Exercise 5: Intermittent encoder fault

An axis occasionally reports a feedback alarm, especially during movement.

Develop an investigation covering connectors, cable routing, shielding, power supply, encoder status, drive diagnostics and mechanical vibration.

Do not assume that changing a parameter will correct an intermittent electrical fault.

Exercise 6: Create a CNC diagnostic report

Create a reusable report with these fields:

  • Machine ID and controller model.
  • Date/time and operating condition.
  • Program block or operation.
  • Alarm number and text.
  • First observed abnormal condition.
  • CNC status and actual values.
  • PLC sequence and interlock states.
  • Drive alarms and feedback.
  • Electrical, mechanical or pressure measurements.
  • Confirmed root cause.
  • Corrective action.
  • Verification result.
  • Required follow-up and configuration changes.

Exercise 7: Distinguish symptoms from causes

For each symptom below, identify at least three plausible causes and one measurement or observation that would help distinguish them:

  • Axis fails to move.
  • Spindle does not start.
  • Tool changer times out.
  • Part is machined in the wrong position.
  • CNC reports a communication alarm.

The objective is to build a diagnostic process based on evidence rather than assumptions.

The key principles are:

  • An alarm is evidence, not automatically a root cause.
  • The first failed condition is often more useful than the final displayed alarm.
  • CNC, PLC, drive, feedback and mechanical systems must be considered together.
  • A commanded output does not prove physical operation.
  • Actual feedback must be interpreted in the context of the controller.
  • Parameter changes and component replacements require evidence.
  • Safety isolation and approved recovery procedures take priority over production urgency.
  • Successful repair includes verification and documentation.

For industrial maintenance, good CNC diagnostics means turning an ambiguous machine symptom into a testable sequence of conditions, then proving where the sequence failed. That is how troubleshooting becomes repeatable rather than dependent on whoever happens to be standing beside the machine.
