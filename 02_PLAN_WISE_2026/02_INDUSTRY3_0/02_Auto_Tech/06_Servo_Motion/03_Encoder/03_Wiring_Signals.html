1. Purpose of encoder wiring and signal integrity

An encoder measures rotary or linear motion and sends information to a servo drive, PLC, motion controller, or measurement system. Correct wiring ensures that the receiver interprets the position and speed accurately.

Encoder wiring must satisfy three independent requirements:

  1. Electrical compatibility: The encoder's supply voltage and output type must match the receiver.
  2. Signal integrity: The cable must transmit clean signals without excessive attenuation, noise, or distortion.
  3. Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC): Shielding, grounding, bonding, and cable routing must limit interference from industrial equipment.

A wiring fault can cause feedback alarms, incorrect position, speed fluctuations, unstable servo control, or intermittent machine stoppages.

In an automotive production line, a robot axis may operate normally at low speed but develop encoder faults when a nearby servo motor, welding gun, or high-power drive operates. The encoder may be healthy; the installation may be the problem.

2. Main encoder wiring connections

An encoder connection may contain the following conductors.

Connection Function Important check
Supply positive, such as +V Powers the encoder electronics Correct voltage and polarity
0 V / supply return Provides the supply return path Correct connection and voltage drop
A and B Incremental position pulses Correct phase relationship and signal levels
Z / index Reference pulse on supported incremental encoders Correct pulse presence and expected frequency
A̅, B̅, Z̅ or A−, B−, Z− Complementary outputs for supported differential interfaces Correct differential receiver and pair wiring
Clock and data conductors Carry serial communication on supported absolute encoders Correct protocol, polarity, and interface
Shield Reduces coupling of electromagnetic interference into the cable Correct shield termination and bonding
Protective earth (PE) Provides protective bonding for equipment as designed Follow the equipment's grounding scheme

Not every encoder uses every connection. Some provide incremental pulses, some provide serial absolute-position data, and some support more than one interface.

Never identify a connector pinout from wire colour alone. Use the exact encoder and drive manuals, including the connector part number and cable specification.

3. Encoder supply voltage

Encoders are designed for particular supply-voltage ranges. Depending on the model, the supply may be 5 V DC, 12 V DC, 24 V DC, or another specified range.

The correct value must come from the manufacturer's documentation.

3.1 Why supply compatibility matters

Suppose an encoder requires a regulated 5 V supply. Applying 24 V can damage the encoder electronics.

Conversely, supplying an encoder that requires 24 V with only 5 V may prevent startup or produce unreliable signals.

Supply checks should include:

  • Correct nominal voltage and permitted tolerance.
  • Correct polarity.
  • Supply current capacity.
  • Voltage measured at the encoder connector, not just at the source.
  • Voltage drop across long cables and connectors.
  • Supply stability during motor acceleration and switching events.
  • Correct connection of supply return and signal reference where specified.

A voltage that appears correct with the encoder disconnected may drop under load because of a damaged conductor, loose terminal, or inadequate supply.

3.2 Practical supply measurement

  1. Identify the exact supply pins from the manufacturer's wiring diagram.
  2. Confirm that the equipment can be measured safely.
  3. Measure the supply at the encoder connector using an appropriate meter.
  4. Compare the reading with the permitted voltage range.
  5. If the fault is intermittent, measure under the conditions that produce the fault.
  6. Check for supply dips when the servo drive, brake, or nearby equipment switches.

Do not probe an unidentified connector. An encoder connector may contain supply, communication, or other conductors with different electrical characteristics.

4. Encoder output types

The receiver must be compatible with the encoder's electrical output. The words incremental encoder describe how position is represented, not necessarily how the electrical signals are transmitted.

4.1 Differential line-driver output

A differential encoder transmits a signal using two conductors for each signal, such as A+ and A−.

The receiver evaluates the voltage difference between the pair:

\[ V\_{\text{diff}}=V\_{A+}-V\_{A-} \]

Noise coupled similarly into both conductors can be rejected by a suitable differential receiver, within its specified common-mode range and other limits.

Typical industrial differential interfaces include RS-422-compatible line drivers and receivers.

Advantages:

  • Good noise immunity.
  • Suitable for many high-speed encoder applications.
  • Useful for longer cable runs when correctly designed.

Requirements:

  • Correct differential receiver.
  • Appropriate twisted-pair cable.
  • Correct termination where required.
  • Compatible voltage levels and common-mode limits.
  • Correct pair polarity and cable pinout.

A− is not the same thing as 0 V. It is the complementary signal output. Connecting it to ground can damage equipment or prevent correct signal reception.

4.2 Single-ended output

A single-ended output represents a signal relative to a common reference, commonly the specified 0 V connection.

It may be suitable for short cable runs and compatible inputs, but it is generally more sensitive to ground-reference differences and electrical interference than a well-designed differential interface.

The maximum cable length and operating frequency depend on the output circuitry, cable capacitance, receiver, and installation conditions.

4.3 Push-pull / HTL output

Push-pull outputs actively drive the signal high and low. HTL commonly refers to higher-level incremental encoder signals, often used with 10–30 V-class industrial interfaces, depending on the actual specification.

Advantages can include compatibility with suitable industrial high-speed inputs and good signal margins in the intended voltage range.

However, HTL and TTL are not interchangeable. The receiver must accept the encoder's actual voltage levels, output current, switching frequency, and circuit arrangement.

4.4 TTL-compatible output

TTL-compatible encoder outputs commonly operate at lower voltage levels. Some are implemented with differential line drivers; others use different output arrangements.

Do not assume that every TTL-labelled encoder has the same electrical interface or connector assignment.

4.5 Open-collector output

An open-collector output requires an appropriate external pull-up or receiver arrangement.

The signal transitions depend on the output transistor, pull-up resistance, cable capacitance, and receiver load. Incorrect pull-up selection can cause slow edges or overload the output.

Use only the manufacturer's specified interface circuit.

5. Incremental encoder signals: A, B and Z

An incremental encoder generates pulses as its shaft moves.

A signal:  __----____----____----
B signal:  ____----____----____--
Z signal:  ____________--________

This is a simplified conceptual representation, not a precise timing diagram.

5.1 Channel A and channel B

Channels A and B are typically phase-shifted by approximately 90 electrical degrees in a quadrature encoder.

The relative phase determines the direction according to the encoder and receiver convention.

  • If A leads B, the receiver interprets one direction.
  • If B leads A, the receiver interprets the opposite direction.

The actual forward/reverse convention depends on wiring, configuration, and the device's definition of positive motion.

5.2 Channel Z or index

Z provides a reference or index pulse on encoders that support it.

It can be used to identify a repeatable shaft reference or assist with homing and position-reference procedures.

The number of Z pulses per revolution and their relationship to A/B signals must be verified from the encoder specification. Do not assume every model has exactly one index pulse per revolution.

5.3 Complementary signals

A differential encoder may provide A+, A−, B+, B−, and optionally Z+, Z−.

For each pair, the signals are complementary within the specified electrical limits.

A differential receiver detects the difference between the two conductors. This improves noise rejection when the wiring and receiver are designed correctly.

6. Absolute encoder wiring and communication

Absolute encoders provide position information encoded as an absolute value rather than requiring the receiver to reconstruct all position changes from incremental pulses since startup.

Their interfaces can include:

  • SSI.
  • BiSS-C.
  • EnDat.
  • HIPERFACE and HIPERFACE DSL.
  • Manufacturer-specific serial interfaces.
  • Fieldbus-based or integrated feedback arrangements.

These interfaces are not interchangeable merely because their cables have a similar number of conductors.

Depending on the interface, wiring may carry clock, data, power, reference, or additional communication signals. Some systems use differential clock/data pairs, while others use different physical-layer arrangements.

Troubleshooting should check:

  1. Correct encoder and drive compatibility.
  2. Correct cable and connector pinout.
  3. Supply voltage and current.
  4. Communication wiring and polarity.
  5. Protocol selection and parameterization.
  6. Feedback status, communication errors, and data-validity diagnostics.
  7. Firmware or configuration compatibility where relevant.

Some interfaces include error-detection mechanisms such as checksums or CRCs. Use the drive's diagnostic information and the protocol documentation to interpret errors.

7. Shielding and electromagnetic interference

Encoder signals can be affected by electromagnetic interference (EMI) generated by:

  • Servo motor power cables.
  • Variable-frequency drives (VFDs).
  • Contactors and relays.
  • Welding equipment.
  • Solenoids and brake circuits.
  • High-current conductors.
  • Poorly bonded machine structures.
  • Switching power supplies.

The interference can couple into the encoder cable through electric fields, magnetic fields, or common impedance in grounding and bonding paths.

7.1 What a cable shield does

A conductive shield around the signal conductors reduces the coupling of external interference into the cable when properly designed and terminated.

The shield is not a substitute for protective earth, and it does not correct every EMC problem. Cable construction, routing, connector design, grounding, and bonding all matter.

7.2 Correct shield termination

For many industrial servo feedback cables, the manufacturer's EMC design uses a low-impedance, often 360-degree shield connection at the drive or cabinet entry. Depending on the equipment and bonding arrangement, both ends may need to be bonded.

There is no universal rule that the shield must always be grounded at one end or always at both ends.

Follow the exact drive and encoder manufacturer's instructions. The correct arrangement depends on the interface, frequency range, machine bonding, and EMC design.

Important practices:

  • Use the specified shielded cable.
  • Maintain a short, low-impedance shield connection as specified.
  • Use the intended EMC clamp or connector shell arrangement.
  • Maintain suitable equipotential bonding between machine sections and the cabinet.
  • Do not replace a proper shield termination with a long, thin drain-wire pigtail where a high-frequency 360-degree connection is specified.
  • Do not use the cable shield as the normal supply-return conductor or as a substitute for PE.

7.3 Protective earth versus cable shield

Feature Protective earth (PE) Cable shield
Primary purpose Electrical shock protection and protective bonding Reduction of electromagnetic interference
Carries normal operating current? Not intended to carry normal load current Not intended to be the normal signal or supply return
Fault-current role Part of the protective fault-current path as designed Not a replacement for the protective-earth conductor
Connection method According to electrical and safety requirements According to the equipment's EMC instructions
Can one replace the other? No No

Both are important, but they serve different purposes.

8. Cable routing and physical installation

Encoder cables should be routed to limit electrical interference and mechanical damage.

Preferred arrangement

[Servo Drive] ===== Motor power cable ===== [Servo Motor]
      |
      +--------- Encoder cable ------------ [Feedback connector]
                   Separate route

The diagram shows separate routes conceptually. Actual separation distances and permitted cable arrangements must follow the equipment's installation instructions.

Practical rules:

  • Separate feedback cables from motor power cables and other high-interference wiring.
  • Use designated cable ducts or partitions where appropriate.
  • If signal and power cables must cross, crossing at approximately 90 degrees can reduce coupling.
  • Avoid long parallel runs close to VFD output cables.
  • Use twisted pairs for interfaces that specify them.
  • Use flex-rated cables for robot axes and moving equipment.
  • Observe the specified minimum bend radius.
  • Provide strain relief at connectors and moving points.
  • Protect cables from oil, coolant, sharp edges, welding spatter, heat, and abrasion.
  • Avoid unapproved splices or substitutions.
  • Use the specified connector shell and cable-entry arrangement.

For a robotic line, a feedback cable may pass through dress packs, energy chains, or moving joints. Repeated flexing can cause intermittent conductor breaks that are difficult to detect with a stationary continuity test.

9. Termination and receiver compatibility

A signal may be correct at the encoder but corrupted by the time it reaches the receiver.

Potential causes include:

  • Wrong cable impedance or type.
  • Incorrect termination.
  • Excessive cable length.
  • Excessive input capacitance.
  • Receiver input not compatible with the encoder output.
  • Loose or oxidized connectors.
  • Damaged twisted pairs.
  • Poor shield termination.
  • Incorrect signal polarity.

For high-speed differential links, termination is generally selected to match the specified cable and receiver design. A termination resistor should not be added or removed arbitrarily because it can alter signal amplitude, loading, and waveform quality.

The manufacturer's wiring guide determines whether termination is required, where it belongs, and which value is appropriate.

10. Tools for checking encoder wiring and signals

Tool Suitable checks Limitations
Digital multimeter Supply voltage, polarity, continuity, resistance checks on isolated wiring Usually cannot reveal pulse quality or short-duration interference
Oscilloscope Pulse amplitude, timing, noise, ringing, missing transitions Requires correct probes, grounding, bandwidth, and measurement setup
Differential probe Differential waveform measurement Must be rated for the signal and electrical environment
Encoder diagnostic software Drive-reported feedback status, errors, position, speed, and supported diagnostics Depends on drive capabilities and available parameters
Suitable logic analyzer Digital timing and protocol inspection Input voltage limits and sampling rate must match the signal
Cable tester Conductor continuity and wiring faults Does not prove correct high-speed signal integrity

10.1 Important measurement precautions

  • Confirm the signal type before connecting a measuring instrument.
  • Use a differential probe or suitable differential measurement arrangement for differential signals.
  • Do not connect an oscilloscope's grounded probe clip to an arbitrary differential output conductor.
  • Do not connect a low-voltage logic analyzer directly to a 12 V or 24 V industrial signal unless a properly rated interface is used.
  • Verify the instrument's voltage, common-mode, and bandwidth limits.
  • Isolate power before continuity or resistance testing.
  • Never short encoder outputs while probing.

A multimeter reading alone cannot prove that an encoder produces clean pulses at operating speed.

11. Checking incremental encoder signals

A useful signal check examines electrical levels, timing, phase relationship, and behavior as speed changes.

Step 1: Check supply

Verify supply voltage at the encoder connector under the relevant operating conditions.

Step 2: Inspect the physical wiring

Check connector seating, pins, cable damage, strain relief, shield termination, and routing.

Step 3: Check A and B

Using suitable equipment, confirm that A and B transition cleanly and that their phase relationship is plausible for quadrature signals.

For a differential output, inspect the differential signals using the correct measurement method.

Step 4: Check Z if fitted

Move the encoder through sufficient rotation to determine whether the index signal occurs as specified.

Step 5: Check behavior at speed

Compare signal quality at low and higher speeds, within the permitted operating range. Missing transitions at higher speed may indicate an input-frequency limit, poor signal integrity, or a measurement setup problem.

Step 6: Compare with receiver diagnostics

Check the drive or high-speed-counter diagnostic values against expected movement.

If the encoder produces a specified number of pulses per revolution, the expected count can be estimated from the encoder resolution and the receiver's decoding method.

12. Encoder resolution, frequency, and PLC input limits

Encoder resolution is often specified in pulses per revolution (PPR) or cycles per revolution. The terminology varies, so check the datasheet carefully.

For an incremental encoder with \(N\) cycles per revolution and shaft speed \(R\) RPM, the channel-cycle frequency is:

\[ f=\frac{N R}{60} \]

For example, an encoder with 2,500 cycles per revolution operating at 3,000 RPM produces:

\[ f=\frac{2500\times3000}{60}=125{,}000\text{ Hz} \]

That is 125 kHz per channel.

A quadrature receiver using x4 decoding may count four edges per cycle, producing up to 500,000 counts per second in this example, assuming all four edges are decoded.

Do not confuse cycles, pulses, edges, and counts. Encoder manufacturers and PLC modules may define PPR or resolution differently.

The receiver must support the resulting signal frequency and count rate. Ordinary PLC digital inputs can miss high-speed transitions because their scan and input-filter behavior may not be suitable.

Use a compatible high-speed counter (HSC), dedicated motion module, or supported drive interface when required.

14. Practical fault-finding procedure

Use a consistent sequence rather than replacing the encoder immediately.

  1. Record the alarm. Capture the drive code, timestamp, machine condition, and whether the fault occurs at startup, at speed, or during movement.
  2. Identify the exact hardware. Record encoder model, drive model, cable part number, and interface type.
  3. Verify compatibility. Confirm supply voltage, output type or communication protocol, connector assignment, and receiver capability.
  4. Inspect the cable. Look for damage, contamination, loose connectors, excessive bending, and incorrect routing.
  5. Check the supply. Measure the specified supply at the appropriate connector under relevant conditions.
  6. Verify shielding and bonding. Compare the installation with the manufacturer's EMC instructions.
  7. Check signal quality. Use appropriate measurement equipment and compare results with the interface specification.
  8. Review configuration. Check encoder type, resolution, protocol, direction, scaling, and relevant drive parameters.
  9. Validate mechanically. Check coupling, backlash, shaft movement, and any load-related issue.
  10. Perform a controlled test. After restoring guards and required safety functions, validate the result at low speed before returning the machine to normal operation.

A fault that disappears after resetting may still be present. Record the evidence before clearing alarms, or useful diagnostic information may be lost.

15. PLC and servo-drive integration

There are two common arrangements for using encoder feedback.

Arrangement A: Encoder connected to a servo drive

Motion Controller
       |
       | Motion command
       v
   Servo Drive <------ Encoder
       |
       | Motor power
       v
   Servo Motor

The drive uses the supported feedback interface to control the motor. This is common in industrial servo systems.

Arrangement B: Encoder connected to a PLC high-speed counter

Incremental Encoder
       |
       | A / B / Z
       v
PLC High-Speed Counter
       |
       v
Position / Speed Logic

This is useful for measuring conveyor movement, counting parts, tracking positions, or performing supported position-based operations.

Important considerations:

  • Match the encoder output to the HSC input type.
  • Check maximum input frequency and count rate.
  • Confirm x1, x2, or x4 decoding behavior.
  • Configure input filtering appropriately.
  • Check A/B direction convention and Z handling.
  • Verify voltage levels and common-reference requirements.
  • Use a suitable differential receiver when needed.

Do not arbitrarily connect one encoder output to multiple receivers in parallel. The electrical loading, termination, isolation, and manufacturer-supported connection arrangement must be verified.

16. Safety during inspection and testing

Encoder circuits may operate at low voltage, but the surrounding servo equipment can contain hazardous energy.

  • Follow the machine's lockout/tagout and electrical safety procedure before inspecting or modifying wiring.
  • Verify the absence of voltage where required.
  • Observe the manufacturer's DC-bus discharge time before working on the servo drive.
  • Secure vertical or gravity-loaded axes before disconnecting feedback, motor power, or brake wiring.
  • Do not disconnect or reconnect feedback cables while energized unless the manufacturer explicitly permits it.
  • Do not megger an encoder cable while the encoder or drive electronics remain connected. Disconnect sensitive equipment and follow the specified insulation-test procedure.
  • Do not bypass encoder or drive faults to keep production running.
  • After repair, restore covers, connectors, protective bonding, guards, and safety functions before controlled validation.

17. Interview questions and answers

1. Why does an encoder need a compatible power supply?

Its internal electronics and output circuitry are designed for a specified voltage range. Incorrect supply voltage or polarity can prevent operation or damage the encoder.

2. What is the purpose of A and B signals?

They provide quadrature pulse information. Their relative phase allows the receiver to determine direction and count movement.

3. What is the purpose of the Z signal?

It provides an index or reference event on encoders that support it. The exact pulse count and use depend on the encoder specification.

4. What is the difference between single-ended and differential signals?

Single-ended signals are interpreted relative to a reference. Differential receivers evaluate the difference between two signal conductors and can reject common-mode noise within their specified operating range.

5. Why is A− not the same as 0 V?

A− is the complementary signal conductor in a differential interface. It must be connected to the matching receiver input, not treated as the supply return.

6. Why are encoder cables shielded?

Shielding reduces the coupling of electromagnetic interference into the signal conductors when the cable and terminations are correctly designed.

7. Should a cable shield always be grounded at one end?

No. The correct shield arrangement depends on the equipment and EMC design. Many industrial servo systems specify low-impedance bonding at both ends or at defined EMC connection points.

8. Why must encoder cables be separated from motor power cables?

Motor switching currents can generate interference that corrupts feedback signals. Separation and correct routing reduce this coupling.

9. Why might an encoder work at low speed but fail at high speed?

The receiver may exceed its input-frequency limit, or the cable and signal interface may not preserve clean transitions at the higher frequency.

10. Why can a multimeter fail to identify an encoder signal problem?

A multimeter generally does not reveal pulse timing, short noise spikes, edge distortion, or missing transitions at high speed.

11. What is the role of a high-speed counter?

It counts rapid digital transitions using hardware designed for higher input frequencies than ordinary PLC scan-based inputs can reliably handle.

12. Why must absolute encoder protocols be matched?

Different protocols use different communication methods, timing, signaling, and data formats. Similar-looking cables do not make the protocols interchangeable.

13. What is a common cause of intermittent encoder faults on a robot?

Damaged conductors or shielding in moving cables, loose connectors, and poor strain relief are common possibilities.

14. Can the cable shield replace protective earth?

No. Protective earth serves an electrical safety function; the shield serves an EMC function.

15. Why should an encoder cable not be megger-tested while connected to the electronics?

An insulation tester can apply a voltage that damages sensitive encoder or drive circuits. Follow the manufacturer's isolation and test instructions.

16. What should be checked after replacing an encoder cable?

Verify the exact cable and pinout, output interface, supply, shield termination, connector seating, configuration, and signal behavior before controlled validation.

18. Practical exercises

Exercise 1: Feedback fault when a nearby drive starts

A servo axis runs normally until a nearby VFD starts, after which the servo reports an encoder fault.

Investigate:

  • Whether the encoder and motor cables are routed together.
  • Whether the specified shield clamps and bonding points are installed.
  • Whether the encoder supply dips during switching.
  • Whether the feedback waveform degrades at the time of the fault.
  • Whether the problem follows a specific operating condition.

Do not conclude that the encoder itself is defective until the electrical installation has been checked.

Exercise 2: Encoder counts fail at high speed

An incremental encoder counts correctly at low speed but reports an incorrect position at high speed.

Calculate the expected channel frequency and decoded count rate. Compare both against the encoder output specification and receiver input limits. Then inspect waveform quality, cable type, and input configuration.

Exercise 3: Absolute encoder fails after cable replacement

The motor reports an encoder communication alarm after a cable is replaced.

Check the exact cable part number, connector pinout, communication pairs, supply and return, shielding, and protocol compatibility. Verify that the replacement cable is suitable for the particular feedback interface.

Exercise 4: Create an encoder wiring record

For one production machine, record:

Record field Information required
Machine and axis Machine identifier and axis name
Encoder Manufacturer and exact model
Supply Nominal voltage and permitted range
Interface Incremental, absolute, or other supported type
Output / protocol Differential, single-ended, SSI, BiSS-C, EnDat, or specified interface
Resolution Manufacturer-defined resolution and units
Cable Approved part number, length, and flex rating where applicable
Receiver Drive, PLC HSC, or supported communication module
Shielding Manufacturer-specified termination and bonding scheme
Diagnostics Relevant drive alarms and signal checks
Reference Exact manual or wiring diagram revision

This record prevents a future replacement from becoming an exercise in connector archaeology.

19. Final mental model

Encoder wiring depends on three fundamentals:

  • Power: The supply voltage, polarity, and current capacity must match the encoder.
  • Signals: The output interface, receiver, frequency capability, and communication protocol must be compatible.
  • Installation: Cable routing, shielding, grounding, bonding, and mechanical protection must preserve signal integrity.

When an encoder fault occurs, check the model-specific wiring, supply, cable, receiver, and signal quality before replacing parts. For high-speed or intermittent faults, use appropriate diagnostics and measurement tools rather than relying on continuity alone.
