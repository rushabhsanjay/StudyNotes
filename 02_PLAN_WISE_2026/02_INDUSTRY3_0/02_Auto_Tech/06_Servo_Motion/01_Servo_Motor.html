1. Definition and purpose

A servo motor is an electric motor used in a closed-loop motion-control system to achieve controlled position, speed, and torque. It typically works with a servo drive and a feedback device, such as an encoder or resolver.

Unlike a conventional motor that primarily runs at a commanded speed, a servo system continuously compares the commanded motion with the actual motion and corrects the error.

Typical applications include robotic joints, CNC axes, precision indexing tables, automated transfer units, pick-and-place mechanisms, and packaging machines.

2. Servo system architecture

A servo motor is only one part of the system. The motor, drive, feedback device, controller, and mechanical load must work together.

          |
          | Position / Speed / Torque Command
          v

          |                          |

          v                          |
      Servo Motor ---------------- Encoder

The servo drive supplies controlled electrical power to the motor. The feedback device reports actual motion, allowing the drive or motion controller to correct deviations.

The exact control-loop arrangement depends on the drive and controller architecture.

4. How a servo motor works

Consider a robotic transfer axis that must move from position A to position B.

  1. The controller generates a motion command.
  2. The servo drive receives the command and determines the required motor response.
  3. The drive applies controlled current to the motor windings.
  4. The motor develops torque and accelerates the load.
  5. The encoder reports actual rotor position.
  6. The control system compares commanded and actual motion.
  7. The drive adjusts motor current to reduce the error.
  8. The motor decelerates and settles at the target position.

The system repeats this process continuously while motion is commanded.

Important distinction: An encoder does not make a motor a servo by itself. The feedback device, compatible drive, control loops, and correctly configured motion system together provide servo behavior.

5. Common servo motor types

5.1 Permanent-magnet synchronous servo motor

This is widely used in modern industrial servo systems.

  • The rotor contains permanent magnets.
  • The stator carries controlled multiphase windings.
  • The drive synchronizes the stator magnetic field with rotor position.
  • The motor provides high torque relative to its size and supports precise dynamic control.

Advantages:

  • High torque density.
  • Fast acceleration and deceleration.
  • Good low-speed torque control.
  • High positioning accuracy when paired with suitable feedback and mechanics.

Limitations:

  • Requires a compatible servo drive.
  • Permanent magnets can be affected by excessive temperature.
  • Feedback and commutation compatibility are important.

5.2 AC induction servo motor

An induction motor can be used in a servo application when paired with a suitable drive and feedback arrangement.

  • The rotor develops torque through electromagnetic induction.
  • The drive controls motor flux and torque.
  • Feedback may be used for accurate speed and position control.

Compared with permanent-magnet servo motors, induction motors can have lower torque density and different low-speed performance characteristics. Their suitability depends on the application and control system.

5.3 Brushed DC servo motor

A brushed DC motor can be used in servo applications.

  • Mechanical brushes and a commutator switch the armature current.
  • Speed and torque can be controlled through armature voltage and current.
  • Position feedback closes the position-control loop.

Brush wear and commutator maintenance make this design less common in modern industrial servo applications than brushless AC designs.

5.4 Brushless DC motor versus AC servo motor

The terminology can be confusing because manufacturers do not always use the labels consistently.

A brushless DC motor and a permanent-magnet synchronous motor can have similar physical construction. Differences may lie in back-EMF waveform, drive commutation method, and control strategy.

Do not select a motor based only on the label BLDC or AC servo. Check the manufacturer's motor-drive compatibility and intended control method.

6. Servo motor versus induction motor versus stepper motor

A servo is not automatically the best choice for every machine. A conveyor running at a steady speed may need only an induction motor and VFD. A robot axis requiring rapid, repeatable positioning is a much stronger candidate for a servo system.

Rated torque and peak torque

Rated torque is associated with continuous operation within the manufacturer's thermal limits. Peak torque is available only within specified limits for a limited time.

A motor should not be selected using peak torque alone. Continuous load torque, acceleration torque, duty cycle, ambient temperature, and drive limitations must all be considered.

8. Torque, speed, and mechanical power

For rotational motion:

\[ P=T\omega \]

Where:

  • \(P\) = mechanical power in watts.
  • \(T\) = torque in newton-metres.
  • \(\omega\) = angular speed in radians per second.

Using rotational speed in RPM:

\[ P=\frac{2\pi NT}{60} \]

Where \(N\) is the speed in RPM.

For example, if a motor produces 5 N·m at 3,000 RPM:

\[ P=\frac{2\pi \times 3000 \times 5}{60} \]
\[ P\approx1571\text{ W} \]

The mechanical output is approximately 1.57 kW at that operating point. Actual continuous capability still depends on the manufacturer's torque-speed curve, cooling conditions, and drive limits.

9. Torque requirements of the machine

The servo motor must generate enough torque for the load, not merely enough torque to rotate itself.

A simplified torque balance is:

\[ T\_{\text{motor}}=J\_{\text{eq}}\alpha+T\_{\text{load}}+T\_{\text{loss}} \]

Where:

  • \(J\_{\text{eq}}\) = equivalent rotational inertia referred to the motor shaft.
  • \(\alpha\) = angular acceleration.
  • \(T\_{\text{load}}\) = torque required by the external load.
  • \(T\_{\text{loss}}\) = torque associated with friction and other losses.

This simplified expression assumes the relevant quantities are referred to the same shaft and uses the appropriate signs for the direction of motion.

The required torque rises when acceleration increases, the load increases, or mechanical friction becomes excessive.

Example: indexing table

A rotary indexing table must accelerate rapidly, stop at a target angle, and repeat the motion throughout a production shift.

The design must account for:

  • Table and fixture inertia.
  • Workpiece mass and distribution.
  • Gearbox ratio and efficiency.
  • Required acceleration and deceleration.
  • Cycle time.
  • Continuous thermal loading.
  • Positioning and settling requirements.

An oversized motor is not automatically a good solution. It may increase cost and inertia, and it can create drive-sizing or tuning problems.

10. Rotor inertia and inertia matching

Rotor inertia is a measure of resistance to changes in rotational speed.

A high-inertia load requires more torque to accelerate than a low-inertia load at the same angular acceleration.

For a direct-drive system, the load inertia can be compared with motor rotor inertia directly. For a geared system, load inertia must be reflected through the gear ratio.

For an ideal gearbox with reduction ratio:

\[ i=\frac{\omega\_{\text{motor}}}{\omega\_{\text{load}}} \]

The load inertia reflected to the motor side is approximately:

\[ J\_{\text{load,ref}}=\frac{J\_{\text{load}}}{i^2} \]

This relationship assumes the stated ratio convention and an ideal gear train. Real gear losses, compliance, backlash, and coupling flexibility also matter.

Practical consequence: A gearbox can reduce the inertia reflected to the motor side, but it also changes output speed and torque. It does not eliminate the need to check the complete motion profile.

11. Servo motor and servo drive compatibility

A motor and drive must be treated as a matched system.

Check the following before replacement or commissioning:

  • Motor manufacturer and exact model.
  • Drive manufacturer, model, and supported motor series.
  • Rated voltage, current, and power.
  • Motor feedback type and supported interface.
  • Feedback connector and cable part number.
  • Commutation and motor identification requirements.
  • Brake voltage and brake-control method, if fitted.
  • Maximum speed and permitted operating range.
  • Drive firmware and parameter compatibility.
  • Motor thermal sensor type and input compatibility.
  • Mechanical flange, shaft, coupling, and mounting dimensions.

A connector that physically fits does not prove electrical or protocol compatibility. Industrial equipment has many creative ways of making an incorrect connection look convincing.

12. Feedback and commutation

A servo drive needs information about motor position to control torque and motion correctly.

The feedback system may use an incremental encoder, absolute encoder, resolver, or another supported device.

Depending on motor and drive design, the feedback provides information used for:

  • Rotor position.
  • Speed estimation.
  • Position control.
  • Electrical commutation.
  • Startup position determination.
  • Detection of feedback or tracking errors.

An incremental encoder may require a homing procedure after power-up to establish the machine's reference position. An absolute encoder can report position without counting every movement from a power-up reference, but the machine may still need homing or reference validation depending on its mechanical arrangement and safety requirements.

Feedback resolution is not the same as actual machine accuracy. Backlash, mechanical compliance, coupling slip, encoder mounting, calibration, and control tuning can all affect the final result.

13. Holding brakes on servo motors

Some servo motors include an electromagnetic holding brake.

Typical purpose:

  • Hold a vertical axis when motor torque is removed.
  • Prevent uncontrolled movement during a safe shutdown sequence.
  • Maintain a stationary load when the motor is de-energized, within the brake's rated capability.

Important points:

  • A holding brake is not automatically a safety-rated brake.
  • Many holding brakes are designed for static holding, not repeated dynamic stopping.
  • The brake must be controlled using the manufacturer's specified voltage and circuit.
  • Brake release and engagement timing must match the drive and machine sequence.
  • A vertical axis may require additional mechanical protection, such as a counterbalance or safety-rated braking arrangement.

Never assume a brake will safely support a suspended load simply because the motor has one.

14. Servo motor wiring overview

A servo motor installation may include several separate connections:

Servo Drive                         Servo Motor
----------                          -----------
U / V / W  -----------------------> Motor phases
PE         -----------------------> Protective earth
Feedback port <-------------------> Encoder / resolver
Brake output ---------------------> Brake circuit, if fitted
Thermal input <------------------- Motor temperature sensor

This is a conceptual diagram, not a universal pinout. The exact terminals, connector assignments, cable requirements, and brake circuit depend on the manufacturer and model.

General requirements:

  • Use the specified motor power cable and feedback cable.
  • Follow the manufacturer's protective-earth and EMC instructions.
  • Keep feedback wiring appropriately separated from motor power wiring.
  • Verify the connector and pinout before energizing.
  • Follow the specified DC bus discharge procedure before drive wiring work.
  • Do not disconnect motor feedback or power cables while energized unless the manufacturer explicitly permits the procedure.

15. Common servo motor faults and troubleshooting

Symptom Possible causes Checks
Motor does not rotate Servo enable missing, drive fault, command absent, brake not released Check drive status, enable chain, command, brake supply and feedback alarms
Motor vibrates or oscillates Incorrect tuning, mechanical resonance, loose coupling, feedback noise Check tuning, mechanics, mounting, feedback quality
Positioning error Incorrect scaling, load disturbance, backlash, wrong parameters Verify units, gear ratio, travel limits, mechanics and feedback
Motor overheats Excessive load, high duty cycle, poor cooling, repeated peak-torque demand Check load profile, current, thermal conditions and sizing
Overcurrent alarm Shorted cable, motor winding fault, mechanical jam, incorrect configuration Isolate safely, inspect cable and motor according to the manual
Feedback alarm Damaged cable, incompatible encoder, incorrect connection, poor shielding Verify model, cable, pinout, connector and diagnostic information
Unexpected direction Incorrect direction configuration, command sign, phase or feedback setup Verify configuration and test at controlled low speed
Excessive following error Load too high, acceleration too aggressive, mechanical binding, feedback problem Inspect mechanics, commanded profile, current limits and feedback
Brake-related fault Incorrect brake voltage, faulty brake circuit, timing or mechanical issue Check the specified brake circuit and sequence
Position shifts after power cycle Homing/reference issue, absolute-position validation problem, mechanical movement Verify encoder type, retained position, machine reference and brake function

Troubleshooting sequence:

  1. Record the exact alarm code and operating conditions.
  2. Check the drive's status and diagnostic history.
  3. Confirm the commanded position, speed, torque, and enable state.
  4. Inspect mechanical movement, coupling, gearbox, and load.
  5. Verify power, feedback, brake, and thermal connections.
  6. Check motor-drive compatibility and parameters.
  7. Perform a controlled low-speed test after confirming the safety conditions.

Do not repeatedly reset a fault without identifying its cause. A reset is not a repair strategy, even if production would prefer it to be one.

16. Servo motor selection checklist

Before choosing a motor, establish:

  1. Required continuous and peak torque.
  2. Required speed range.
  3. Acceleration, deceleration, and motion profile.
  4. Load inertia and reflected inertia.
  5. Required positioning accuracy and repeatability.
  6. Duty cycle and thermal conditions.
  7. Motor-drive and feedback compatibility.
  8. Gearbox ratio, efficiency, backlash, and mechanical stiffness.
  9. Brake requirements for vertical or gravity-loaded axes.
  10. Environmental conditions, cable movement, protection rating, and mounting.
  11. Drive current, voltage, regenerative-energy handling, and braking requirements.
  12. Commissioning, maintenance, spare-part, and diagnostic requirements.

For a production machine, sizing should be verified against the manufacturer's torque-speed curves and sizing software where available.

17. Servo motor versus servo drive

Servo motor Servo drive
Converts electrical energy into mechanical torque and rotation Converts the command into controlled electrical power for the motor
Contains the rotor, stator, and associated motor components Contains power electronics, control electronics, and feedback interfaces
Produces the mechanical motion Regulates current and coordinates speed or position control
May contain an encoder, resolver, brake, or thermal sensor Reads supported feedback and monitors faults
Selected based on torque, speed, inertia, environment, and mechanics Selected based on motor compatibility, voltage, current, control features, and system requirements

A servo motor without a suitable drive cannot provide normal industrial servo operation. A drive without a compatible motor and feedback arrangement cannot deliver the intended motion-control performance.

18. Interview questions and answers

1. What is a servo motor?

A motor used in a motion-control system to regulate position, speed, or torque, typically with feedback and a compatible servo drive.

2. Why is feedback used in a servo system?

Feedback allows the control system to compare actual motion with commanded motion and correct deviations.

3. What is the difference between rated torque and peak torque?

Rated torque is the specified continuous operating capability under stated conditions. Peak torque is a higher, time-limited capability subject to the manufacturer's limits.

4. Why is rotor inertia important?

It affects acceleration torque, settling behavior, and the ability of the system to respond to commanded motion.

5. What is inertia matching?

It is the evaluation of motor and load inertia, including load inertia reflected through the transmission, to determine whether the system can achieve the required dynamic performance.

6. Can a servo motor run without an encoder?

Some motors and drives support sensorless control or alternative feedback methods, but conventional high-performance servo positioning commonly depends on supported feedback.

7. Does a high-resolution encoder guarantee high positioning accuracy?

No. Mechanical backlash, compliance, calibration, mounting, feedback quality, and control tuning also affect accuracy.

8. Why might a servo motor overheat?

Excessive load, frequent acceleration, high RMS current, poor cooling, incorrect sizing, or mechanical binding can cause overheating.

9. Why is a holding brake used?

To hold a shaft stationary when required, particularly on vertical axes. Its permitted duty and safety function must be verified.

10. Why must a servo motor and drive be compatible?

Their electrical ratings, feedback interfaces, commutation requirements, parameters, and control capabilities must match.

11. What can cause excessive following error?

Excessive load, aggressive motion commands, insufficient torque, mechanical obstruction, incorrect scaling, or feedback problems.

12. What is the difference between repeatability and accuracy?

Accuracy describes closeness to the true target. Repeatability describes how consistently the system returns to the same position under comparable conditions.

13. Why can a servo motor vibrate while stationary?

Possible causes include poor tuning, feedback noise, mechanical resonance, load disturbance, or excessive control-loop gain.

14. Why should encoder cables be routed carefully?

Electromagnetic interference can corrupt feedback signals and cause position errors, alarms, or unstable motion.

15. Is a servo motor always better than an induction motor with a VFD?

No. The correct choice depends on motion precision, dynamic response, cost, operating duty, and control requirements.

19. Practical exercises

Exercise 1: Identify the motor system

Choose a servo-driven axis from an industrial machine and record:

  • Motor model and rated power.
  • Rated torque and speed.
  • Drive model.
  • Feedback type.
  • Brake and thermal sensor details, if present.
  • Gearbox or coupling arrangement.

Use the manufacturer's nameplate and manuals rather than guessing from appearance.

Exercise 2: Investigate a following-error alarm

Build a diagnostic record containing:

  • Alarm code and timestamp.
  • Commanded versus actual position.
  • Commanded speed and acceleration.
  • Motor current or torque demand.
  • Mechanical condition.
  • Feedback and drive status.

Determine whether the evidence points toward a mechanical, electrical, feedback, or parameter issue.

Exercise 3: Evaluate a motor replacement

Compare the original and proposed motor for torque, speed, inertia, feedback compatibility, mounting, shaft dimensions, brake, thermal sensor, and drive compatibility. A similar power rating alone is insufficient.

Exercise 4: Observe the control response

During an approved controlled test, compare commanded and actual position or speed using the drive's diagnostics. Observe acceleration, deceleration, settling, and any following error. Do not alter tuning parameters on a production machine without authorization and a rollback plan.

20. Final mental model

  • The servo motor produces mechanical torque and motion.
  • The servo drive controls the electrical power supplied to the motor.
  • The feedback device reports motion or rotor position.
  • The controller determines the required motion profile.
  • The mechanics determine how effectively that motion reaches the load.
  • The complete system determines positioning performance, repeatability, stability, and reliability.

A servo system is only as capable as its weakest part. Excellent motor specifications cannot compensate for a loose coupling, incorrect feedback configuration, poor tuning, or an improperly sized mechanical load.
