1. Definition of positioning

Positioning is the controlled movement of a machine axis from its current position to a target position, using defined motion parameters such as speed, acceleration, deceleration, and stopping behavior.

A positioning system must do more than move a motor. It must determine where the axis is, calculate how it should move, execute the movement, and verify whether the target has been reached within the required tolerance.

Examples in industrial automation include:

  • Moving a robotic transfer unit to a loading station.
  • Positioning a CNC axis at a programmed coordinate.
  • Moving a conveyor indexing table to the next station.
  • Positioning a welding gun or fixture.
  • Moving a linear actuator to a specified distance.
  • Rotating a component to a defined angle for assembly.

A servo motor provides the motion, but positioning depends on the complete system: controller, drive, feedback, mechanics, parameters, and machine reference.

The exact feedback path depends on the architecture. Many servo drives close the motor's current, velocity, and position-related loops internally, while a PLC or motion controller issues higher-level motion commands.

The controller compares the commanded position with the actual position, directly or through drive diagnostics, and determines whether the move has completed successfully.

4. Types of positioning

4.1 Point-to-point positioning

The axis moves from one target to another. The path between the points is generally not the main controlled objective, provided the movement remains within the permitted operating envelope.

Examples:

  • A pneumatic-style transfer mechanism implemented with an electric servo axis.
  • A pick-and-place unit moving between pickup and placement locations.
  • A rotary indexer moving between fixed stations.

Point-to-point positioning is common where the axis must reach a destination accurately, but does not need to follow a continuously specified path.

4.2 Continuous-path positioning

The system controls the motion path over time, not just the start and end points.

Examples:

  • CNC contour machining.
  • Robotic welding.
  • Coordinated motion of multiple axes.
  • Cutting or dispensing while moving.

Continuous-path control requires suitable interpolation, synchronization, and coordinated motion capabilities. A simple single-axis move instruction is not sufficient for every such application.

4.3 Absolute positioning

The target is specified as a coordinate in the configured position system.

Example:

  • Current position: 100 mm.
  • Target position: 450 mm.
  • Requested movement: to coordinate 450 mm.

The controller calculates the required movement based on the current coordinate and target coordinate.

The word absolute here describes the positioning command, not necessarily the encoder type. An absolute positioning command can be used in a system with incremental feedback, provided the machine has established a valid reference position.

4.4 Relative positioning

The target is specified as a distance or increment from the current position.

Example:

  • Current position: 100 mm.
  • Relative command: +50 mm.
  • Final target: 150 mm.

A negative relative command moves in the opposite direction according to the configured coordinate convention.

Relative moves are useful for repetitive indexing and incremental adjustments.

4.5 Absolute versus relative commands

Feature Absolute move Relative move
Reference Configured coordinate system Current position when the command is evaluated
Example Move to 500 mm Move another 50 mm
Typical use Station coordinates and programmed locations Indexing and incremental corrections
Main risk Incorrect or invalid machine reference Repeated increments can accumulate unexpected travel
Key check Verify coordinate system and reference validity Verify starting position, command timing, and permitted travel

A relative command does not inherently guarantee a safe movement. Repeated relative commands can drive an axis toward a limit if the control logic does not check the resulting position.

5. Motion profile: acceleration, constant speed, and deceleration

A positioning move typically has three phases:

  1. Acceleration: the axis increases speed.
  2. Constant-speed travel: the axis moves at the commanded speed if enough distance is available.
  3. Deceleration: the axis reduces speed to reach the target.

A simplified trapezoidal velocity profile looks like this:

Velocity
   ^
   |         __________
   |        /          \
   |       /            \
   |______/              \______> Time
       Accel   Constant   Decel
                speed

For short movements, the axis may not reach the commanded maximum speed. It accelerates and then decelerates immediately. This is often called a triangular velocity profile.

5.1 Why motion profiles matter

The profile affects:

  • Cycle time.
  • Motor torque and current demand.
  • Mechanical stress.
  • Vibration and resonance.
  • Positioning stability.
  • Load movement and product handling.
  • Regenerative energy during deceleration.

A faster command is not always a faster production cycle. If it creates excessive vibration or settling time, the total cycle can become longer.

6. Position, speed, and acceleration relationships

For constant acceleration, the relationship between velocity, acceleration, and distance is:

\[ v^2=u^2+2as \]

Where:

  • \(u\) = initial velocity.
  • \(v\) = final velocity.
  • \(a\) = constant acceleration.
  • \(s\) = displacement.

For a move starting and ending at rest, the ideal distance required to accelerate to speed \(v\) at acceleration \(a\), then decelerate at the same magnitude, is:

\[ s\_{\text{total}}=\frac{v^2}{a} \]

This assumes symmetric acceleration and deceleration, no jerk limitation, and sufficient distance for the constant-acceleration profile.

Example

An axis must move 200 mm with a maximum speed of 200 mm/s and acceleration of 500 mm/s².

The ideal distance needed to accelerate and decelerate is:

\[ s\_{\text{total}}=\frac{200^2}{500}=80\text{ mm} \]

Since the available distance is 200 mm, the axis can reach the commanded speed under these simplified assumptions.

If the required movement were only 40 mm, it could not reach 200 mm/s using the same acceleration and deceleration limits. The controller would need to use a lower peak speed.

Real systems may also apply jerk limits, torque limits, speed limits, and additional machine-specific constraints.

7. Jerk and smooth motion

Jerk is the rate of change of acceleration.

\[ j=\frac{da}{dt} \]

A sudden change in acceleration creates high jerk. This can excite mechanical resonances and produce vibration, noise, or load disturbance.

Motion controllers may use S-curve profiles to limit jerk and make acceleration changes smoother.

Trapezoidal versus S-curve profiles

Feature Trapezoidal profile S-curve profile
Acceleration changes Can change abruptly at profile transitions Changes more gradually
Jerk Higher at idealized transition points Limited according to the configured profile
Motion time Often shorter for equivalent basic limits May take longer, depending on settings
Mechanical impact Can be greater in sensitive systems Often lower
Typical application General positioning Sensitive loads, high-performance axes, vibration reduction

S-curve motion is not automatically superior for every application. The correct profile depends on mechanical stiffness, payload, cycle time, and drive capabilities.

8. Servo positioning control

A typical servo system uses several control loops.

8.1 Current or torque control

The innermost loop controls motor current, which is closely related to motor torque within the operating range.

It responds quickly to changes in load and motion demand.

8.2 Velocity control

The velocity loop controls speed based on the difference between commanded and actual velocity.

It adjusts the torque demand to accelerate, decelerate, and regulate the axis.

8.3 Position control

The position loop regulates the difference between the target trajectory and measured position.

It generates a motion demand intended to reduce position error.

The exact implementation and location of these loops vary by drive and motion controller. Some architectures split functions between the drive and controller.

8.4 Position error

Using a target-minus-actual convention:

\[ e_p=x\_{\text{target}}-x\_{\text{actual}} \]

If the target is 250 mm and actual position is 247 mm:

\[ e_p=250-247=3\text{ mm} \]

The axis has 3 mm remaining in the positive direction under this convention.

During motion, the commanded position is changing, so following error should be interpreted in relation to the commanded trajectory, drive configuration, and operating conditions.

9. Position scaling and engineering units

The controller must convert encoder information into usable machine coordinates, such as millimetres, degrees, or revolutions.

For a rotary axis:

\[ \theta=\frac{C}{C\_{\text{rev}}}\times360^\circ \]

Where:

  • \(C\) = measured counts from a defined reference.
  • \(C\_{\text{rev}}\) = effective counts per revolution according to the receiver's decoding configuration.

For a linear axis driven by a leadscrew:

\[ x=\frac{C}{C\_{\text{rev}}}\times L \]

Where:

  • \(x\) = linear displacement.
  • \(L\) = linear travel per motor revolution after accounting for the transmission.
  • \(C\_{\text{rev}}\) = effective counts per motor revolution.

This simplified relationship assumes a consistent transmission ratio and no significant slip or compliance.

Example: leadscrew axis

Assume:

  • Effective feedback resolution: 10,000 counts per motor revolution.
  • Leadscrew travel: 5 mm per motor revolution.

Then:

\[ \text{Distance per count}=\frac{5}{10000}=0.0005\text{ mm} \]

The theoretical feedback increment is 0.0005 mm per count.

This does not mean the machine is accurate to 0.0005 mm. Backlash, screw lead error, thermal expansion, mounting, and other mechanical effects may be much larger.

Common scaling errors

  • Incorrect encoder resolution.
  • Confusing encoder cycles with decoded counts.
  • Incorrect gearbox ratio.
  • Incorrect leadscrew pitch.
  • Incorrect units conversion.
  • Incorrect direction sign.
  • Wrong scaling between PLC and drive.
  • Using a scaling parameter intended for another feedback interface.

A scaling error can make the axis move farther or less than commanded, even when the motor and encoder are healthy.

10. Single-axis and multi-axis positioning

10.1 Single-axis positioning

One axis moves to a specified position.

Example: a transfer slide moves from the loading point to a fixture position.

The controller checks the target, permitted travel, motion profile, and completion criteria.

10.2 Coordinated multi-axis positioning

Two or more axes move together to achieve a coordinated result.

Examples:

  • X and Y axes on a CNC machine.
  • Robot axes moving along a programmed path.
  • A gantry using two synchronized motors.
  • A flying cutter synchronized with a moving conveyor.

Coordinated motion requires suitable controller support and correct configuration of axis relationships, synchronization, and kinematics.

Two motors starting at the same time does not necessarily mean they are synchronized correctly.

11. Positioning modes and command execution

Motion controllers and servo drives provide different motion commands. Common command concepts include:

  • Absolute move.
  • Relative move.
  • Jog movement.
  • Velocity-controlled movement.
  • Homing or reference movement.
  • Synchronized or interpolated movement.
  • Stop or controlled deceleration.
  • Emergency or safety-related stop functions, as supported by the system.

The exact instruction names depend on the platform. For example, PLC motion libraries may use commands such as MC_MoveAbsolute, MC_MoveRelative, MC_Home, and MC_Stop, but the available commands and behavior depend on the controller, firmware, and motion library.

Typical command lifecycle

A well-designed sequence checks more than whether the command instruction was triggered. It should distinguish between command acceptance, motion in progress, completion, and fault.

12. In-position and move-complete signals

A controller may provide status signals such as:

  • Busy or active.
  • Done or complete.
  • In-position.
  • Error.
  • Command aborted.
  • Limit reached.
  • Axis enabled.
  • Homed or reference valid.

These signals have different meanings.

For example, a command-complete status may indicate that the commanded profile finished. An in-position status may additionally depend on configured position tolerance, velocity threshold, and settling behavior.

The exact semantics must be confirmed in the platform's documentation.

Typical PLC sequence

Start request
     |
     v
Validate axis ready
     |
     v
Issue positioning command
     |
     v
Monitor Busy / Done / Error
     |
     +---- Error ------> Fault handling
     |
     +---- Done -------> Verify required conditions
                              |
                              v
                       Continue sequence

Avoid relying only on a fixed timer, such as waiting two seconds and assuming the axis has arrived. A timer can be used as a timeout, but motion status and fault checks should govern the sequence.

13. Travel limits and position boundaries

Positioning systems commonly use software travel limits and, where fitted, physical limit switches or sensors.

13.1 Software limits

Software limits restrict commanded movement to a configured coordinate range.

Example:

  • Minimum position: 0 mm.
  • Maximum position: 800 mm.

A command to move to 900 mm should be rejected or handled according to the configured motion system.

Software limits depend on valid position feedback and correct configuration. They are not a substitute for required safety-related hardware or safety functions.

13.2 Hardware limits

Physical limit switches, proximity sensors, or other devices can detect a boundary or end-of-travel condition.

Their function may be operational, protective, or safety-related depending on the design and certification.

A conventional limit switch must not automatically be treated as a safety-rated device.

13.3 Soft-limit design considerations

  • Verify the reference position before enabling coordinate-dependent movement.
  • Ensure minimum and maximum values match the physical machine travel.
  • Include stopping distance and mechanical overtravel considerations.
  • Check behavior for a target outside the permitted range.
  • Check how the controller behaves if feedback becomes invalid.
  • Ensure the safety design does not rely solely on ordinary PLC software limits where independent safety functions are required.

14. Positioning accuracy and repeatability

These are related but different performance measures.

Accuracy describes how close the actual position is to the intended or calibrated target.

Repeatability describes how consistently the system reaches the same position over repeated attempts under comparable conditions.

A machine can have good repeatability but poor accuracy. For example, it may repeatedly stop 0.4 mm away from the true target.

Factors affecting positioning performance include:

  • Encoder resolution and accuracy.
  • Feedback mounting and alignment.
  • Backlash in gears or screws.
  • Mechanical compliance.
  • Coupling slip.
  • Bearing condition.
  • Thermal expansion.
  • Payload variation.
  • Control-loop tuning.
  • Acceleration and deceleration settings.
  • Vibration and settling behavior.
  • Calibration and coordinate offsets.

Direct versus indirect feedback

A motor-mounted encoder measures motor motion. It does not necessarily measure the actual position of the final load.

For example, if a gearbox has backlash, the motor can reach the requested angle while the output mechanism remains offset.

A direct load-side measurement can improve knowledge of actual load position, but requires suitable sensor placement, system architecture, and control support.

16. Troubleshooting sequence for a positioning problem

Use this order to separate control, feedback, and mechanical causes.

  1. Record the fault code and the exact motion command.
  2. Verify that the axis is enabled and the controller considers its position reference valid.
  3. Check the commanded target, actual position, and units.
  4. Confirm encoder resolution, gear ratio, and scaling.
  5. Review speed, acceleration, deceleration, jerk, and torque limits.
  6. Check software-limit and hardware-limit status.
  7. Inspect mechanical couplings, transmission, guides, bearings, and load.
  8. Review feedback diagnostics and following error.
  9. Check the configured in-position criteria and completion status.
  10. Validate any parameter changes under an approved controlled test.

Do not increase torque limits or disable limits simply because the axis cannot complete a move. That can conceal an obstruction, scaling fault, or incorrect machine reference.

17. Safety considerations

Positioning faults can cause unexpected motion, crushing, impact, or movement of suspended loads.

  • Follow the machine's approved operating and maintenance procedures.
  • Verify guarding, interlocks, and required safety functions before testing.
  • Keep personnel outside hazardous movement zones.
  • Secure gravity-loaded or vertical axes against uncontrolled movement.
  • Do not use an ordinary software limit as the sole safety measure when the risk assessment requires an independent safety function.
  • Confirm the reference position before coordinate-dependent motion.
  • Test direction and scaling at controlled low speed after changes.
  • Do not bypass following-error, encoder, limit, or drive faults without an approved diagnostic and safety process.
  • Make parameter changes under the site's authorization and change-control requirements.

The target coordinate is only meaningful when the system knows its reference and the machine's safety conditions are valid.

18. Interview questions and answers

1. What is positioning in servo control?

Controlled movement of an axis to a target coordinate using defined motion parameters and feedback.

2. What is the difference between absolute and relative positioning?

An absolute command specifies a target in the configured coordinate system. A relative command specifies a movement increment from the current position.

3. What is a motion profile?

The planned relationship between position, speed, acceleration, and time during a movement.

4. What is the difference between a trapezoidal and an S-curve profile?

A trapezoidal profile generally changes acceleration abruptly at transitions, while an S-curve profile limits jerk by changing acceleration more gradually.

5. What is following error?

The difference between commanded and actual position, interpreted according to the control system's configured convention.

6. What is an in-position signal?

A status indicating that configured positioning conditions are satisfied. The exact conditions depend on the controller or drive.

7. Why might a servo axis overshoot?

Possible causes include incorrect tuning, unsuitable deceleration settings, excessive inertia, mechanical resonance, or incorrect motion parameters.

8. Does high encoder resolution guarantee high positioning accuracy?

No. Mechanical backlash, compliance, thermal effects, calibration, feedback location, and tuning also affect accuracy.

9. Why are software limits used?

They restrict commanded movement within configured coordinate boundaries. They do not replace required independent safety functions.

10. What is the difference between accuracy and repeatability?

Accuracy is closeness to the true target. Repeatability is consistency when returning to the same target under comparable conditions.

11. Why is homing important for absolute positioning commands?

The controller needs a valid machine reference to interpret coordinates correctly. An absolute encoder may preserve position information, but that does not automatically establish or validate the machine coordinate system.

12. Why might an axis reach the target but fail to settle?

Vibration, poor tuning, excessive jerk, flexible mechanics, or overly strict settling criteria may prevent the axis from meeting the configured in-position conditions.

13. What is a triangular velocity profile?

A profile in which the axis accelerates and then decelerates without reaching a constant maximum-speed phase because the move is too short.

14. What is inertia matching?

Evaluating the motor and reflected load inertia to determine whether the system can achieve the required acceleration, response, and stability.

15. Why should move completion not be based only on a fixed timer?

Actual move duration changes with load, faults, and motion conditions. The sequence should use motion status and fault handling, with a timer serving as a timeout where appropriate.

19. Practical exercises

Exercise 1: Calculate a position increment

A rotary axis uses 20,000 effective counts per revolution. The axis rotates 90 degrees.

Calculate the ideal count increment:

\[ C=20000\times\frac{90}{360} \]
\[ C=5000\text{ counts} \]

This assumes the stated resolution is already the effective decoded count value.

Exercise 2: Investigate an inaccurate linear axis

A transfer slide is commanded to move 300 mm but physically travels approximately 270 mm.

Investigate:

  • Command units and scaling.
  • Encoder resolution.
  • Gearbox ratio and leadscrew pitch.
  • Actual position feedback versus physical load position.
  • Coupling slip or transmission problems.
  • Calibration and coordinate offsets.

Do not assume that the encoder is defective merely because the physical travel differs from the command.

Exercise 3: Investigate a slow production cycle

An axis reaches its target quickly but waits before the next machine operation starts.

Check:

  • Whether the controller is waiting for an in-position status.
  • Whether settling time is excessive.
  • Whether the sequence waits on an unnecessary fixed timer.
  • Whether the motion profile creates vibration.
  • Whether a mechanical or feedback problem prevents stable completion.

The correct improvement may be in sequence logic, tuning, or mechanics, not simply a higher commanded speed.

Exercise 4: Define a positioning specification

For one machine axis, record:

Maximum speed Permitted operating speed
Acceleration/deceleration Required dynamic limits
Jerk or profile type If supported and specified

20. Final mental model

Positioning is the complete process of moving an axis to a defined coordinate and confirming that the required motion conditions have been met.

  • Target position defines where the axis should go.
  • Motion profile defines how it should move.
  • Servo drive and motor execute the motion.
  • Feedback reports actual movement.
  • Scaling and mechanics determine the relationship between feedback and physical position.
  • In-position criteria determine when the controller considers the move complete.
  • Limits and safety functions constrain movement and protect people and equipment.

When an axis does not reach its target correctly, investigate coordinate scaling, motion parameters, feedback, mechanics, and completion logic as separate parts of the system.
