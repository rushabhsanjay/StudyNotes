1. What is synchronization?

Synchronization is the coordinated operation of two or more axes, motors, conveyors or machine mechanisms so that their movements maintain a required timing or positional relationship.

In industrial automation, synchronization ensures that one movement occurs at the correct time and in the correct relationship to another movement.

Examples include:

  • A conveyor and a feeder moving at coordinated speeds.
  • Two servo motors driving opposite sides of a gantry.
  • A robot picking a component from a moving conveyor.
  • A flying cutoff system cutting material while the material continues moving.
  • A press feeder delivering a sheet at the correct point in the press cycle.
  • A master conveyor controlling the speed of a follower conveyor.

Synchronization is more than starting two motors at the same time. Two motors can start simultaneously and still drift apart because of differences in speed, acceleration, loading or feedback.

The required relationship must be maintained throughout the relevant operation.

The exact implementation varies. Some systems use a dedicated motion controller, while others use PLC motion functions, drive-integrated synchronization or mechanical coupling.

The master can be a physical axis, a virtual axis, a conveyor encoder, a line-speed reference or a calculated process value.

Important distinction: Synchronization defines the relationship between motions. The servo control loops still need to regulate each axis accurately.

3. Three basic forms of synchronization

Type What must match? Example
Position synchronization Position relationship Two gantry motors maintain alignment
Velocity synchronization Speed relationship Two conveyors run at a defined speed ratio
Time synchronization Movement timing A clamp closes at a defined point in the machine cycle

A system can require more than one form simultaneously. For example, two axes may need to maintain a fixed position offset while moving at the same velocity.

3.1 Position synchronization

Position synchronization means that the axes maintain a required relationship between their positions.

For example, a dual-drive gantry may have two servo motors driving opposite sides of a common carriage. If one side moves farther than the other, the gantry can skew, bind or damage its mechanical structure.

The controller can compare the positions of both axes and correct the difference.

A simple position error is:

\[ e=x_1-x_2 \]

Where:

  • \(x_1\) is the position of axis 1.
  • \(x_2\) is the position of axis 2.
  • \(e\) is their position difference.

If both axes should have identical coordinates, the desired difference is zero.

However, the allowable difference depends on the mechanical arrangement, calibration and control strategy. A nonzero offset may be intentional.

3.2 Velocity synchronization

Velocity synchronization means that two axes maintain a defined speed relationship.

For equal-speed synchronization:

\[ v_2=v_1 \]

For ratio-based synchronization:

\[ v_2=k v_1 \]

Where \(k\) is the required speed ratio.

For example, if a follower conveyor must run at 0.8 times the master conveyor's speed:

\[ v_2=0.8v_1 \]

If the master runs at 500 mm/s, the follower's target velocity is 400 mm/s.

This relationship can be useful for feeders, conveyors, unwinders, winders and other continuous production equipment.

3.3 Time synchronization

Time synchronization coordinates events against a common timeline or machine-cycle reference.

Examples include:

  • Closing a gripper after a robot reaches a pickup position.
  • Triggering an inspection camera when a component reaches a known position.
  • Starting a feeder at a specified point in a press cycle.
  • Activating a cutting mechanism at the correct material location.

A fixed delay can be sufficient when movement is predictable and the timing requirement is genuinely time-based. When the event must occur at a particular machine position, position-based triggering is usually more robust than a timer alone.

4. Master-follower control

Master-follower is one of the most common synchronization arrangements.

The master provides the reference. The follower adjusts its movement to maintain the required relationship with that reference.

Master position:  0 -> 100 -> 200 -> 300 mm
                       |
                       v
Follower reference: 0 -> 100 -> 200 -> 300 mm

This illustrates a 1:1 position relationship with zero offset. Real systems also account for scaling, gearing, phase offset, coordinate systems and startup conditions.

4.1 Physical master

A physical master is a real axis or mechanism whose motion determines the reference for other axes.

Examples:

  • A conveyor encoder.
  • A press crank encoder.
  • A main drive motor's position feedback.
  • A rotary table.

The follower tracks the actual reference movement, not merely an independent speed command.

4.2 Virtual master

A virtual master is a position or motion reference generated in software. It may represent a simulated line shaft or a coordinated machine cycle.

Advantages include:

  • Multiple axes can reference a common motion source.
  • The machine sequence can be coordinated without one physical motor serving as the reference.
  • The reference profile can be designed centrally.

A virtual master must still be managed correctly during startup, stopping, recovery and mode changes. Its position and velocity are software-generated values, not direct measurements of a physical mechanism.

4.3 Why actual master feedback matters

Suppose a master conveyor is commanded to run at 500 mm/s but slows to 430 mm/s because of a load change.

A follower tracking only the commanded speed may continue at 500 mm/s and lose the required relationship.

A follower tracking the appropriate actual master reference can adapt to the slowdown, subject to the controller architecture and configured limits.

This is one reason electronic synchronization can outperform two independent speed commands.

5. Electronic gearing

Electronic gearing makes a follower axis follow the position or velocity of a master axis according to a configured ratio.

The relationship can be expressed as:

\[ x_f=kx_m+b \]

Where:

  • \(x_f\) is the follower position.
  • \(x_m\) is the master position.
  • \(k\) is the electronic gear ratio.
  • \(b\) is a configured position offset.

For a simple 1:1 relationship with no offset:

\[ x_f=x_m \]

For a ratio of 2:1:

\[ x_f=2x_m \]

This means that, in the chosen coordinate units, the follower travels twice the master displacement. Whether the configuration is called 2:1 or 1:2 depends on the platform's ratio convention, so verify the actual definition in the motion controller.

Example: feeder and main conveyor

Suppose a feeder must travel 120 mm for every 200 mm of master movement.

\[ k=\frac{120}{200}=0.6 \]

If the master moves 500 mm, the ideal follower displacement is:

\[ x_f=0.6(500)=300\text{ mm} \]

This is a displacement relationship, not necessarily a guarantee that the follower can physically achieve the requested movement under every load or speed condition.

Electronic gearing versus mechanical gearing

Feature Electronic gearing Mechanical gearing
Ratio adjustment Usually configurable in software Depends on physical gear arrangement
Mechanical connection Axes may be mechanically independent Axes are linked through gears or transmission
Flexibility High Limited by installed hardware
Feedback dependency Depends on reference and control design Mechanical ratio establishes the relationship, but backlash and compliance remain
Fault considerations Encoder, communication, control and drive faults Wear, backlash, lubrication, alignment and mechanical damage

Electronic gearing does not eliminate mechanical limitations. Belts, gearboxes, couplings and shafts still introduce compliance, backlash, wear and load-dependent behaviour.

6. Electronic camming

Electronic camming coordinates a follower according to a programmed relationship between master position and follower position.

Unlike a fixed gear ratio, the relationship can vary throughout the master cycle.

A cam table defines how the follower should move at different master positions.

Master position
      |
      v
Cam table / interpolation
      |
      v
Follower position reference
      |
      v
Follower motion control

Example: flying cutoff

In a flying cutoff system, material continuously moves along a production line. A cutting mechanism must accelerate to match the material speed, cut the material, and then return to its starting region.

A simplified cycle is:

  1. Wait for the correct master position.
  2. Accelerate the cutting axis.
  3. Synchronize its speed with the moving material.
  4. Execute the cutting operation during the permitted position window.
  5. Decouple from the master reference.
  6. Return the cutting axis for the next cycle.

If the cutter is not synchronized during cutting, the material may be pulled, distorted or cut at the wrong location.

Electronic camming is also used in packaging machines, printing, rotary cutters, filling systems and assembly equipment.

7. Phase synchronization

Phase describes the relative position or timing offset between coordinated axes.

Two axes can have the same velocity but still be out of phase.

For example, two rotating mechanisms may rotate at identical RPM while one is angularly ahead of the other.

For a rotary system, phase difference can be expressed as:

\[ \Delta\theta=\theta_1-\theta_2 \]

For a system requiring a fixed phase offset:

\[ \theta_2=\theta_1+\theta\_{\text{offset}} \]

The offset may be intentional. For example, a feeder might need to deliver material 30° ahead of a press's reference position.

In linear systems, a similar offset can be expressed in millimetres.

Key point: Equal velocity does not guarantee correct phase. Position or phase feedback is needed when the relative position matters.

8. Synchronization during acceleration and deceleration

A follower must often match the master's movement during changing speed, not just during steady-state operation.

Suppose a master conveyor accelerates from 0 to 500 mm/s. Its follower must maintain a 0.8 speed ratio.

The follower's ideal velocity reference is:

\[ v_f=0.8v_m \]

If the master reaches 500 mm/s, the follower target becomes 400 mm/s.

If the master accelerates, the follower must respond to the changing reference while respecting its own acceleration, jerk, torque and speed limits.

If the follower cannot keep up, a synchronization error may develop.

Common causes include:

  • Follower acceleration limits being too low.
  • Incorrect gear ratio or scaling.
  • Excessive load or mechanical binding.
  • Incorrect master feedback selection.
  • Poor servo tuning.
  • Encoder or communication faults.
  • Incorrect engagement timing.

The correct response is not automatically to increase acceleration. First determine whether the limitation is caused by the profile, drive, mechanics, feedback or control configuration.

9. Coordinating two motors on one mechanical axis

A gantry or wide transfer unit may use two servo motors to drive opposite sides of the same structure.

Both motors must move together to avoid skewing.

A simple conceptual control structure is:

             Common Position Reference
                       |
               +-------+-------+
               |               |
               v               v
          Axis 1 target    Axis 2 target
               |               |
               v               v
          Servo drive 1    Servo drive 2
               |               |
               v               v
          Motor 1          Motor 2
               |               |
               v               v
          Encoder 1        Encoder 2
               |               |
               +-------+-------+
                       |
                       v
                Skew / mismatch
                  monitoring

In practice, the controller may use cross-coupled correction, gantry-specific control functions or another coordinated-axis strategy.

Important engineering requirements

  • Both axes need correctly configured feedback and coordinate scaling.
  • Homing and coordinate references must be consistent.
  • The mechanical structure must be aligned.
  • Position mismatch limits must reflect the actual machine design.
  • Startup, fault recovery and re-engagement must be handled safely.
  • A failed drive or encoder must not cause the remaining axis to force the structure into a skewed condition.

Two motors driving one structure are not automatically a synchronized gantry just because they receive identical commands.

10. Synchronization error and following error

These terms describe different problems.

Following error is the difference between a commanded axis position and its actual measured position.

\[ e_f=x\_{\text{command}}-x\_{\text{actual}} \]

Synchronization error is the difference between the actual relationship of multiple axes and the relationship that the machine requires.

For two axes expected to have identical positions:

\[ e_s=x_1-x_2 \]

For axes with a defined ratio and offset:

\[ e_s=x_f-(kx_m+b) \]

A system can have small individual following errors but still have a synchronization problem if the relationship between the axes is incorrect. Conversely, both axes can track the same incorrect reference well and still produce a process-position error.

The controller's definitions and sign conventions must be checked before interpreting diagnostic values.

11. Synchronization hardware and signals

Component Purpose Typical failure
Encoder Measures position or speed Missing pulses, noise, incorrect scaling
Motion controller Calculates coordinated references Configuration or execution fault
Servo drive Controls motor response Following error, current or voltage fault
Network or fieldbus Exchanges commands and feedback Delayed or lost communication
Master reference Provides the motion source Incorrect signal, lost reference, invalid scaling
Mechanical transmission Transfers movement Backlash, slip, wear, misalignment
Home/reference sensor Establishes a coordinate reference Incorrect switching point or wiring fault
Safety system Enforces safety functions Safety fault prevents permitted movement

Not every architecture depends on every component. A mechanically synchronized system may use a common shaft, while an electronic system may depend on encoders, network timing and motion-controller functionality.

Encoder scaling

Incorrect scaling can produce a persistent synchronization error even when the hardware is functioning correctly.

For example, if one axis reports 1,000 counts/mm and the other is configured for 950 counts/mm, the calculated positions will diverge as the axes travel, even if the physical movement is mechanically correct.

Check:

  • Encoder resolution.
  • Gear ratio.
  • Travel per motor revolution.
  • Engineering-unit conversion.
  • Direction and sign convention.
  • Master and follower coordinate definitions.

12. PLC and motion-controller implementation

A generic synchronization sequence is:

Validate master and follower axes
              |
              v
Check homing / reference status
              |
              v
Check safety and process permissives
              |
              v
Configure ratio, offset or cam table
              |
              v
Enable synchronization
              |
              v
Monitor active / synchronized status
              |
              v
Monitor position and velocity errors
              |
              v
Continue operation or execute fault response

Depending on the platform, motion libraries may provide functions for electronic gearing, camming, synchronized motion and disengagement. Exact instruction names and parameter behaviour vary between Siemens, Rockwell, Mitsubishi and other systems.

Implementation rules

  1. Confirm the master and follower coordinate systems before engagement.
  2. Ensure both axes have valid references where the application requires them.
  3. Verify gear ratios, offsets and cam-table units.
  4. Confirm the follower can meet the master's expected speed and acceleration.
  5. Monitor actual synchronization status rather than assuming engagement succeeded.
  6. Apply defined tolerances and timeout handling.
  7. Handle communication loss, drive faults and interrupted motion deliberately.
  8. Validate startup and recovery, not just normal production operation.

A synchronization command being accepted does not necessarily mean that the axes are already synchronized. Some controllers need time to acquire the reference or execute a catch-up movement.

13. Common synchronization faults and troubleshooting

Symptom Initial checks Likely causes
Follower drifts from master Master and follower actual positions Incorrect ratio, scaling or reference
Speed matches but position does not Phase/offset error Incorrect engagement position or offset
Follower cannot synchronize during ramp Acceleration and velocity trends Follower limits, load, tuning
Gantry skews Difference between axis feedback values Encoder scaling, drive fault, mechanical binding
Synchronization drops intermittently Controller status and communication diagnostics Network interruption, invalid master reference
Motion is reversed Direction and sign conventions Incorrect polarity or coordinate mapping
Cam cycle is incorrect Master position versus cam-table output Wrong table, interpolation or cycle scaling
Position mismatch grows with travel Error trend against distance Incorrect units, gear ratio or encoder scaling
Axes synchronize but process timing is wrong Reference event and phase offset Incorrect trigger position or process sequence
Follower moves unexpectedly on engagement Current position, engagement mode and reference Incorrect catch-up strategy or coordinate mismatch

Recommended diagnostic order

  1. Record the exact PLC, motion-controller and drive alarms.
  2. Confirm the master reference is valid and moving as expected.
  3. Compare master and follower commanded and actual positions.
  4. Check velocity ratio, position offset and coordinate scaling.
  5. Review engagement status and the synchronization error trend.
  6. Inspect encoders, communication, mechanical transmission and load.
  7. Verify the configured fault response and recovery procedure.
  8. Test one approved change at a time under controlled conditions.

A useful diagnostic question is whether the error is constant, increasing with distance, or increasing during acceleration:

  • A constant error often suggests a phase or offset issue.
  • An error that grows with distance often suggests a ratio or scaling issue.
  • An error that grows during acceleration may indicate a dynamic limitation, load problem or tuning issue.

These patterns are diagnostic clues, not definitive proof.

14. Practical industrial examples

Example A: Two conveyors

A main conveyor moves at 600 mm/s. A feeder conveyor must move at 0.75 times the master speed.

\[ v_f=0.75(600)=450\text{ mm/s} \]

The feeder target is 450 mm/s. The control system must also accommodate changes in master speed and verify that the follower can respond within its limits.

Example B: Gantry alignment

Two motors drive opposite sides of a gantry. The controller expects both measured positions to match within an approved tolerance.

If the position mismatch increases during movement, check feedback scaling, drive status, mechanical alignment and the controller's coordinated-axis strategy. Do not keep running the gantry if the mismatch creates a risk of binding or structural damage.

Example C: Flying cutoff

Material moves continuously. The cutter must match material speed during the cut window.

The synchronization controller coordinates cutter velocity and position against the material reference. Incorrect phase can produce a misplaced cut; insufficient speed matching can distort the material or produce poor cut quality.

Example D: Robot picking from a moving conveyor

The robot must estimate or receive the component's position from the conveyor tracking system and coordinate its movement with the conveyor reference.

Accurate tracking depends on encoder data, coordinate calibration, communication, timing and the robot's motion limits. A stationary-coordinate pick command alone cannot account for the component continuing to move.

15. Safety considerations

Synchronization faults can create mechanical hazards. A follower may move unexpectedly when it engages a master reference, or one axis may continue moving after another axis faults.

  • Follow the machine's risk assessment and approved commissioning procedure.
  • Do not bypass guards, interlocks or safety functions to test synchronization.
  • Verify permitted motion during startup, homing, engagement and recovery.
  • Check the behaviour of the complete mechanism if one drive or encoder fails.
  • Validate gantry skew limits and stopping behaviour.
  • Confirm that safety-rated functions are implemented by the approved safety architecture.
  • Treat normal PLC synchronization logic as operational control, not as a substitute for safety-rated protection.

16. Interview questions and answers

1. What is synchronization in motion control?

It is the coordinated movement of multiple axes so that they maintain a required position, velocity, phase or timing relationship.

2. Is starting two motors simultaneously sufficient for synchronization?

No. They can drift apart due to speed differences, load variation, acceleration limits, feedback errors or mechanical differences.

3. What is a master-follower arrangement?

The master supplies a motion reference, and the follower adjusts its motion to maintain the configured relationship.

4. What is electronic gearing?

A control function that maintains a defined position or velocity ratio between a follower and a master.

5. What is electronic camming?

A function that defines a potentially nonlinear relationship between master position and follower position over a motion cycle.

6. What is phase offset?

A defined positional or angular difference between coordinated axes.

7. Can two axes have the same speed but be out of synchronization?

Yes. They may have the same velocity but an incorrect relative position or phase.

8. What is the difference between following error and synchronization error?

Following error compares a single axis's command with its actual position. Synchronization error compares the actual relationship between axes with the required relationship.

9. Why does a follower lose synchronization during acceleration?

Possible causes include insufficient acceleration capability, torque limits, excessive load, incorrect scaling, tuning problems or an invalid master reference.

10. Why is a virtual master used?

It provides a software-generated common motion reference for coordinating multiple axes without requiring one physical axis to serve as the master.

11. Why might synchronization error increase with travel distance?

An incorrect gear ratio, encoder scaling or engineering-unit conversion can cause the position mismatch to accumulate.

12. What is a typical application of electronic camming?

Flying cutoff, packaging, printing, rotary indexing and other machines that require a follower to follow a programmed position relationship with a master.

13. Why is a common position reference useful for a gantry?

It coordinates both drives and helps maintain alignment of the shared mechanical structure.

14. What should be checked when a follower moves unexpectedly during engagement?

Check the initial positions, reference validity, phase offset, engagement mode, catch-up behaviour and configured motion limits.

15. Why should synchronization status be monitored?

The command may be accepted before the axes have acquired the required relationship. Status and error monitoring confirm whether the expected state has actually been achieved.

17. Practical exercises

Exercise 1: Calculate an electronic gear ratio

A master axis moves 250 mm while the follower must move 175 mm.

\[ k=\frac{175}{250}=0.7 \]

The follower ratio is 0.7 in the chosen coordinate convention.

If the master then moves 400 mm, the ideal follower displacement is:

\[ x_f=0.7(400)=280\text{ mm} \]

Assume zero offset and consistent coordinate units.

Exercise 2: Diagnose a growing position error

A follower starts correctly aligned with its master, but the mismatch grows as the line runs.

Investigate encoder scaling, engineering-unit conversion, gear ratio, direction conventions and whether the controller is using the intended master position.

Exercise 3: Diagnose a phase problem

Two rotary axes run at identical speeds, but a component arrives at the station too early.

Compare actual master and follower positions, inspect phase offset and confirm the event trigger is tied to the intended reference. Matching RPM alone does not guarantee the correct process position.

Exercise 4: Diagnose a gantry fault

One side of a gantry moves less than the other during acceleration.

Compare both axes' command and feedback trends. Check drive status, encoder scaling, acceleration limits, mechanical binding and the coordinated-axis configuration. Follow the machine's approved response if the skew threatens the mechanism.

Exercise 5: Prepare a synchronization specification

For an actual machine, record:

Item Required information
Master reference Physical axis, encoder or virtual master
Follower axes All axes participating in synchronization
Relationship Position ratio, velocity ratio, phase offset or cam table
Coordinate system Units, direction, reference and scaling
Motion limits Velocity, acceleration, deceleration and jerk
Engagement Conditions and permitted initial position
Tolerances Following error and synchronization error limits
Fault response Behaviour after drive, encoder or communication faults
Recovery Re-reference, re-engagement and restart requirements
Validation Trends, cycle tests, fault tests and safety checks

18. Final mental model

Remember these principles:

  1. Synchronization coordinates relationships between movements, not merely start times.
  2. Master-follower control uses a common reference to maintain a defined relationship.
  3. Electronic gearing establishes a ratio; electronic camming establishes a programmed relationship that can vary throughout the cycle.
  4. Equal speed does not guarantee equal position or correct phase.
  5. Feedback, scaling, acceleration capability, communication and mechanical condition determine whether synchronization can be maintained.
  6. Synchronization logic must include status monitoring, error handling, safe stopping behaviour and controlled recovery.

The practical goal is to ensure that every axis moves in the correct relationship to the others, under normal conditions and during faults.
