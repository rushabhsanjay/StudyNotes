1. Definition and Purpose

An HMI alarm is a notification generated when a configured condition indicates an abnormal state, a process limit violation, an equipment fault, or an event requiring operator attention.

Alarms help operators and maintenance engineers identify problems, understand their significance, and take appropriate action.

Examples in an automotive manufacturing plant include:

  • Robot not ready.
  • Drive fault active.
  • Cylinder extension timeout.
  • Low pneumatic pressure.
  • Motor overload.
  • PLC communication failure.
  • Safety circuit not healthy.
  • Process temperature above its permitted limit.

The primary purposes of an alarm system are to:

  1. Notify personnel of abnormal operating conditions.
  2. Identify the equipment or process affected.
  3. Explain what has happened.
  4. Help personnel determine the appropriate response.
  5. Record alarm occurrence and recovery where supported.
  6. Reduce unnecessary downtime and improve troubleshooting.

An alarm is not merely a red message on an HMI screen. It is part of the overall control-system design, including the detection condition, priority, operator notification, acknowledgement behavior, and event recording.

The exact location of alarm evaluation depends on the system. Some conditions are evaluated in the PLC, while others may be evaluated in the HMI or SCADA software.

For important machine faults, keeping the underlying detection and control logic in the appropriate controller generally makes the behavior easier to validate and maintain.

Important distinction: An HMI alarm informs the operator about a condition. It does not automatically correct the condition or make the machine safe.

3. Alarm Versus Status Versus Event

These terms are related but have different meanings.

Term Meaning Example
Status Indicates the current state of equipment Motor running
Alarm Indicates an abnormal condition requiring attention Motor overload
Event Records that something occurred Operator changed mode
Warning Alerts the operator to a condition that may need attention Filter maintenance due soon
Trip A protective action or state resulting from a detected condition Drive trips on overcurrent
Interlock A condition that permits or prevents an operation Clamp must be closed before cycle start

The terminology may vary between manufacturers and plants.

A status can change without generating an alarm. For example, a motor transitioning from stopped to running is normally a status change, not an alarm.

An event can be important without indicating a fault. For example, a user login or recipe change may be recorded as an event.

A trip is not necessarily the same thing as an alarm. The trip may stop equipment, while the alarm reports the reason.

4. Main Types of Industrial Alarms

4.1 Equipment Fault Alarms

These indicate that a machine component or device has reported a fault.

Examples:

  • Servo drive fault.
  • VFD fault.
  • Robot controller fault.
  • Motor overload.
  • CNC alarm.
  • Sensor diagnostic fault.

Typical information includes the equipment identifier, fault description, fault code, and relevant diagnostic guidance.

4.2 Process Limit Alarms

These are generated when a measured value crosses a defined threshold.

Examples:

  • Temperature too high.
  • Pressure too low.
  • Tank level too high.
  • Motor current above its operating limit.
  • Cooling-water flow below its required value.

Process alarms require suitable limits and consideration of normal variation, sensor accuracy, and process response time.

4.3 Sequence and Timeout Alarms

These occur when an expected action or transition does not happen within the allowed time.

Examples:

  • Cylinder failed to extend.
  • Robot failed to reach the expected position.
  • Clamp did not close within the allowed time.
  • Part transfer did not complete.
  • Automatic cycle exceeded its permitted duration.

A timeout alarm commonly depends on a command, a feedback condition, and a timer.

For example:

Clamp Extend Command = TRUE
        |
        v
Start / evaluate timeout
        |
        +---- Extend Feedback arrives in time
        |                |
        |                v
        |           No timeout alarm
        |
        +---- Feedback does not arrive
                         |
                         v
                   Timeout alarm

The real implementation must account for valid starting conditions, sequence state, feedback behavior, and timer reset logic.

4.4 Communication Alarms

These indicate that a device or communication connection is unavailable or not functioning as expected.

Examples:

  • PLC communication lost.
  • Robot controller connection lost.
  • Remote I/O unavailable.
  • HMI data source unavailable.
  • Drive communication failure.

Communication alarms are especially important because a missing value must not be mistaken for a healthy equipment state.

4.5 Utility and Facility Alarms

These indicate problems with supporting systems.

Examples:

  • Low compressed-air pressure.
  • Cooling-water supply failure.
  • Hydraulic pressure low.
  • Excessive cabinet temperature.
  • Power-supply fault.

A utility problem can affect several machines at once, so the alarm should identify the affected utility and relevant equipment scope.

4.6 Maintenance and Advisory Alarms

These indicate a condition requiring planned attention rather than an immediate emergency response.

Examples:

  • Maintenance interval reached.
  • Filter replacement due.
  • Battery replacement recommended.
  • Lubrication cycle due.
  • Operating-hour threshold reached.

These conditions may be presented as warnings or maintenance notifications, depending on plant conventions.

4.7 Safety-Related Indications

Examples include a safety gate open or a safety circuit not healthy.

These indications may be shown on the HMI, but the HMI is not the safety authority. Required safety functions must be implemented by the appropriate safety-related control system and validated according to the machine's safety requirements.

An HMI acknowledgement must never be treated as proof that a safety hazard has been eliminated.

5. Alarm Priority and Severity

Alarm priority communicates the urgency of a condition and helps operators decide what to investigate first.

A plant may use priority levels such as Critical, High, Medium, and Low. The exact naming and response requirements must be defined by the plant's alarm philosophy.

Illustrative priority Meaning Example
Critical Immediate action required according to the defined operating procedure A serious process condition requiring immediate response
High Urgent production or equipment problem Robot fault stopping a production station
Medium Significant abnormal condition requiring timely attention Drive fault on non-running standby equipment
Low Advisory condition or less urgent issue Maintenance interval reached

These examples are illustrative, not universal assignments. The actual priority depends on the consequence, required response time, and operating context.

Priority is not the same as severity

  • Severity describes the consequence or seriousness of the condition.
  • Priority helps determine how quickly the operator must respond.

A high-severity issue may require immediate protective action, but an alarm's displayed priority should be based on a documented risk and response assessment.

Do not assign every alarm the highest priority. If everything is urgent, operators eventually stop distinguishing urgent conditions from routine noise.

6. Alarm Properties

An alarm configuration commonly contains several properties.

Property Purpose
Alarm ID Unique identifier for the alarm

Priority Relative urgency
Equipment or location Identifies the affected asset
Activation time Records when the alarm became active
Return-to-normal time Records when the condition cleared, where supported
Acknowledgement state Indicates whether an operator acknowledged it
Deadband or hysteresis Helps prevent repeated transitions near a threshold
Delay or debounce Filters brief or transient conditions where appropriate
Help text Provides investigation guidance
History configuration Determines whether alarm occurrences are recorded

Not every HMI product supports every property in the same way.

7. Alarm States

Understanding alarm states is essential for operation and troubleshooting.

7.1 Normal

The alarm condition is not present.

Example: the monitored temperature is below its configured high-alarm threshold.

7.2 Active, Unacknowledged

The alarm condition is present, but an operator has not acknowledged it.

This state means the condition exists and the alarm has not yet been acknowledged.

7.3 Active, Acknowledged

The alarm condition is still present, but an operator has acknowledged the notification.

Acknowledgement does not mean the fault has been fixed.

7.4 Cleared, Unacknowledged

The condition has returned to normal, but the operator has not acknowledged the alarm, where the platform supports this state.

This distinction helps preserve the fact that an alarm occurred even if it cleared quickly.

7.5 Cleared, Acknowledged

The alarm condition has returned to normal and has been acknowledged.

The alarm may remain visible in the history even when it is no longer active.

Alarm state summary

Alarm condition Acknowledged? Typical interpretation
Active No Fault condition present; notification not acknowledged
Active Yes Fault condition still present; notification acknowledged
Cleared No Fault condition has cleared; acknowledgement outstanding
Cleared Yes Fault condition cleared and acknowledged

The exact display states depend on the HMI platform and configuration.

8. Alarm Acknowledgement Versus Alarm Reset

These actions are frequently confused.

Acknowledgement

Acknowledgement confirms that the operator has recognized the alarm notification.

It does not necessarily change the machine state or remove the cause.

Reset

Reset is a command or action intended to clear a resettable fault or return a control function to an appropriate state after the required conditions are satisfied.

Resetting a fault does not necessarily mean the underlying cause has been removed.

For example, a drive fault may require the operator to investigate the cause before resetting the drive. The HMI should not conceal a recurring fault simply because the Reset button was pressed.

Action Purpose Does it guarantee the cause is fixed?
Acknowledge Confirm notification was recognized No
Reset Request a supported fault-reset operation No
Corrective action Address the underlying problem Depends on the repair and verification
Return to normal Indicates the alarm condition is no longer true Not necessarily proof that the machine is ready to run

The alarm system should distinguish acknowledgement, fault reset, condition clearance, and permission to restart.

9. Alarm Trigger Logic

An alarm requires a condition that determines when it becomes active.

9.1 Boolean Alarm

A Boolean alarm activates when a Boolean condition becomes true.

Example:

Drive_Fault = TRUE
        |
        v
Drive Fault Alarm Active

The source tag may come from a PLC variable, a communication status, or another supported data source.

9.2 High-Limit Alarm

A high-limit alarm activates when a measured value exceeds a defined threshold.

Example:

Temperature > High_Limit
        |
        v
High Temperature Alarm

A practical implementation may also require a delay, quality check, or additional condition to prevent nuisance alarms.

9.3 Low-Limit Alarm

A low-limit alarm activates when a measured value falls below a configured threshold.

Example:

Pressure < Low_Limit
        |
        v
Low Pressure Alarm

The threshold must match the equipment requirements and expected operating range.

9.4 Timeout Alarm

A timeout alarm activates when a required condition does not occur within the permitted time.

Example:

Extend_Command = TRUE
AND
Extend_Feedback = FALSE
AND
Timeout_Expired = TRUE
        |
        v
Cylinder Extension Timeout Alarm

This is a conceptual condition. A real PLC implementation must define when the timer starts, when it resets, whether the sequence is active, and how contradictory or unavailable feedback is handled.

10. Deadband and Hysteresis

Analog values can fluctuate around a threshold. Without suitable hysteresis, an alarm may repeatedly activate and clear.

Suppose a temperature high alarm activates above 80 °C.

If the temperature fluctuates around 80 °C, the alarm could repeatedly change state.

A hysteresis design uses different activation and clearance thresholds.

For example:

  • Alarm activates when temperature rises above 80 °C.
  • Alarm clears when temperature falls below 77 °C.

The 3 °C difference is an illustrative hysteresis band, not a recommended setting for every process.

This creates a more stable alarm state.

Important distinction

  • Deadband is a range used to prevent unnecessary state changes around a threshold.
  • Hysteresis describes different thresholds for activation and clearance.

Manufacturers may use these terms differently for particular alarm objects, so check the platform documentation.

11. Alarm Delay and Debouncing

Some abnormal signals occur only briefly. An alarm delay can prevent short transients from creating unnecessary notifications.

For example, a pressure signal may momentarily fall below its threshold during a normal process transition.

A configured delay might require the low-pressure condition to persist before the alarm activates.

Conceptually:

Low Pressure Condition
          |
          v
Condition persists for configured delay?
          |
       +--+--+
       |     |
      No    Yes
       |     |
       v     v
   No alarm  Activate alarm

The delay must be chosen carefully.

A delay that is too short may produce nuisance alarms. A delay that is too long may hide a meaningful problem or delay operator awareness.

Do not add delays to important conditions without evaluating the required response time and the consequences of delayed notification.

12. Alarm Messages and Operator Guidance

An alarm message should identify the problem clearly enough for the operator to understand what needs attention.

A useful message usually identifies:

  • Equipment or station.
  • Fault condition.
  • Relevant location or component.
  • Additional context where useful.

Poor versus better messages

Poor message Better message
Error 12 FR1010: Robot controller fault active
Fault FR1010: Clamp failed to extend within timeout
Pressure Low Utility: Compressed-air pressure below operating limit
Not Ready FR1010: Start permissive not satisfied
Communication Error FR1010: Robot controller communication lost

A good message should be concise and technically meaningful.

If the alarm system supports help text, provide useful diagnostic information such as:

  • What condition triggered the alarm.
  • Which component is affected.
  • Which status signals should be checked.
  • Which approved procedure applies.
  • What must be verified before restart.

Avoid giving a generic instruction such as "Check machine" when the system can provide more specific information.

13. Alarm Banner, Alarm Summary and Alarm History

These are different ways to present alarm information.

13.1 Alarm Banner

A banner shows selected important alarms on the current screen.

Typical contents:

  • Highest-priority active alarm.
  • Alarm description.
  • Station or equipment identifier.
  • Acknowledgement control, if permitted.

A banner gives the operator immediate awareness without requiring navigation to a separate screen.

13.2 Alarm Summary

The alarm summary lists alarms and their current states.

Typical columns include:

Column Purpose
Priority Indicates urgency
Time Shows activation time
Equipment Identifies the affected station
Message Describes the condition
State Shows active or cleared status
Acknowledgement Shows acknowledgement status

Filtering by station, priority, or state can make a large alarm list easier to use.

13.3 Alarm History

Alarm history records past alarm occurrences and state changes where supported.

It may include:

  • Activation time.
  • Clearance time.
  • Acknowledgement time.
  • Alarm identifier.
  • Equipment identifier.
  • User identity for relevant actions.
  • Associated event information.

Alarm history is useful for investigating recurring faults, intermittent communication problems, and the sequence of events leading to downtime.

Historical recording depends on the platform and configuration. A current alarm list does not automatically imply that complete historical records are available.

14. Alarm Floods and Nuisance Alarms

Alarm flood

An alarm flood occurs when many alarms appear in a short period, making it difficult to identify the most important condition.

For example, one upstream utility failure may cause multiple downstream stations to report faults simultaneously.

The alarm system should help distinguish the initiating problem from its secondary effects.

Nuisance alarm

A nuisance alarm occurs when a notification provides little operational value or occurs repeatedly without requiring a meaningful response.

Common causes include:

  • Poorly selected thresholds.
  • Incorrect alarm delays.
  • Missing hysteresis.
  • Duplicate alarms.
  • Incorrect tag mapping.
  • Alarms triggered during normal transitions.
  • Poorly designed startup or shutdown behavior.
  • Alarms that operators cannot meaningfully act upon.

Alarm rationalization

Alarm rationalization is the process of reviewing alarms to establish whether each one is necessary and how it should behave.

Questions include:

  1. What condition does the alarm represent?
  2. What consequence could occur if it is ignored?
  3. What action should the operator take?
  4. How quickly is a response required?
  5. What priority is appropriate?
  6. Is the alarm duplicated elsewhere?
  7. Does it need a delay or hysteresis?
  8. Is the message clear and actionable?

An alarm that has no defined purpose or operator response should be reviewed rather than automatically assigned a high priority.

15. Alarm Troubleshooting

Symptom Possible cause Investigation
Alarm never activates Wrong trigger tag or condition Compare the live source value with the alarm condition
Alarm remains active after the fault clears Source remains true, incorrect reset logic, or clearance configuration Check the trigger value and alarm state configuration
Alarm activates repeatedly Threshold fluctuations or insufficient hysteresis Review signal stability and threshold settings
Too many alarms appear at once Common upstream fault or alarm flood Identify the earliest initiating condition
Alarm message is misleading Incorrect description or tag mapping Trace the alarm to the correct source
Alarm cannot be acknowledged User permissions or platform configuration Check access rights and acknowledgement behavior
Alarm history is missing Historical logging disabled or storage issue Verify history configuration and storage
Alarm occurs during normal startup Normal transition not accounted for Review sequence conditions and approved delay requirements
Alarm appears during communication loss Data-quality handling or communication detection problem Check connection monitoring and tag quality
Reset works but fault returns Underlying cause remains present Investigate the physical fault and its control conditions

Recommended diagnostic sequence

  1. Record the exact alarm message and station identifier.
  2. Determine whether the alarm is active, acknowledged, or cleared.
  3. Identify the alarm trigger tag or condition.
  4. Compare the trigger with the live PLC or source value.
  5. Check communication quality and data freshness.
  6. Verify threshold, delay, hysteresis, and sequence conditions.
  7. Investigate the underlying equipment or process.
  8. Perform the approved corrective action.
  9. Verify that the alarm condition has cleared.
  10. Confirm that restart conditions are satisfied before returning the equipment to service.

Do not repeatedly acknowledge or reset alarms merely to clear the screen. That removes useful information without fixing the underlying problem.

16. Practical Example: Robot Station Alarm System

Consider an automotive robotic station named FR1010.

The following alarm list is illustrative.

Alarm ID Alarm message Trigger condition Example priority
ALM_001 Robot controller fault active Robot_Fault = TRUE High
ALM_002 Robot not ready Readiness absent when required Medium or High
ALM_003 Clamp failed to extend within timeout Required extension feedback not received in time High
ALM_004 Drive fault active Drive_Fault = TRUE High
ALM_005 PLC communication lost Communication failure detected High
ALM_006 Maintenance interval reached Operating counter exceeds configured threshold Low

The final priorities depend on the consequences and required response for the actual machine.

Example: Clamp extension timeout

Assume the PLC issues a clamp-extension command.

  1. The sequence requests clamp extension.
  2. The PLC evaluates the extension feedback.
  3. A timer measures the permitted response interval.
  4. If feedback arrives in time, the sequence can continue if all other conditions are satisfied.
  5. If the timeout expires without the required feedback, the PLC generates the timeout condition.
  6. The HMI displays the alarm and identifies the affected clamp.
  7. Maintenance checks the relevant pneumatic supply, valve, cylinder, sensor, wiring, and PLC feedback state.
  8. After the cause is corrected, the alarm condition clears according to the designed logic.
  9. The machine is restarted only after the required operating conditions and approved restart procedure are satisfied.

This example demonstrates why a useful alarm needs more than a Boolean bit. Its message, trigger condition, timing, state behavior, and diagnostic context all matter.

17. Alarm Design Checklist

Before commissioning an alarm, verify the following:

  • The trigger condition matches the actual fault.
  • The alarm has a unique identifier.
  • The message is clear and meaningful.
  • The equipment location is identified.
  • The priority is justified.
  • Any delay or hysteresis is appropriate.
  • Normal operating transitions do not create unnecessary alarms.
  • Acknowledgement behavior is understood.
  • Alarm clearance behavior is tested.
  • Communication failure is handled appropriately.
  • Historical recording is configured where required.
  • Operator guidance is available.
  • Required access controls are configured.
  • Alarm behavior has been tested in normal and abnormal conditions.
  • Alarm testing does not bypass required safety controls.

18. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is an HMI alarm?

An HMI alarm is a notification generated when a configured condition indicates an abnormal state or a condition requiring operator attention.

Q2. What is the difference between an alarm and an event?

An alarm indicates a condition that requires attention. An event records something that happened, such as a mode change or user login, whether or not a fault occurred.

Q3. What is the difference between alarm acknowledgement and reset?

Acknowledgement confirms that the notification was recognized. Reset requests a supported fault-reset action. Neither guarantees that the underlying cause has been corrected.

Q4. What is alarm priority?

Priority indicates the relative urgency of responding to an alarm, based on its consequence and required response time.

Q5. What is hysteresis?

Hysteresis uses different activation and clearance thresholds to prevent an alarm from repeatedly changing state when a value fluctuates near a limit.

Q6. Why is an alarm delay used?

A delay can filter brief or transient conditions so that alarms activate only when the abnormal condition persists for the configured interval.

Q7. What is an alarm flood?

An alarm flood occurs when many alarms appear in a short period, making it difficult for the operator to identify the most important conditions.

Q8. What is alarm rationalization?

Alarm rationalization is the review of alarm purpose, priority, trigger, operator response, and configuration to ensure alarms are useful and actionable.

Q9. Why is alarm history important?

It provides a record of past alarm occurrences and state changes that can help investigate intermittent faults and recurring downtime.

Q10. Should the HMI be responsible for making the machine safe when an alarm occurs?

No. Required protective and safety functions must be implemented by the appropriate control and safety systems. The HMI communicates alarm information to the operator.

19. Practical Study Exercises

Use a simulator or an approved non-production training system.

  1. Create a Boolean equipment-fault alarm.
  2. Create a high-limit alarm for an analog value.
  3. Create a low-limit alarm with an appropriate hysteresis band.
  4. Simulate a sequence timeout alarm.
  5. Test the distinction between active and acknowledged states.
  6. Verify that acknowledging an alarm does not falsely indicate that the fault has cleared.
  7. Configure clear and actionable alarm messages.
  8. Test communication-loss indication.
  9. Review alarm priorities using the consequences and required response.
  10. Create a sample alarm summary and review its filtering options.
  11. Inspect the available alarm-history functions.
  12. Investigate a simulated alarm flood and identify the initiating condition.
  13. Document the alarm trigger, priority, expected response, and clearance behavior.

20. Final Mental Model

An effective industrial alarm system follows this logic:

Abnormal condition detected
            |
            v
      Alarm condition
            |
            v
   Priority and message assigned
            |
            v
     Operator notified
            |
            v
   Condition investigated
            |
            v
     Corrective action
            |
            v
     Condition clears
            |
            v
   Recovery is verified

The key distinctions are:

  • Alarm: Notifies personnel about an abnormal condition.
  • Priority: Indicates the urgency of response.
  • Acknowledgement: Confirms recognition of the notification.
  • Reset: Requests a supported reset action.
  • Clearance: Indicates that the configured alarm condition is no longer active.
  • Alarm history: Records past occurrences and state changes where configured.

A good alarm system helps operators identify meaningful problems quickly, while a poorly designed one produces noise that hides the problems that actually matter.
